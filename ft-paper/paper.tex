    \section{Background}\label{background}

    \subsection{Introduction}
\label{problem}

$k$-mer based classification tools such as Kraken 1 \citep{kraken}, Kraken 2 \citep{kraken2}, KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq} and Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub} assign reads to taxa by matching their $k$-mers against a database that maps each $k$-mer to a node of a taxonomy tree. Since a $k$-mer may occur in the genomes of several organisms, a $k$-mer database is subjected to the \emph{lowest common ancestor update} (LCA update): a $k$-mer occurring in the genome of more than one taxon is moved up to the lowest node that holds all corresponding taxa as descendants. Later, at classification time, the LCA update prevents $k$-mers in reads from being falsely assigned to an overly low-ranked taxon, since genomes of other taxa such as siblings contain that $k$-mer too. Thus, essentially, the LCA update avoids a potentially false assignment of overly specific taxa to reads.

Unfortunately, the LCA update comes at a price that originates in the ever-growing number of genomes from underlying source databases such as RefSeq: as Nasko et al. \citep{Nasko:2018aa} have shown, that it leads to a growing set of species and corresponding genomes under a single genus.
    Each newly added species closely related to an already covered one turns a $k$-mer that used to be specific to a single species into a shared one, and so the LCA update moves it up towards the genus. Consequently, with newer RefSeq versions \emph{more} reads are classified in total, but \emph{fewer} of them are classified on the species level \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. In other words, the resolution of $k$-mer databases deteriorates while the underlying genomic data deepens.
    We observed the same effect in our first study on Genestrip, where databases with a deeper genomic coverage for organisms of interest classified more reads but tended to accumulate them on higher ranks \citep{genestrip-pub}.

Regarding RefSeq, the expansion happens at a remarkable pace: between its releases 66 (July 2014) and 71 (July 2015) alone, the number of bacterial organisms grew by 40 \% to 39,660, so that release 71 covered more than 55,000 organisms in total \citep{refseq}. A few years later, Nasko et al. quantified what this influx does to $k$-mer based classification, and since their study the expansion has continued unabated: the number of prokaryotic genomes in RefSeq alone has increased by a further factor of 5.8 \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038}.
    The trend persists to this day: in the two years from release 225 (July 2024) to release 236 the number of covered organisms grew by about 20 \% from 152,668 to 182,465 \citep{refseqstats}.

Figure \ref{kmerongenusborrelia} illustrates the effect on the distribution of $k$-mers across ranks for two Genestrip databases: regarding the bacterial database \texttt{tb}, a median of about 68 \% of a species' $k$-mers no longer reside at the species rank or below but somewhere between the genus and the species rank, whereas for \texttt{protozoa} the picture is almost reversed, with a median of about 79 \% of a species' $k$-mers remaining at the species rank or below.
    Both databases rest exclusively on RefSeq genomes, and bacteria are covered by far more close relatives than protozoa: release 236 comprises 109,133 bacterial but merely 944 protozoan species \citep{refseqstats}. The $k$-mers of a bacterial species are pushed up accordingly.

    At first glance, such $k$-mers appear to be lost for fine-grained classification, but Figure \ref{kmerviaranks} shows that this is not the case: for the $k$-mers that ended up on a genus, we determined the number of the genus' children whose genomes actually contain them.
    A median of about 84 \% (\texttt{tb}) and 89 \% (\texttt{protozoa}) of them occur in just \emph{two} of the children, and the share drops steeply for higher numbers. So a $k$-mer sitting on a genus is typically not characteristic of the genus as a whole but just of a small group of species under it. The LCA update simply has no place to put it, because the taxonomy offers no node that represents exactly this group. \emph{This is the potential that our approach exploits}: if the taxonomy is refined by additional nodes that do represent such groups, then the very same $k$-mers can potentially be pushed back down and become informative again.

    To the best of our knowledge, none of the established $k$-mer based classification tools makes use of this potential. Reads whose $k$-mers would suffice to narrow the candidates down to two or three species are therefore often reported on the genus, even though the information about which species actually share these $k$-mers is fully contained in the underlying genomes.

One route to improving the database is to correct the classification results afterwards. E.g., Bracken \citep{lu2017bracken} re-estimates species-level abundances from the output of a Kraken-style classification by redistributing the reads assigned to higher ranks according to a background distribution, which describes how the $k$-mers of each species in the database are expected to spread across the taxonomy. Nasko et al. \citep{Nasko:2018aa} confirm that this mitigates the shift described above, but also that it struggles with novel genomes. The approach differs from a refinement of the database itself: First, Bracken does not add information but reallocates counts on the basis of \emph{probabilistic assumptions} about which species are present and how their $k$-mers distribute. Its estimates degrade when a sample deviates from these assumptions such as for rare or unexpected organisms. Second, it operates on aggregated abundances of an entire sample rather than on individual reads, so that a single read remains classified on the genus even after the re-estimation. A refined database, in contrast, relies on \emph{hard data}: which species actually share a given $k$-mer is known exactly from the underlying genomes, and this knowledge is encoded in the database itself. Each read is then classified more specifically by construction rather than by estimation.
%... , and any subsequent analysis -- including an abundance estimation -- benefits from it.

    \subsection{Approach}
    \label{approach}
    Our contribution builds on the first study on Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub} by further improving precision of read classifications without sacrificing recall. Genestrip-FT refines the taxonomy tree of a $k$-mer database by grouping those taxa that share many $k$-mers, and it reassigns the affected $k$-mers to the newly added nodes. Hereby, a $k$-mer is only pushed down as far as it may still be attributed to all taxa whose genomes contain it, so that no read is ever classified below the level justified by the underlying genomes.

Figure \ref{ftsketch} captures our approach with regard to a simplistic $k$-mer database
with $k = 2$:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Species $X$, $Y$, $Z$ under a common genus $W$ have respective sequences associated. 
	\item After filling the database, the $2$-mers are assigned to their species. The figure simplifies the state of the database after this step.
	\item The lowest common ancestor update (LCA update) pushes the $2$-mer \texttt{CT} up to $W$ because it occurs in the sequence of both $X$ and $Y$. However, it does not occur in $Z$. So, according to this database a read \texttt{CT} would just be classified as $W$ although $Z$ is not even a candidate species for \texttt{CT}.
	\item Genestrip-FT's refinement adds an artificial node $h$ to the taxonomy with $X$ and $Y$ as children but not $Z$. Also, the refinement \emph{pushes down} \texttt{CT} from $W$ to $h$. According to this refined database, a read \texttt{CT} would be classified as $h$ and so, $Z$ is excluded as candidate species for \texttt{CT}.
\end{enumerate}
 So in general, this type of refinement potentially improves classification precision: E.g., if a read was classified under a genus taxon in a unrefined database, then \emph{the corresponding refined database may allow for narrowing down the candidate species to fewer species than originally listed under that genus}.

For the sake of simplicity, we restrict the refinement to the rank genus only, although \emph{it is normally performed simultaneously on all levels of the database's taxonomy} but its leaves.

Genestrip structures the complex computation of a $k$-mer database via goals as explained in \cite{genestrip-pub}. The goals form a dependency graph where a goal $g_1$ is executed only if its computational result is needed by a dependent goal $g_2$, and if $g_1$'s result has not been computed yet. Genestrip-FT is implemented as an extension of the original goal graph formed by Genestrip. Figure \ref{ftgoals} presents an extract of the additional goals, with most of Genestrip's original goals omitted.

        \centering

In the following, we briefly explain the essential goals needed for the computation of a refined database:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \verb|kmerindexbloom| associates each $k$-mer stored on the genus rank with the original species taxon of the sequence in which it occurred. To achieve this, the original sequences that were used to generate the unrefined database must all be reread. The mapping of a $k$-mer to its original species taxon is hashed; the resulting hash code is stored via a Bloom filter \cite{bloomfilter} in order to save main memory.\footnote{A Bloom filter is a probabilistic data structure for an efficient and compressed storing of values but also for a fast lookup of entered values. However, this comes at the expense of a configurable, usually low false positive rate at lookup time. Bloom filters and probabilistic filters in general \cite{10.1145/2674005.2674994,9920402,10.1145/3510449,10.1145/3376122} are widely used in the context of metagenomics tools \cite{biobloom, Piro:2020aa, 10.1093/nargab/lqaf094}.}
	
	\item \verb|intersectcount| computes the number of common $k$-mers $c(t_1, t_2)$ between any two species taxa $t_1$ and $t_2$ under the same genus. To do so, it consults the Bloom filter produced via the goal \verb|kmerindexbloom|. Based on $c(t_1, t_2)$, it also determines a Laplace-smoothed Jaccard index
	\[ J(t_1, t_2) = \frac{c(t_1, t_2) + 1}{c(t_1, t_1) + c(t_2, t_2) - c(t_1, t_2) + 1} \]
	\cite{jaccard} as a similarity measure of the two sets of $k$-mers belonging to $t_1$ and $t_2$ on the genus, respectively. The Laplace correction adds $1$ to both the intersection count (numerator) and each self-count (denominator), which avoids division by zero when both species share no $k$-mers on the genus and yields $J = 1$ in that degenerate case. Regarding our running example, Table \ref{intersectioncounts} shows the raw intersection counts whereas Table \ref{jaccard} states the resulting Jaccard indices. For simplicity, we skip the background of the column \enquote{OTHER} for now --- it will be explained at the end of this section.

	\item The goal \verb|dendrogram| from Figure \ref{ftgoals} computes a dendrogram using the standard agglomerative clustering algorithm \enquote{HAC} \cite{King01031967,1394399}, where the cluster distance is based on the Jaccard index $J$ from \verb|intersectcount|. Since the taxonomic refinement is governed by the aim of improving classification precision (see Section \ref{approach}), we chose \enquote{Single Linkage} \cite{King01031967} as the default linkage strategy. This linkage strategy is suitable because it merges clusters on the basis of their most similar $k$-mer sets they contain. Nevertheless, other linkage strategies such as UPGMA and WPGMA \cite{Sokal1958ASM} may be configured instead. Figure \ref{dendrogram} depicts the resulting dendrogram as computed on the basis of Table \ref{jaccard}.

	\item The goal \verb|ftupdatedb| reorganizes the database's taxonomy tree by adding new tree nodes under a genus as induced by the previous goal's dendrogram. Furthermore, $k$-mers are pushed down, i.e. reassigned, to the newly added nodes accordingly. Hereby, each $k$-mer is pushed down as far to a new node $n$, such that all species with sequences containing that $k$-mer still remain descendants of $n$.

	\item Finally, \verb|ftdb| stores the refined databases as a file.
\end{enumerate} 

	Figure \ref{simplerefdb} depicts the refined taxonomy for the Orthopoxvirus example and also states the number of $k$-mers as stored per node in the refined database. As can be seen, about 56 \% of the $k$-mers that originally resided on the genus Orthopoxvirus got pushed down to the newly added nodes (see Figure \ref{simplelcadb} for comparison). The new nodes obtain an artificial taxon prefixed with \enquote{000} to distinguish them from regular taxonomic entries, and their artificial rank is set to \enquote{REFINED}.

\subsection{Intrinsic quality measures for $k$-mer databases}
\label{intrinsicqms}    
    In this section, we consider new quality measures regarding $k$-mer databases irrespective of read analysis and read classification results.
    For this purpose, we establish \emph{path correctness} as a prerequisite and the precision of a single $k$-mer as the actual quality measure, from which \emph{node precision} and \emph{subtree precision} follow by averaging.
    The three measures generally apply to any $k$-mer database $d$ that has a taxonomy tree associated and where stored $k$-mers have taxa, i.e., nodes, assigned from that tree.
    In the results section, we use the new precision measures along with well-established classification-based measures to assess refinement quality. \todo{As will be shown, these new intrinsic measures correlate well with the established, classification-based measures.}

\subsubsection{Data taxa and path correctness}\label{pathcorrectness}
    Let $t$ be a \emph{data taxon}, i.e., \emph{a taxon with a complete genome directly associated whereby the $k$-mers from that genome are also stored in $d$}. For example in RefSeq, complete genomes are often associated with taxa of the rank species or below. Moreover, let $\nu(a)$ denote the node at which a $k$-mer $a$ is stored in $d$; since each $k$-mer is stored exactly once, $\nu$ is well defined.

For a fair assessment of the measures below, we require that each data taxon must be a leaf node in the taxonomy tree of database $d$. This can easily be enforced by a tree transformation explained via (3) and (4) in Figure \ref{fttrans}. If the requirement was not met, then two data taxa on the same path would likely result in an inadequately lowered node precision (as in Figure \ref{fttrans} (1) and (2)). Note that the described transformation leaves the phylogenetic structure of a given taxonomy intact.
Figure \ref{simplerefdbtrans} exemplifies the transformation when integrated into the refinement of the database from Figure \ref{simplelcadb}.\footnote{When carefully comparing the $k$-mer counts of similar nodes from Figures \ref{simplerefdb} and \ref{simplerefdbtrans}, one finds minor differences such as for \enquote{10245/OTHER}. Note that these differences originate in the use of a probabilistic data structure, i.e. a Bloom filter, during the refinement procedure according to Section \ref{dbrefinement}.}

    On this basis, we call a $k$-mer database $d$ \emph{path correct} if
for every $k$-mer $a$ stored in $d$ and every data taxon $t$ whose genome contains $a$, the node $\nu(a)$ is an ancestor of $t$ or $t$ itself. Equivalently, $\nu(a)$ lies on the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root for each such $t$.

    Both unrefined and refined databases are path correct by construction. The LCA update moves a $k$-mer up in the taxonomy just far enough that all data taxa whose genomes contain it become descendants of the resulting node. The refinement, in turn, may push a $k$-mer down only so far that the condition above is still maintained --- this is what keeps \verb|ftupdatedb| from placing a $k$-mer below the lowest common ancestor of the data taxa supporting it.\footnote{Due to its probabilistic nature, the Bloom filter used during the \texttt{kmerindexbloom} step (see Section~\ref{dbrefinement}) occasionally falsely attributes a species to a $k$-mer, which may prevent the $k$-mer from being pushed down to its lowest possible node. This leaves path correctness intact and only keeps the $k$-mer at a less specific node than necessary.}

\subsubsection{Node precision and subtree precision}\label{nodeprecision}
Given path correctness, every data taxon under a $k$-mer's node is a candidate carrier of that $k$-mer, and how many of them actually carry it indicates how well the $k$-mer is placed.

    Let $D_n$ denote the set of data taxa under a node $n$ including $n$ itself. For a $k$-mer $a$ stored in $d$ with its node $\nu(a)$ from Section \ref{pathcorrectness}, let $c(a)$ be the number of data taxa $t \in D_{\nu(a)}$ whose genomes contain $a$ at least once. We define the \emph{precision of the $k$-mer $a$} as
\[ p(a) = \frac{c(a)}{|D_{\nu(a)}|} , \]
    i.e.\ as the fraction of the data taxa under $a$'s node whose genomes actually contain $a$. It is defined whenever $D_{\nu(a)} \neq \emptyset$, which excludes only the $k$-mers stored at artificial nodes carrying no data taxon at all.

    Due to path correctness, every data taxon whose genome contains $a$ is subordinate to $\nu(a)$, and so the count $c(a)$ does not depend on where $a$ is stored: it is simply the number of data taxa in $d$ that support $a$. Only $|D_{\nu(a)}|$ varies with the placement, and it is smallest when $\nu(a)$ is the lowest common ancestor of the data taxa supporting $a$.
    Note that raising $p(a)$ is the very purpose of the refinement as it introduces new nodes and moves $k$-mers onto them.

    Averaging $p$ over the $k$-mers stored at a single node $n$ yields \emph{node precision} $np(n)$. With $A_n$ denoting the set of $k$-mers associated with that node:
\[ np(n) = \frac{1}{|A_n|} \sum_{a \in A_n} p(a). \]
    Averaging $p$ over the $k$-mers of an entire subtree yields \emph{subtree precision} $sp(m)$. Let $m$ be a node of $d$'s taxonomy tree and let
$A^{*}_m = \bigcup_{n} A_n$ be the set of $k$-mers stored at the nodes $n$ of the subtree rooted at $m$ --- including $m$ itself --- with $D_n \neq \emptyset$.
    Then
\[ sp(m) = \frac{1}{|A^{*}_m|} \sum_{a \in A^{*}_m} p(a) . \]
    For $m$ being the taxonomic root, $A^{*}_m$ comprises all $k$-mers of $d$ that reside at a node with data taxa underneath, and $sp(m)$ hence assesses the database as a whole; for $m$ being a genus, it assesses the refinement of that genus in isolation.
    The two averages are undefined when the set of $k$-mers they range over is empty, i.e.\ $np(n)$ for a node without $k$-mers and $sp(m)$ for a subtree without any.
    Moreover, given the transformation from Figure \ref{fttrans}, it follows by definition that $np(n) = 1$ for a node $n$ of a data taxon $t$ that stores $k$-mers at all.

    In both averages, every stored $k$-mer contributes equally, regardless of how the taxonomy happens to distribute the $k$-mers over nodes. In particular, inserting an inner node into the taxonomy changes neither $\nu(a)$ nor $|D_{\nu(a)}|$ nor $c(a)$ for any $k$-mer already stored, so neither $np$ nor $sp$ can be improved by refining the tree alone.
    Note also that $np(n)$ may deteriorate if data taxa's genomes under $n$ are \emph{incomplete}, because in this case a $k$-mer $a$ at node $n$ might simply not be supported by such taxa, even though their completed genome would contain $a$.

    \subsection{Database refinement performance}

Since a refined database is always generated \emph{on top of} an existing, unrefined one, the relevant cost of the refinement is its \emph{additional} resource consumption. Table \ref{ftdbperf} therefore reports the wall time and the maximum RAM of the unrefined database generation in absolute terms, but the corresponding figures of the subsequent refinement relative to them.

As can be seen, the refinement typically costs less than the generation of the unrefined database it builds upon: for \texttt{protozoa} and \texttt{parasites} it stays below two thirds of the wall time, for \texttt{tb} below 85 \% and for \texttt{vineyard} still below 100 \%. The database \texttt{cv} forms an exception with 184 \%, i.e. the refinement takes almost twice as long as the original database generation. This is because the refinement's runtime is essentially driven by the number of $k$-mers residing on refinement ranks and by the number of subtrees each of them must be tested against, whereas the generation time of the unrefined database is dominated by reading and hashing the underlying genomic sequences. With 14,988 species and below (see Table \ref{dbdesc}), \texttt{cv} is by far the most species-rich database considered here, so that testing a single $k$-mer becomes correspondingly expensive.
Regarding main memory, the refinement stays below the unrefined database generation for the larger databases, since it does not need to hold the sequence-related data structures of the latter. Only for \texttt{vineyard}, the smallest database in our collection, it exceeds them slightly with 111 \%, because the refinement must keep the $k$-mer store and the Bloom filter of the goal \verb|kmerindexbloom| in memory at the same time, which for such a small database outweighs what its generation ever allocated.
Most notably, the refinement is almost for free in terms of disk space: a refined database is at most 2.5 \% larger than the corresponding unrefined one. This is because the refinement does not add any $k$-mers to the database --- it only reassigns them and adds a comparatively small number of new tree nodes.

    \section{Conclusions}\label{discussion}

% TODO (added 2026-08-02): argument recorded for later polishing.
A further reason why a refinement does not carry over to Kraken 1 or KrakenUniq is more fundamental than the effort of the refinement itself: the \emph{lossless} broad coverage database that it would have to build upon can hardly be generated in the first place. Already in 2018, producing a database for Kraken 1 --- and thus equivalently for KrakenUniq --- that merely covers the bacterial genomes from RefSeq \enquote{required ca. 2.5 TB of RAM and ca. 11 days} on a suitable computer \citep{Nasko:2018aa}, and RefSeq has expanded considerably ever since, as laid out in Section \ref{problem}. The first study on Genestrip therefore concluded that $k$-mer databases with very broad and comprehensive coverage have reached a \enquote{dead-end} when combined with a lossless storing of $k$-mers \citep{genestrip-pub}. A refinement inherits this verdict, since it presupposes precisely such a lossless database, as we detail at the end of this section. On top of that, it would add its own effort, which is essentially governed by the number of $k$-mers residing on refinement ranks \emph{multiplied by} the number of subtrees each of them must be tested against. Both factors grow with the taxonomic breadth of the reference set, and our own measurements already reflect this: for \texttt{cv}, by far the most species-rich database considered here with 14,988 species, the refinement takes almost twice as long as the generation of the unrefined database, whereas it stays well below that for all smaller ones (Table \ref{ftdbperf}). Kraken 2 and Ganon do evade this memory barrier --- but only by compressing $k$-mers, which forfeits the very property a refinement relies on. Taken together, the two options thus close in on each other: stored losslessly, a broad coverage database can barely be built, and compressed, it can no longer be refined.

Simply shrinking such a database is not a way out either, and this is where Genestrip's particular design becomes essential. As the first study on Genestrip has shown, a database restricted to just the genomes of the organisms of interest provokes many false positives: a large fraction of a species' $k$-mers are unspecific in the sense that they also occur in genomes of organisms \emph{not} contained in the database, so that a read composed of such $k$-mers is falsely attributed to a species that happens to be present \citep{genestrip-pub}. Genestrip escapes this dilemma because its LCA update is \emph{comprehensive}: it takes all taxa and associated genomes under the relevant RefSeq categories into account, not merely those whose $k$-mers are eventually stored (see Section \ref{dbrefinement} and the placeholder \enquote{OTHER} in Figure \ref{ftother}). A Genestrip database may therefore remain small \emph{and} precise at the same time --- and it is exactly this combination that renders a refinement computationally affordable to begin with.

Finally, it is worth noting that a refinement is tied to an \emph{exact} representation of $k$-mers. Genestrip stores $k$-mers uncompressed, so that each of them remains individually identifiable in the database \citep{genestrip-pub}. Only this property allows the refinement to decide \emph{per $k$-mer} which species share it and, consequently, how far down it may be pushed. The $k$-mer compressing databases of Kraken 2 and Ganon deliberately give up that property: they either store $k$-mers under loss of information or as members of probabilistic filters, so that a database entry no longer corresponds to a uniquely identifiable $k$-mer \citep{kraken2, Piro:2020aa, lowmemkraken, genestrip-pub}. A refinement on such a representation would have to attribute a more specific taxon to an entry that stands for several $k$-mers with potentially differing sets of containing species, and it could thereby push a $k$-mer below the node that its genomes justify --- which is precisely the error that the LCA update exists to prevent. In this sense, compression and refinement pursue opposite directions: compression discards exactly those distinctions on which a refinement relies. The price for keeping them is low though, since the refinement itself increases the size of a database by at most 2.5 \% (see Table \ref{ftdbperf}).

\subsection{Path precision and path recall}\label{apppathprecision}

Section \ref{pathcorrectness} states path correctness as a boolean property and Section \ref{nodeprecision} assesses refinement quality per node. Both can equivalently be expressed by counting $k$-mers per data taxon, which yields the more conventional pair of precision and recall. We develop it here for reference and to relate it to the measures used in the main text.

Let $t$ be a data taxon of a database $d$, as defined in Section \ref{pathcorrectness}. The following counts assess the quality of the taxonomic refinement per data taxon $t$ in $d$:
\begin{description}
    \item \textbf{Positive counts $P$ per data taxon $t$ in database $d$:} The number of unique $k$-mers in $d$ that occur in the genome associated with $t$.

    \item \textbf{True positive counts $TP$ per data taxon $t$ in database $d$:} The number of unique $k$-mers in $d$ that occur in the genome associated with $t$ where in $d$, such $k$-mers are associated with some taxon on the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root including $t$ itself.

    \item \textbf{True positive plus false positive counts $TP + FP$ per data taxon $t$ in database $d$:} The number of unique $k$-mers in $d$ assigned to any taxon on the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root including $t$ itself.
\end{description}

   These counts allow for defining \emph{path precision} as $TP / (TP + FP)$ and \emph{path recall} as $TP / P$.

   As an example, consider Figure \ref{ftsketch} from above: In (3), the path precision of $Z$ is $2/3$ because there are three $2$-mers on the path from $Z$ to the root $W$ but one of them, \texttt{CT}, is not in $W$'s sequence. In (3), $X$ and $Y$ both have a path precision of $1$. The refined database in (4) has a precision of 1 for $X$, $Y$ \emph{and} $Z$. Path recall is $1$ for all of the cases in (3) and (4).

    Path recall is the counting counterpart of path correctness: a $k$-mer $a$ that occurs in the genome of $t$ contributes to $P$ in any case, but to $TP$ only if $\nu(a)$ lies on the path from $t$ to the root. Hence $TP = P$ for a data taxon $t$ exactly if the condition of Section \ref{pathcorrectness} holds for every $k$-mer supported by $t$, and \emph{a database is path correct if and only if its path recall equals one for every data taxon}. Since both the LCA update and the refinement establish that condition by construction, path recall carries no information beyond the boolean statement, which is why the main text uses the latter.

    Path precision, in contrast, is a genuine quality measure and an alternative to node precision. We report unweighted\footnote{The unweighted average considers every data taxon as equally relevant.} average path precision by averaging over all data taxa in $d$ or subsets thereof, in particular over all taxa under the same species or the same genus. E.g., in (3) of Figure \ref{ftsketch}, the average path precision of the genus $W$ is 8/9.

    The counts of a data taxon $t$ above refer to the entire path from $t$ to the taxonomic root. As for node precision, it is useful to relativize them to a subtree: let $m$ be a node of $d$'s taxonomy tree and let $D_m$ be the set of data taxa under $m$ including $m$ itself. For a data taxon $t \in D_m$, let $TP_m(t)$ and $(TP + FP)_m(t)$ be defined as above, but with respect to the path from $t$ to $m$ -- including $t$ and $m$ themselves -- instead of the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root. Besides the unweighted average, we then consider the \emph{weighted} average path precision of the subtree rooted at $m$,\footnote{We deliberately refrain from smoothing $\overline{pp}_w(m)$ -- or $np(n)$ from Section \ref{nodeprecision} -- by a Laplace correction, i.e.\ from adding a virtual $k$-mer per node that counts as a true positive by definition. Such a correction contributes the same amount to numerator and denominator, and since the ratio is at most one, every contribution of this kind strictly raises it -- without a single $k$-mer of $d$ supporting the increase. Its total size grows with the number of nodes of the subtree, and the refinement is precisely a procedure that \emph{adds} nodes to the taxonomy. A corrected measure would therefore reward a refined tree over an unrefined one for its mere node count, and it would do so the more the deeper the refined subtrees become, i.e.\ exactly along the axis the measure is meant to assess.}
\[ \overline{pp}_w(m) = \frac{\sum_{t \in D_m} TP_m(t)}{\sum_{t \in D_m} (TP + FP)_m(t)} , \]
    which relates the pooled counts instead of averaging the per-taxon ratios. For $m$ being the taxonomic root, it ranges over all data taxa of $d$ and hence assesses the database as a whole. The measure is undefined if no $k$-mer at all resides in the subtree rooted at $m$.

    Note that for a taxonomy tree with a branching factor $\geq 2$ (which is the standard case) a high average path precision ensures that a $k$-mer $a$ in $d$ is located as closely as possible above a data taxon or right at a data taxon whose genome(s) contain $a$. Otherwise, if $a$ was located higher up in the taxonomy, then $a$ would also be on the path to the root starting from a data taxon $t$ whose genome does not contain $a$. But this would increase the $FP$-count of $t$ and in turn would lower the average path precision.

    Grouping the pooled counts by the node holding each $k$-mer rather than by data taxon shows how $\overline{pp}_w(m)$ relates to the subtree precision $sp(m)$ of Section \ref{nodeprecision}. Both average the per-$k$-mer precision $p(a)$ over the $k$-mers of the subtree, but $\overline{pp}_w(m)$ weights each $k$-mer by $|D_{\nu(a)}|$, i.e.\ by the number of data taxa its node covers, whereas $sp(m)$ weights all $k$-mers equally. Path precision therefore lets $k$-mers sitting high in the taxonomy dominate the value, while subtree precision does not, which is the reason the main text builds on the latter.

\subsection{Phylogenetic Mapping}

Genestrip-FT's database refinement lends itself to a comprehensive visualization of even large phylogeny trees with evolutionary distances being integrated. Resulting tree representations refine the existing NCBI taxonomy \cite{taxonomy} and derive from a refined $k$-mer database. Here, we briefly discuss computational aspects for embedding the evolutionary distance in such trees.

   \subsubsection{The evolutionary distance $d$}
    
We briefly introduce an approximation of the evolutionary distance $d$ as a ratio of mutations in genomes or sequences, respectively. The approximation has been derived before in \cite{skmer} where it is expressed via the Jaccard index over shared $k$-mers between genomes. Besides, \cite{Ondov:2016aa} pointed out a similar approximation. For clarity of discussion, we also derive $d$, but express it in terms of counts available in a Genestrip database and related taxonomy trees. Afterwards, we build on these considerations to adequately integrate $d$ into depicted, refined trees such as produced via Genestrip-FT. 
    
The following assumptions are essentially the same as in \cite{skmer} and \cite{Ondov:2016aa}:
    Let $|s|$ be the length of a sequence $s$ transformed to a sequence $s'$ via mutations of bases. We assume that $|s| = |s'|$ and consider the number of observable mutations $m$, i.e. all base positions $1 \leq i \leq |s|$ where $s(i) \neq s'(i)$. We also assume that these mutations are uniformly distributed over $s'$.
    Let $X$ be a random variable for the number of differing $k$-mers between $s$ and $s'$ depending on the $m$ mutations. For simplicity we assume that there are no duplicate $k$-mers in either $s$ or $s'$ so that $X$ is also the number of differing \emph{unique} $k$-mers. As our last simplification, we consider observations of $k$-mers within $s$ and analogously within $s'$ to be mutually independent. 

    A mutation occurs at a chance of $m/|s|$. So the probability for a changed $k$-mer, which must contain at least one mutation, is $p(m) = 1 - (1 - m/|s|)^k$. Since the number of $k$-mers in $s$ and $s'$ is $|s|-k+1$, $X$ follows the binomial distribution $X \sim B(|s|-k+1, p(m))$.         
    In our context, \emph{we measure the number $E$ of unique $k$-mers differing between $s$ and $s'$ but we want to estimate the distance $d:=m/|s|$ as a ratio of mutations over the sequence $s$}. We therefore approximate $E \approx E[X] = (|s|-k+1) \cdot p(m)$ and solve by $d$:
 \begin{equation}
d \approx 1 - \sqrt[k]{1 - E / (|s| - k + 1)} \approx 1 - \sqrt[k]{1 - E / |s|} =: d(E/|s|)\label{distanceapprox} 	
 \end{equation} 
Figure \ref{nfunc} shows a plot of the function $d(E/|s|)$: It starts with a slope of $1/k$ because each mutation affects about $k$ $k$-mers (those spanning the mutated position), so $E \approx k \cdot m$ for small $m$, giving $d = m/|s| \approx (E/|s|)/k$. The estimate $d(E/|s|)$ becomes very sensitive, i.e. less reliable, when $E$ reaches $|s|$. For practical considerations, we therefore indicate it in taxonomy trees, when $d$ exceeds a configurable threshold $t_d$.

\subsubsection{Embedding of $d$ in taxonomy trees}

We want to apply $d$ to a node $n$ in the context of a taxonomy tree such as in Figure \ref{simplerefdb}. More precisely, we estimate $d_n$ to be the maximum evolutionary distance \emph{from $n$'s parent to the data taxa\footnote{See Section~\ref{intrinsicqms} for the definition of the term \enquote{data taxon}.} under $n$}:
\begin{itemize}
\item
For that, $above(n)$ shall sum over the $k$-mers assigned to nodes above $n$ towards the tree's root excluding $n$. 
\item 
Regarding $below(n)$, we consider each path starting from $n$ to any leaf node under $n$ including $n$ itself. We sum over the $k$-mers assigned to all nodes along each path and set $below(n)$ to be the maximum sum from such paths. $p_n$ denotes some path leading to the maximum sum of $below(n)$. 
\end{itemize}
We set $d_n := d(E_n/|s_n|)$ by letting $E_n := below(n)$ and $|s_n| := above(n) + below(n)$.
This way, $d_n$ measures the evolutionary distance from $n$'s parent to the leaf of $p_n$. 
Since we use the maximum sum in $below(n)$, this estimate is conservative for $n$.
However, to depict evolutionary distances in a given taxonomy tree we must represent the portion $dp_n$ of $d_n$ relating to the branch from a node $n$'s parent to $n$ itself.
Let $c$ be the child of $n$ on the path $p_n$. It can be shown that $d_n \geq d_c$,\footnote{$E_n = below(n) = k_n + below(c) = k_n + E_c \geq E_c$ (where $k_n \geq 0$ is the number of $k$-mers stored at $n$ itself), while $|s_n| = above(n) + below(n) = above(n) + k_n + E_c = above(c) + below(c) = |s_c|$, so $E_n/|s_n| \geq E_c/|s_c|$, and therefore $d_n \geq d_c$ by monotonicity of $d$.} so we simply set $dp_n := d_n - d_{c}\geq 0$.

Figure \ref{simplerefdbdist} depicts a resulting taxonomy tree for the same database as in Figure \ref{simplerefdb} but with horizontal line lengths encoding distance portions.\footnote{We configuratively set the horizontal line lengths of the highest ranked nodes to a minimum although their $dp_n$ might be large. \subsection{Evolutionary distance distribution}

\subsubsection{Posterior distribution for the evolutionary distance}

We derive the probability distribution of $d$ given the observed count $E$ of differing unique $k$-mers, using Bayes' theorem. Let $N := |s|-k+1$ be the number of $k$-mers in $s$. We adopt a uniform prior $\pi(d) = 1$ on $d\in[0,1]$, reflecting no preference for any particular mutation rate. The binomial model from above gives the likelihood
\[
  P(E \mid d) = \binom{N}{E}\,p(d)^E\,[1-p(d)]^{N-E}, \qquad p(d) = 1-(1-d)^k.
\]
By Bayes' theorem the posterior satisfies
\[
  P(d \mid E) \;\propto\; [1-(1-d)^k]^E\,(1-d)^{k(N-E)}.
\]
To identify the normalising constant we substitute $v := (1-d)^k$, so $d = 1-v^{1/k}$ and $\mathrm{d}d = \frac{1}{k}\,v^{1/k-1}\,\mathrm{d}v$. The posterior density in $v$ becomes
\[
  P(v \mid E) \;\propto\; v^{\,N-E+\frac{1}{k}-1}(1-v)^{E},
\]
which is recognised as a $\mathrm{Beta}\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\;E+1\right)$ density. Hence the normalised posterior for $d$ is
\begin{equation}
  P(d \mid E) \;=\;
  \frac{k\,(1-d)^{k(N-E)}\bigl[1-(1-d)^k\bigr]^E}
       {B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\;E+1\right)},
  \qquad d\in[0,1].
  \label{eq:dposterior}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Posterior mean and variance}

Since $d = 1-v^{1/k}$ with $v\sim\mathrm{Beta}\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\,E+1\right)$, and the $t$-th moment of a $\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$ variable equals $B(\alpha+t,\beta)/B(\alpha,\beta)$, the posterior mean is
\begin{equation}
  E[d\mid E] \;=\; 1 \;-\;
  \frac{B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{2}{k},\;E+1\right)}
       {B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\;E+1\right)}.
  \label{eq:dpostmean}
\end{equation}
For the variance we use $\mathrm{Var}(d\mid E) = \mathrm{Var}(v^{1/k}\mid E) = E[v^{2/k}\mid E]-(E[v^{1/k}\mid E])^2$, giving
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Var}(d\mid E) \;=\;
  \frac{B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{3}{k},\;E+1\right)}
       {B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\;E+1\right)}
  \;-\;
  \left(\frac{B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{2}{k},\;E+1\right)}
             {B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\;E+1\right)}\right)^{\!2}.
  \label{eq:dpostvar}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Delta method approximation for large $|s|$}

For genomic sequences $|s|$ is in the millions, so the posterior concentrates tightly around the point estimate $\hat{d} = d(E/|s|) = 1-(1-E/|s|)^{1/k}$ from Eq.~\eqref{distanceapprox}. Setting $\hat{v} = (1-\hat{d})^k$, the posterior variance of $v$ is approximately $\mathrm{Var}(v\mid E)\approx \hat{v}(1-\hat{v})/|s|$. Applying the delta method to $d = g(v) = 1-v^{1/k}$ with $g'(v) = -\tfrac{1}{k}\,v^{1/k-1}$ yields
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Var}(d\mid E) \;\approx\;
  \frac{(1-\hat{d})^{2-k}\bigl[1-(1-\hat{d})^k\bigr]}{k^2\,|s|}.
  \label{eq:ddeltavar}
\end{equation}
An approximate $\pm 1\sigma$ credible interval for $d$ is therefore $\hat{d}\,\pm\,\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(d\mid E)}$. The bands are tight for moderate $E/|s|$ but widen rapidly as $E$ approaches $|s|$, confirming that the estimate becomes unreliable in that regime.
