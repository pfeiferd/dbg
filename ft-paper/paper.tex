  title={#1}, title style={font=\footnotesize},

    \section{Background}\label{background}

    \subsection{Introduction}
\label{problem}

$k$-mer based classification tools such as Kraken 1 \citep{kraken}, Kraken 2 \citep{kraken2}, KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq} and Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub} assign reads to taxa by matching their $k$-mers against a database that maps each $k$-mer to a node of a taxonomy tree. Since a $k$-mer may occur in the genomes of several organisms, a $k$-mer database is subjected to the \emph{lowest common ancestor update} (LCA update): originally introduced for metagenomic classification by MEGAN \citep{megan} and now applied by many $k$-mer database tools, it moves a $k$-mer occurring in the genome of more than one taxon up to the lowest node that holds all corresponding taxa as descendants. Later, at classification time, the LCA update prevents $k$-mers in reads from being falsely assigned to an overly low-ranked taxon, since genomes of other taxa such as siblings contain that $k$-mer too. Thus, essentially, the LCA update avoids a potentially false assignment of overly specific taxa to reads.

Unfortunately, the LCA update comes at a price that originates in the ever-growing number of genomes from underlying source databases such as RefSeq \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038, refseqstats}: as Nasko et al. \citep{Nasko:2018aa} have shown, it leads to a growing set of species and corresponding genomes under a single genus.
    \emph{Each newly added species closely related to an already covered one turns a $k$-mer that used to be specific to a single species into a shared one, and so the LCA update moves it up towards the genus.} Consequently, with newer RefSeq versions \emph{more} reads are classified in total, but \emph{fewer} of them are classified on the species level \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. In other words, the resolution of $k$-mer databases deteriorates while the underlying genomic data deepens.
    We observed the same effect in our first study on Genestrip, where databases with a deeper genomic coverage for organisms of interest classified more reads but tended to accumulate them on higher ranks \citep{genestrip-pub}.

% Nasko et al. put numbers to this. Bacterial RefSeq has doubled in size every 1.5 years since 2003, and its unique $31$-mers have grown at much the same rate; measured against nine of its versions, the share of reads classified at the species level peaked at version 30 and declined from there on, while genus-level assignments kept rising \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. Nothing has slowed since: the number of prokaryotic genomes has grown by a further factor of 5.8 \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038}, and in the two years to release 236 the number of covered organisms rose by about 20 \% from 152,668 to 182,465 \citep{refseqstats}.

Figure \ref{kmerongenusborrelia} illustrates the effect on the distribution of $k$-mers across ranks for two Genestrip databases, each covering twelve genera. For the bacterial one, a median of about 68 \% of a species' $k$-mers no longer reside at the species rank or below but somewhere between the genus and the species rank, whereas for the protozoan one a median of about 90 \% of them remain at the species rank or below.
    Both databases rest exclusively on RefSeq genomes, and bacteria are covered by far more close relatives than protozoa: release 236 comprises 109,133 bacterial but merely 944 protozoan species \citep{refseqstats}. The LCA update pushes the $k$-mers of a bacterial species up accordingly.

    At first glance, such $k$-mers appear to be lost for fine-grained classification, but Figure \ref{kmerviaranks} shows that it does not need to be that way: for the $k$-mers that ended up on a genus, we determined the number of the genus' children whose genomes actually contain them.
    A median of about 84 \% and 88 \% of them, for the bacterial and the protozoan database respectively, occur in just \emph{two} of the children, and the share drops steeply for higher numbers. So a $k$-mer sitting on a genus is typically not characteristic of the genus as a whole but just of a small group of species under it. The LCA update simply has no better place to put it, because the taxonomy is too coarse-grained. \emph{This is the potential that our approach exploits}: if the taxonomy is \emph{refined} by additional nodes, then the very same $k$-mers can potentially be pushed back down and become more informative again.

    \end{figure}

    To the best of our knowledge, none of the established $k$-mer based classification tools makes use of this potential. Reads whose $k$-mers would suffice to narrow the candidates down to two or three species are therefore often reported on the genus, even though the information about which species actually share these $k$-mers sits in the underlying genomes.

One route to improving the database is to correct the classification results afterwards. E.g., Bracken \citep{lu2017bracken} re-estimates species-level abundances from the output of a Kraken-style classification by redistributing the reads assigned to higher ranks according to a background distribution, which describes how the $k$-mers of each species in the database are expected to spread across the taxonomy. The approach differs from a refinement of the database itself: First, Bracken does not add information but reallocates counts on the basis of \emph{probabilistic assumptions} about which species are present and how their $k$-mers distribute. Its estimates degrade when a sample deviates from these assumptions, as for rare or unexpected organisms. Second, it operates on aggregated abundances of an entire sample rather than on individual reads, so that a single read remains classified on the genus even after the re-estimation. A refined database, in contrast, relies on \emph{hard data}: which species actually share a given $k$-mer is known exactly from the underlying genomes, and this knowledge is encoded in the database itself. Each read is then classified more specifically by construction rather than by estimation. 

HTTMM \citep{httmm} estimates an abundance for every node of the taxonomy, for the internal ones as well as for the genomes at the leaves, so that sequence shared among relatives is reported at the clade rather than pushed down to a species. Yet the probability that a node accounts for a read is assumed as under Bracken. However, a genome under a taxon $z$ with $r$ successors is given the fixed probability $1/r$, which is independent of the sequences and reflects nothing but the shape of the taxonomy. The nodes the reads are reported at are those of the given taxonomy. Within a node every genome is held equally likely to have produced a read, although some are far likelier than others --- a limitation the authors name themselves and leave to future work. In contrast, our approach adds new taxonomy nodes, and it replaces the assumed $1/r$ by the $k$-mers themselves. So like Bracken, HTTMM relies on probabilistic assumptions whereas our approach relies on hard data.

    \subsection{Approach}
    \label{approach}
    Our contribution builds on the first study on Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub} by further improving precision of read classifications without sacrificing recall. Genestrip-FT refines the taxonomy tree of a $k$-mer database by grouping those taxa that share many $k$-mers, and it reassigns the affected $k$-mers to the newly added nodes. In doing so, a $k$-mer is only pushed down as far as it may still be attributed to all taxa whose genomes contain it, so that no read is ever classified below the level justified by the underlying genomes.

Figure \ref{ftsketch} captures our approach with regard to a simplistic $k$-mer database
with $k = 2$:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Species $X$, $Y$, $Z$ under a common genus $W$ have respective sequences associated. 
	\item After filling the database, the $2$-mers are assigned to their species. The figure simplifies the state of the database after this step.
	\item The LCA update pushes the $2$-mer \texttt{CT} up to $W$ because it occurs in the sequence of both $X$ and $Y$. However, it does not occur in $Z$. So, according to this database, a read \texttt{CT} would just be classified as $W$ although $Z$ is not even a candidate species for \texttt{CT}.
	\item Genestrip-FT's refinement adds an artificial node $h$ to the taxonomy with $X$ and $Y$ as children but not $Z$. Also, the refinement \emph{pushes down} \texttt{CT} from $W$ to $h$. According to this refined database, a read \texttt{CT} would be classified as $h$ and so $Z$ is excluded as a candidate species for \texttt{CT}.
\end{enumerate}
 So in general, this type of refinement potentially improves classification precision: E.g., if a read was classified under a genus taxon in an unrefined database, then \emph{the corresponding refined database may allow for narrowing down the candidate species to fewer than originally listed under that genus}.
 Note that the refinement only moves $k$-mers, so a refined database stores exactly the same $k$-mers as the unrefined one it was derived from.

Genestrip structures the complex computation of a $k$-mer database via goals as explained in \cite{genestrip-pub}. The goals form a dependency graph where a goal $g_1$ is executed only if its computational result is needed by a dependent goal $g_2$, and if $g_1$'s result has not been computed yet. Genestrip-FT is implemented as an extension of the original goal graph formed by Genestrip. Figure \ref{ftgoals} presents an extract of the additional goals, with most of Genestrip's original goals omitted. 

  \begin{figure}[!htbp]%
        \centering        
\includegraphics[width=8cm]{figs/ft-goals-extract.pdf}
         \caption{Extract of additional goals required by Genestrip-FT for the refinement of a corresponding $k$-mer database. The file goal \texttt{ftdb}, prefixed with \texttt{f:} stores the refined database as a file and depends on several, so-called object goals prefixed with \texttt{o:}. In the following, we briefly explain the essential goals needed for the computation of a refined database:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \verb|kmerindexsize| estimates how many $k$-mer-to-species associations a Bloom filter of the following goal, \verb|kmerindexbloom|, will have to hold, so that the filter can be sized for them. By default Genestrip estimates that count with HyperLogLog \cite{Flajolet07hyperloglog}.

	\item \verb|kmerindexbloom| associates each $k$-mer stored on the genus rank with the original species taxon of the sequence in which it occurred. To achieve this, the original sequences that were used to generate the unrefined database must all be reread. The mapping of a $k$-mer to its original species taxon is hashed; the resulting hash code is stored via a Bloom filter \cite{bloomfilter} in order to save main memory.\footnote{A Bloom filter is a probabilistic data structure for an efficient and compressed storing of values but also for a fast lookup of entered values. However, this comes at the expense of a configurable, usually low false positive rate at lookup time. Bloom filters and probabilistic filters in general \cite{10.1145/2674005.2674994,9920402,10.1145/3510449,10.1145/3376122} are widely used in the context of metagenomics tools \cite{biobloom, Piro:2020aa, 10.1093/nargab/lqaf094} and beyond.}
	
	\item \verb|intersectcount| computes the number of common $k$-mers $c(t_1, t_2)$ between any two species taxa $t_1$ and $t_2$ under the same genus. To do so, it consults the Bloom filter produced via the goal \verb|kmerindexbloom|. Based on $c(t_1, t_2)$, it also determines a Laplace-smoothed Jaccard index
	\[ J(t_1, t_2) = \frac{c(t_1, t_2) + 1}{c(t_1, t_1) + c(t_2, t_2) - c(t_1, t_2) + 1} \]
	\cite{jaccard} as a similarity measure of the two sets of $k$-mers belonging to $t_1$ and $t_2$ on the genus, respectively. The Laplace correction adds $1$ to both the intersection count (numerator) and each self-count (denominator), which avoids division by zero when both species share no $k$-mers on the genus and yields $J = 1$ in that degenerate case. Regarding our running example, Table \ref{intersectioncounts} shows the raw intersection counts whereas Table \ref{jaccard} states the resulting Jaccard indices. For simplicity, we skip the background of the column \enquote{OTHER} for now --- it will be explained at the end of this section.

	\item The goal \verb|dendrogram| from Figure \ref{ftgoals} computes a dendrogram using the standard agglomerative clustering algorithm \enquote{HAC} \cite{King01031967,1394399}, where the cluster distance is based on the Jaccard index $J$ from \verb|intersectcount|. Since the taxonomic refinement is governed by the aim of improving classification precision (see Section \ref{approach}), we chose \enquote{Single Linkage} \cite{King01031967} as the default linkage strategy. This linkage strategy is suitable because it merges clusters on the basis of the most similar $k$-mer sets they contain. Nevertheless, other linkage strategies such as UPGMA and WPGMA \cite{Sokal1958ASM} may be configured instead. Figure \ref{dendrogram} depicts the resulting dendrogram as computed on the basis of Table \ref{jaccard}.

	\item The goal \verb|ftupdatedb| reorganizes the database's taxonomy tree by adding new tree nodes under a genus as induced by the previous goal's dendrogram. Furthermore, $k$-mers are pushed down, i.e. reassigned, to the newly added nodes accordingly. In doing so, each $k$-mer is pushed down only as far as a new node $n$ such that all species with sequences containing that $k$-mer still remain descendants of $n$.

	\item Finally, \verb|ftdb| stores the refined database as a file.
\end{enumerate} 

	Figure \ref{simplerefdb} depicts the refined taxonomy for the Orthopoxvirus example and also states the number of $k$-mers as stored per node in the refined database. As can be seen, about 43 \% of the $k$-mers that originally resided on the genus Orthopoxvirus got pushed down to the newly added nodes, leaving the remaining 57 \% there (see Figure \ref{simplelcadb} for comparison). The new nodes obtain an artificial taxon prefixed with \enquote{000} to distinguish them from regular taxonomic entries, and their artificial rank is set to \enquote{REFINED}.

The Jaccard index over $k$-mer sets is not specific to Genestrip: it underlies a family of alignment-free tools for comparing sequence collections. Mash \cite{Ondov:2016aa} reduces each genome or read set to a MinHash sketch of a few thousand hash values, estimates $J$ from these sketches and converts it into a mutation distance. Skmer \cite{skmer} builds on such sketches for unassembled low-coverage \enquote{genome skims}, correcting the estimate for sequencing depth and error so that a sample can be identified without being assembled. Kmer-db \cite{kmerdb} forgoes sketching altogether and computes $J$ exactly over the complete $k$-mer sets, which it keeps as a compressed sparse binary matrix in order to obtain all pairwise distances among thousands of genomes at once.

What sets \verb|intersectcount| apart is its purpose: The tools above compare genomes or sequencing samples with one another, and the resulting distance is their end product. Here, $c(t_1, t_2)$ is computed over the $k$-mer sets that the database itself assigns to the species taxa under a common genus --- sets that already result from the LCA update and that include the placeholder \enquote{OTHER} discussed above. The index therefore does not serve the computation of an evolutionary distance, but is merely a means for refinement: it feeds the clustering of \verb|dendrogram|, which in turn restructures the taxonomy via \verb|ftupdatedb|.

\subsubsection{Data taxa and path correctness}\label{pathcorrectness}
    Let $t$ be a \emph{data taxon}, i.e., \emph{a taxon with sequence data directly associated whereby the $k$-mers of those sequences are also stored in $d$}. For example in RefSeq, genomes are often associated with taxa of the rank species or below. Moreover, let $\nu(b)$ denote the node at which a $k$-mer $b$ is stored in $d$; since each $k$-mer is stored exactly once, $\nu$ is well defined. Throughout, every taxon and node named below belongs to the taxonomy tree of $d$ and every $k$-mer to what $d$ stores; we write $w \preceq n$ for a node $w$ equal to $n$ or below $n$ in that tree, and $w \prec n$ where it lies strictly below.

For a fair assessment of the measures below, we require that each data taxon be a leaf node in the taxonomy tree. This can easily be enforced by a tree transformation explained via (3) and (4) in Figure \ref{fttrans}. Without it, a data taxon would compete against its own descendants.\footnote{Since the data taxa under $n$ include $n$ itself, a $k$-mer occurring in that taxon's sequences alone is stored at its node, yet counted against every data taxon below it as well --- and those taxa have no reason to carry it. Its precision would fall short of one although no lower node could hold it, so the measure would penalise a placement that is in fact optimal, and the penalty would grow with the number of data taxa that happen to sit underneath (as in Figure \ref{fttrans} (1) and (2)).} Note that the described transformation leaves the phylogenetic structure of a given taxonomy intact.
Figure \ref{simplerefdbtrans} exemplifies the transformation when integrated into the refinement of the database from Figure \ref{simplelcadb}.\footnote{When carefully comparing the $k$-mer counts of similar nodes from Figures \ref{simplerefdb} and \ref{simplerefdbtrans}, one finds minor differences such as for \enquote{10245/OTHER}. Note that these differences originate in the use of a probabilistic data structure, i.e. a Bloom filter, during the refinement procedure according to Section \ref{dbrefinement}.}

    On this basis, we call a $k$-mer database $d$ \emph{path correct} iff \emph{$t \preceq \nu(b)$ for every stored $k$-mer $b$ and every data taxon $t$ whose sequences contain $b$}. Both unrefined and refined databases are path correct by construction: The LCA update moves a $k$-mer up in the taxonomy just far enough that all data taxa whose sequences contain it become descendants of the resulting node. The refinement, in turn, may push a $k$-mer down only so far that the condition above is still maintained --- this is what keeps \verb|ftupdatedb| (see Section \ref{dbrefinement}) from placing a $k$-mer below the LCA of the data taxa supporting it.\footnote{Due to its probabilistic nature, the Bloom filter used during the \texttt{kmerindexbloom} step (see Section~\ref{dbrefinement}) occasionally falsely attributes a species to a $k$-mer, which may prevent the $k$-mer from being pushed down to its lowest possible node. This leaves path correctness intact and only keeps the $k$-mer at a less specific node than necessary.}

\subsubsection{Subtree precision}\label{subtreeprecision}
    For a node $n$ let
\[ D(n) = \{ t \preceq n : t \text{ is a data taxon} \} \]
    be the data taxa equal to $n$ or below $n$. For a stored $k$-mer $b$, with its node $\nu(b)$ from Section \ref{pathcorrectness}, let
\[ C(b) = \{ t \in D(\nu(b)) : b \text{ occurs in a sequence filed at } t \} \]
    be those of them that support it. We define the \emph{$k$-mer precision} $\mathit{kprec}(b)$ as
\[ \mathit{kprec}(b) = \frac{|C(b)|}{|D(\nu(b))|} , \]
    i.e.\ as the fraction of the data taxa under $b$'s node whose sequences actually contain $b$. It is always defined: $b$ was read from a sequence of a data taxon $t$, and path correctness gives $t \preceq \nu(b)$, so $D(\nu(b))$ is never empty. It also makes $C(b)$ independent of where $b$ is stored: $C(b)$ is simply the set of data taxa that support $b$. Only $|D(\nu(b))|$ varies with the placement, and it is smallest when $\nu(b)$ is the LCA of the data taxa supporting $b$.\footnote{We do not report an intrinsic recall for $k$-mers since it is always one by construction: writing it in the shape of the precision above, $\mathit{krec}(b) = |C(b)| \,/\, |\{ t \text{ a data taxon} : b \text{ occurs in a sequence filed at } t \}|$, path correctness from Section \ref{pathcorrectness} guarantees that the two sets of the fraction are identical.}
%    The leaf requirement of Section \ref{pathcorrectness} matters here. Without it a data taxon competes against its own descendants.
%   Cut in the shortening pass (now the footnote in Section \ref{pathcorrectness}), kept to restore easily:
%  $D(n)$ comprises the data taxa under $n$ including $n$ itself, so a $k$-mer that occurs in that taxon's sequences alone is stored at its node but counted against every data taxon below it as well, and those taxa have no reason to carry it. Its precision would fall short of one even though no lower node could hold it. The measure would then penalise an optimal placement, and the penalty would grow with the number of data taxa that happen to sit underneath (Figure \ref{fttrans} (1) and (2)).
%   Cut in the shortening pass (said more precisely at the end of this section), kept to restore easily:
%   Note that raising $\mathit{kprec}(b)$ is the very purpose of the refinement as it introduces new nodes and moves $k$-mers onto them.

    Averaging $\mathit{kprec}$ over the $k$-mers of an entire subtree yields \emph{subtree precision} $\mathit{sprec}_m$, the measure a database is assessed by. For a node $m$ let
\[ \mathcal{A}_m = \{ b : \nu(b) \preceq m \} \]
    be the $k$-mers stored in its subtree. Then
\[ \mathit{sprec}_m = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_m|} \sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}_m} \mathit{kprec}(b) . \]
    If $m$ is the taxonomic root, then $\mathcal{A}_m$ comprises every stored $k$-mer, and $\mathit{sprec}_m$ hence assesses the database as a whole; if $m$ is a genus, then it assesses the refinement of that genus in isolation. It is undefined for a subtree holding no such $k$-mer.

    After the transformation of Section \ref{pathcorrectness}, a $k$-mer $b$ stored at a data taxon has $|D(\nu(b))| = 1$ and therefore $\mathit{kprec}(b) = 1$ by construction. Such $k$-mers are the bulk of a database, as will be shown, so they dominate $\mathit{sprec}$ although they cannot be improved. We therefore report the \emph{restricted subtree precision} $\widehat{\mathit{sprec}}_m$ alongside it, taken over
\[ \widehat{\mathcal{A}}_m = \{ b \in \mathcal{A}_m : \nu(b) \text{ is not a data taxon} \} , \]
    the $k$-mers a refinement can still move:
\[ \widehat{\mathit{sprec}}_m = \frac{1}{|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_m|} \sum_{b \in \widehat{\mathcal{A}}_m} \mathit{kprec}(b) . \]
    In both averages, every stored $k$-mer contributes equally, regardless of how the taxonomy happens to distribute the $k$-mers over nodes. $\mathit{sprec}_m$ and $\widehat{\mathit{sprec}}_m$ rise only where a refinement moves a $k$-mer onto a node covering its supporting data taxa more tightly than the one it sat at.

    Note that an incomplete genome can lower subtree precision: assume $b$ occurs in the sequences of several neighbouring data taxa, so that it is stored at a common ancestor of theirs, and one of those taxa is held by a single draft assembly that happens not to cover the region $b$ lies in. That taxon is counted in $D(\nu(b))$ but not in $C(b)$, although the finished genome would contain $b$, and $\mathit{kprec}(b)$ falls below what the organisms warrant. Most assemblies of a reference database are drafts rather than closed genomes, which makes every figure reported here a lower bound.

    \subsubsection{Measuring classification quality}\label{classmeasures}

    Given a read $r$, let $\sigma(r)$ be the species taxon its underlying genome belongs to. Reads whose $\sigma(r)$ cannot be determined are left out of $R$ for the measures of this section, as they can be neither right nor wrong.

    Every classification is done with one database, and we always compare two \emph{variants} of the same one: the unrefined database, written $u$, and the refined database, written $f$ for the finer taxonomy it carries. The variant appears as a superscript throughout. E.g., $\kappa^v(r)$ is the taxon variant $v$ assigns to $r$, undefined where the read remains unclassified, and $\mathit{prec}^f$ is the precision of the refined database. The same holds for the intrinsic measures of Section \ref{subtreeprecision}, which are likewise reported for both variants of a database and hence written $\mathit{sprec}^u_m$ and $\mathit{sprec}^f_m$.

    The established way to score a read assignment is a \emph{boolean positive count}: $r$ counts as a hit if $\kappa^v(r)$ is equal to or below $\sigma(r)$, and as a miss otherwise, and likewise one rank higher with the genus in place of the species. Both variants, however, do not recognize what a refinement does. A refinement introduces nodes \emph{between} a genus and its species and moves $k$-mers onto them, so a read that used to be classified to its genus now likely ends up at one of these new nodes. That is a substantial gain since the read is narrowed down from all species of the genus to a handful. Yet the boolean count at the genus rank scores it as a hit in both cases and the one at the species rank as a miss in both cases. So, neither of them registers the improvement at all.

    We therefore refine the count in proportion to how far the assignment narrows the species down. For a node $n$ let
\[ \Sigma(n) = \{ s : s \text{ is a species taxon with } s \preceq n \text{ or } n \preceq s \} \]
    be the set of \emph{species in question} at $n$, i.e.\ the species that are still consistent with an assignment to $n$. The \emph{ratio-based positive count} of a read then is
\[ q^v(r) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{|\Sigma(\kappa^v(r))|} & \text{if } \sigma(r) \in \Sigma(\kappa^v(r)), \\[2ex] 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \]
    and $q^v(r) = 0$ for an unclassified read. So an assignment that pins the species down scores $1$, an assignment to a genus comprising $40$ species scores $1/40$, and one to a refined node covering $3$ of them scores $1/3$. In particular $q^v(r)$ agrees with the boolean count at the species rank whenever the species is actually determined, and it grades the cases in between that the boolean counts collapse. The refinement adds nodes but neither adds nor removes species taxa, so $\Sigma(n)$ is the same in the refined and the unrefined database for every node $n$ that exists in both. What differs is the node a read is assigned to, i.e.\ $\kappa^u(r)$ against $\kappa^f(r)$, which is the effect we want to measure.

    We obtain precision, recall and the $F_1$-score in the usual way: with $R$ denoting the reads of a fastq file and $R^v_c \subseteq R$ those variant $v$ classified at all,
\[ \mathit{prec}^v = \frac{\sum_{r \in R^v_c} q^v(r)}{|R^v_c|}, \qquad \mathit{rec}^v = \frac{\sum_{r \in R} q^v(r)}{|R|}, \qquad F_1^v = \frac{2 \cdot \mathit{prec}^v \cdot \mathit{rec}^v}{\mathit{prec}^v + \mathit{rec}^v} , \]
    and analogously for the boolean counts, which we report alongside. Note that a read can only profit from the refinement if the unrefined database classified it correctly at or below its genus but above its species. We therefore report the same measure a second time, restricted to exactly these reads.

    Let further the classified \emph{genus-only subset} of the unrefined database be
\[ R^u_g = \{ r \in R^u_c : \sigma(r) \prec \mathit{LCA}(\sigma(r), \kappa^u(r)) \preceq \mathit{genus}(\sigma(r)) \} , \]
    where $\mathit{LCA}$ denotes the LCA of two nodes and $\mathit{genus}(\sigma(r))$ the genus the read's own species belongs to. The right bound keeps the reads the unrefined database placed correctly at least down to their genus, and the strict left bound limits them to remain above their species. This is because a read already at its species has $\mathit{LCA}(\sigma(r), \kappa^u(r)) = \sigma(r)$ and scores at $1$ in both variants, so it has nothing to gain from a refinement. The \emph{restricted precision} of a variant is then
\[ \mathit{prec}^v_g = \frac{1}{|R^u_g|} \sum_{r \in R^u_g} q^v(r) . \]
    $R^u_g$ covers a window of ranks rather than the genus alone, because a taxonomy places further ranks inside it: NCBI's \emph{subgenus} and \emph{species group} both lie between a genus and its species, and a read left at either is left with several species in question exactly as one left at the genus is. Note that $R^u_g$ is determined by the unrefined run and then kept fixed, so that both variants are scored on the very same reads.

    \subsubsection{Estimating the gain without ground truth}\label{groundtruthfree}

    The measures from Section \ref{classmeasures} rest on $\sigma(r)$, which determines a read's species. For real-world data, where this ground truth is unknown, we can still assess how far a classification narrows the species down but not whether it does so correctly. Dropping the underlying test from $q$ leaves
\[ \tilde{q}^v(r) = \frac{1}{|\Sigma(\kappa^v(r))|} \]
    for a classified read, and $\tilde{q}^v(r) = 0$ for an unclassified one. Since $q^v(r)$ is $\tilde{q}^v(r)$ multiplied by the indicator of $\sigma(r) \in \Sigma(\kappa^v(r))$, we have $q^v(r) \le \tilde{q}^v(r)$ for each read, and hence for an average over reads as well.

    The subset $R^u_g$ is unobservable as well, since it is defined through $\sigma(r)$. Nevertheless, we can still observe where the classification came to rest. An ungated version of precision is therefore taken over
\[ \widetilde{R}^u_g = \{ r \in R^u_c : \kappa^u(r) \text{ lies at or below a genus and above any species} \} , \]
    the reads the unrefined database placed no further than a genus. The window of ranks is the same as in $R^u_g$, only the correctness condition is gone. A read already placed at a species has $\tilde{q}^v(r) = 1$ under either variant and can gain nothing from a refinement, so keeping such reads in would pull the average towards one. This gives the \emph{ungated precision}
\[ \widetilde{\mathit{prec}}^v_g = \frac{1}{|\widetilde{R}^u_g|} \sum_{r \in \widetilde{R}^u_g} \tilde{q}^v(r) \]
    standing against its gated version $\mathit{prec}^v_g$ from Section \ref{classmeasures}.

    The same removal applies to recall, but we do not report an ungated version of it. Over the restricted set $\widetilde{R}^u_g$ recall and precision share their denominator, so the two coincide there. Over all reads of a file they differ, since an unclassified read contributes $\tilde{q}^v(r) = 0$. But such a measure, i.e.\ the ungated recall over all reads, just states how much of a sample lies within the scope of a database, which depends on the sample and on the database, not on a refinement.

\section{Results}\label{results}

    \emph{The experiments below compare a database against its refined variant}, as indicated by the superscripts $u$ and $f$ from Section \ref{classmeasures}. This means that the same database is assessed twice, or the same reads are classified twice, once before the refinement and once after it, with everything else held fixed. This design is narrow but fair, whereas a comparison across tools would vary the database and the classification algorithm along with the refinement. \emph{How Genestrip compares with other $k$-mer analysis tools is a separate question that has already been answered in \citep{genestrip-pub}.} In this previous study, various unrefined Genestrip databases outperformed KrakenUniq, Kraken 2 and Ganon regarding classification precision and recall as well as computational performance.

    A refinement can only move $k$-mers from an unrefined node $n$ down to nodes newly entered below $n$. Below a genus, the NCBI taxonomy
    commonly places a bucket of genomes it cannot assign to a species, named \emph{unclassified} after
    that genus, and thus a $k$-mer that a named species shares with one of them has its LCA at the
    \emph{genus} --- even after refinement. The
    databases reported here therefore leave \enquote{unclassified} branches out of both the database and the
    update, which in \texttt{tb} means nine buckets holding \num{349}
    unnamed species and \num{24}\,\% of that database, and in \texttt{nocardia} one holding \num{287}
    and \num{40}\,\%. The exception is \texttt{cv}, where every one of the \num{826} species below such
    buckets carries a name, because in virus taxonomy \emph{unclassified} usually marks a genus that is
    not yet subdivided, so excluding those would delete content, not filler.

    \subsection{Classification quality}\label{classificationquality}

    We present the effect of refinement on read classification quality and start with simulated fastq files whose ground truth is known by construction. Based on the measures from Section \ref{groundtruthfree} we are also able to estimate the precision gain of a refinement for real-world data with missing ground truth.

    Table \ref{classquality} (a) confirms empirically what Section \ref{classmeasures} argued on principle: the boolean counts hardly register the refinement. At the genus rank the $F_1$-score is unchanged to four decimal places for \texttt{cv} under every one of the four read sets. This is not a shortcoming of the refinement but of the boolean measure: a read moved from a genus to a node grouping three of its species has not reached its species, so the species count still scores it as a miss, and it has not left the genus subtree either, so the genus count scored it as a hit all along. Block (a) therefore gives precision and recall for the unrefined variant alone and only the $F_1$ scores for both, since the two variants differ only marginally there, by at most $0.0006$ over the fourteen read sets.

    In contrast, \emph{the candidate-weighted measure $\mathit{prec}^v$ does register the refinement}, as shown in Table \ref{classquality} (b). E.g., precision rises by at least $0.010$ for \texttt{cv}, consistently across all four read sets. These figures are small in absolute terms, and column 4 of Table \ref{genusonly} gives the reason: only between 8.4\,\% and 11.9\,\% of the reads can be improved on at all, because for the remainder the assignment is either at the species already or wrong above the genus. However, when focusing precision on the genus-only reads via $\mathit{prec}^u_g$ and $\mathit{prec}^f_g$ from Section \ref{classmeasures}, the numbers rise considerably, as Table \ref{genusonly} shows. For example, the \enquote{MiSeq} figure changes from $0.098$ to $0.210$ and the \enquote{HiSeq} one from $0.101$ to $0.192$. This means that the refinement halves the number of species left in question for a read that the unrefined database could not resolve.

    Using the precisions $\mathit{prec}^v_g$ and $\widetilde{\mathit{prec}}^v_g$ from Sections \ref{classmeasures} and \ref{groundtruthfree} with $v \in \{u, f\}$, we can compute a ratio between them via the classification result of a simulated read set $s$:
\[ \rho^v_d(s) = \frac{\mathit{prec}^v_g(s)}{\widetilde{\mathit{prec}}^v_g(s)} \]
    $\rho^v_d(s)$ then serves as a correction factor for an estimated precision $\widetilde{\mathit{prec}}^v_g(s')$ which can be provided for a real-world read set $s'$. We obtain a corrected but still estimated precision $\mathit{prec}^{v}_g{}'(s')$, derived from the ungated one via $\mathit{prec}^{v}_g{}'(s') := \rho^v_d(s) \cdot \widetilde{\mathit{prec}}^v_g(s')$. For a sharp estimate we always take the correction from the simulated fastq file $s$ that was derived from the real-world file $s'$ whose true precision we are interested in. E.g., for the real-world fastq file of \enquote{Tick 3} we apply $\rho$ from its simulated counterpart \enquote{Sim. Tick 3} and so on.

    Table \ref{genusonly} puts the two factors at every pair the simulations provide. Across every reported pair there is $\widetilde{\mathit{prec}}^v_g(s) \geq \mathit{prec}^v_g(s)$, and so an ungated precision never falls below the gated one it stands for. The ungated columns of Table \ref{genusonly} tell what an analysis without ground truth would have seen --- e.g., precision gains of $0.113$ and $0.091$ for the two Illumina models, which are the same to three decimals as those measured against the truth.

    On the error-free reads there is $\widetilde{R}^u_g = R^u_g$ by construction: a read without errors carries only $k$-mers of the genome it was drawn from, and path correctness puts every one of them on that genome's own path, so a genus it comes to rest at cannot be the wrong one. Thus, errors are what separates $\widetilde{R}^u_g$ from $R^u_g$.

    The refined database likewise assigns an error-free read $r$ to a node $\kappa^f(r)$ on the path from $\sigma(r)$ to the taxonomic root, since it stores the same $k$-mers and is path correct as well. What separates $\tilde{q}^v(r)$ from $q^v(r)$ is the test whether $\sigma(r)$ lies in $\Sigma(\kappa^v(r))$, and a node on that path has $\sigma(r)$ at or below it. The test holds for every $r$ of the error-free subset, so $q^v(r) = \tilde{q}^v(r)$ throughout, and hence $\rho^u_d(s) = \rho^f_d(s) = 1$.

    \subsubsection{Real-world read data}\label{realreads}

    Table \ref{realdata} lists the three real read sets we classify. The saliva runs are short Illumina reads, the tick runs are long Nanopore reads, and the \emph{Nocardia} runs are short BGISEQ reads. None of them comes with a ground truth, so we use the ungated precision of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} together with the correction factor $\rho^v_d(s)$ of Section \ref{simreads}. Each tick run has a simulated counterpart trained on it and therefore a factor of its own, while the saliva and the \emph{Nocardia} runs share the factor of the one set built for their parameters.

    Table \ref{realgain} reports the classification results for the refined and unrefined cases. The effect is largest on \texttt{nocardia}, whose block holds the fourteen clinical runs of \citep{nocardiamngs}, metagenomic sequencing of patients with confirmed nocardiosis. A read the unrefined database leaves at the genus has all \dbstat{speciesrank}{nocardia} species of \emph{Nocardia} open behind it, so $\widetilde{\mathit{prec}}^u_g$ is the same $0.002$ in every run. Averaged over the \num{28546} reads from column $|\widetilde{R}^u_g|$, the refinement raises that to $0.087$, a factor of \num{36}; per run the factor varies between $1.1$ and $177$.

    The saliva runs of Table \ref{realdata} are classified against \texttt{strepto} and against \texttt{cv}. Saliva is a streptococcal habitat, so the two databases meet on identical material and the comparison is between them rather than between samples. Against \texttt{strepto} the unrefined database leaves up to $16.0$ percent of the classified reads at a genus, and $\widetilde{\mathit{prec}}^v_g$ rises by a factor of three to six. The refinement pays off where a database is dense with close relatives: \texttt{strepto} holds \dbstat{species}{strepto} taxa at the species rank and below in a single genus, and \num[round-mode=places,round-precision=1]{\dbstat{above}{strepto/root}}\,\% of its $k$-mers sit above the data taxa.

    On the same runs the unrefined \texttt{cv} database classifies just under two percent of the reads, and between \num[group-separator={,},group-minimum-digits=4]{\dbstat{rg}{viral/subsetmin}} and \num[group-separator={,},group-minimum-digits=4]{\dbstat{rg}{viral/subsetmax}} of those come to rest at a genus, which is $0.003$ to $0.13$ percent of the classified reads. There $\widetilde{\mathit{prec}}^v_g$ rises from $0.166$ to $0.258$ on the weakest run and from $0.307$ to $0.328$ on the strongest, and $\mathit{prec}^{v}_g{}'$ stays close to it, since the factors in the \enquote{saliva-like} row of Table \ref{genusonly} are close to one. In \texttt{cv} only \num[round-mode=places,round-precision=1]{\dbstat{above}{viral/root}}\,\% of the $k$-mers sit above the data taxa.

    \subsection{Performance}\label{performance}

All experiments reported in Section \ref{results} were performed on a machine with \sysRamGB{} GB of RAM, \sysCores{} CPU cores and a \sysDiskSize{} \sysDiskType{} disk.\footnote{We ensured that as few competing processes as possible were running during the experiments, and OS disk swapping was \sysSwap. Platform details: \sysCpuModel, \sysOs, Java \sysJavaVendor{} \sysJavaVersion{} \sysJavaVm.} Database generation, refinement and read classification alike were run with \sysTotalThreads{} threads and a maximum allowed JVM heap size of 64 GB.


    \subsubsection{Database refinement}\label{refinementperformance}

Since a refined database is always generated \emph{on top of} an existing, unrefined one, the relevant cost of the refinement is its \emph{additional} resource consumption. Table \ref{ftdbperf} therefore reports the wall time and the maximum RAM of the unrefined database generation in absolute terms, but the corresponding figures of the subsequent refinement relative to them.

As can be seen, the refinement costs less than the generation it builds upon for \texttt{cv}, \texttt{protozoa}, \texttt{parasites} and \texttt{tb}, and more for \texttt{strepto} and \texttt{nocardia}. The latter two stay within the same order of magnitude because of the high branching degree and the many $k$-mers that reside at the genus in the unrefined database. This drives up the memory consumption and the entry cost of the goal \verb|kmerindexbloom|, which associates each of those $k$-mers with every species it occurred in. The goal \verb|ftupdatedb| also has more work to do then, since it must access all the entries produced by \verb|kmerindexbloom| and push the $k$-mers down from the genus to the tree nodes added by the refinement.

    Since the refinement must hold the $k$-mer database and the Bloom filter of the goal \verb|kmerindexbloom| at the same time, their byte size sum dominates memory consumption.
Most notably, the refinement is almost for free in terms of disk space: a refined database is hardly larger than the corresponding unrefined one, and that growth comes from the new tree nodes alone.

    \subsubsection{Read classification}\label{classificationperformance}

A refinement is only worthwhile if the resulting database can be used at no extra cost. We therefore classified the simulated fastq files of Section \ref{simreads} once against the unrefined and once against the refined database and measured wall time and maximum RAM of each run in the same way as in Table \ref{ftdbperf}. Each variant was run in its own process, and separately from the quality measurements of Section \ref{classificationquality}, whose per-read scoring would otherwise be counted as classification work.

There is no reason to expect much of a difference and Table \ref{matchperf} confirms it. Classifying a read amounts to looking up its $k$-mers in the store and aggregating the taxa they carry, and the refinement changes neither of the two. Memory follows the same argument, since its consumption is dominated by the $k$-mer database.



The figures also stand against those the first study reports for the same scenarios, measured with ten threads on a machine of the same core count: \citet{genestrip-pub} classified the tick runs at \num{27303} reads per second and the joint run at \num{116642}, where Table \ref{matchperf} reaches \num{39252} and \num{162814}. This is due to several optimizations including an improvement of the $k$-mer database's underlying data structure, where we moved from a sorted array to a so-called radix store. The latter combines several favourable qualities detailed in Appendix \ref{radixstore}.

    \section{Discussion}

    Bracken \citep{lu2017bracken} is sometimes taken to answer the same question, since it moves assignments from higher ranks down to the species rank. It works over a whole sample: for the reads a database left at a genus, it estimates how the species below that genus are distributed and redistributes the reads accordingly, with the distinguishability of those species in the database as its prior. What comes out is a table of proportions for the sample. A refined node states something else, and per read: these species remain possible, and the $k$-mers this read carries support no finer distinction. Neither answer replaces the other, and the two can be used side by side.

\subsection{The radix store and its benefits}\label{radixstore}

The performance figures of Section \ref{performance} rest on a $k$-mer store that has been replaced since \citep{genestrip-pub}. The store described there kept all $k$-mers in one sorted array and answered a lookup by binary search over it. It is now a \emph{radix store} where the entry point of a lookup is a radix index, an array of $2^{r}$ buckets with $r = 17$ by default. A $k$-mer is encoded with two bits per base, and its $r$ low bits are the address of the bucket it belongs to. Only the remaining $2k - r$ bits are kept in the bucket, i.e.\ a sorted array, and searched there. Resolving bits of a key by address arithmetic instead of by comparison is digital searching in the sense of \citet{knuthtaocp3}. Alternatively, the radix store can be seen as a cross between a very flat tree and a sorted array, and it holds several advantages:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item The store is compact by construction. The bits that address a bucket need not be stored in it, so an entry holds $2k - r$ bits of a $k$-mer instead of all $2k$. \emph{This is a reduction of $r/2k$ without any loss of information}, i.e.\ 27\,\% under Genestrip's default setting of $k = 31$ and $r = 17$.

	Each entry is a single 64-bit word. It holds the remaining $2k - r$ bits of the $k$-mer, the index of the taxon the $k$-mer is assigned to and one bit that marks the $k$-mer as already seen, which is how the unique $k$-mer counts of a classification are kept. The implementation from \citep{genestrip-pub} needed a parallel array of \texttt{short}s for the taxon index and a separate bit vector for the marks, and both are now part of the entry. The taxon index has 18 bits at Genestrip's default setting instead of the 16 of a \texttt{short}, so a database may hold \num{262144} distinct taxa rather than \num{65535}, and a wider radix raises that further.
	\item The $r$ leading comparisons of the search are replaced by a single indexed read. A binary search over $n$ $k$-mers costs $\log_2 n$ probes, and resolving $r$ bits by address arithmetic leaves a bucket of $n/2^{r}$ entries and hence $\log_2 n - r$ of them. For a database of a billion $k$-mers that is thirty probes reduced to thirteen.
	\item The index is the one structure every lookup touches. With $2^{r}$ references it takes one megabyte at $r = 17$, which is small enough to stay in the last-level cache. Reading a bucket's address from it therefore costs no access to main memory.
	\item The buckets give write access a natural granularity of locking. Filling a database is a parallel read of the reference sequences. With one array there is one lock, with $2^{r}$ buckets there are $2^{r}$, each guarding only the entries that share a $k$-mer suffix. Two threads inserting $k$-mers that differ in their trailing bases do not contend, so the fill and the lowest common ancestor update scale with the cores available.
	\item A technicality of the implementation language disappears. A Java array is indexed by a signed \texttt{int}, so a single array cannot hold more than $2^{31}$ entries. A bucket holds $n/2^{r}$ entries, so for any database that fits in memory it is an ordinary \texttt{int}-indexed array.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}
	\item The saving of the first point above is smaller for a database as a whole. Both stores carry a Bloom filter of the same size, since the number of entries fixes it and the layout does not touch it.

	\item Without the Bloom filter all $k$-mers of each read must be looked up in the underlying store, and this is when the efficiency of the two store types can actually be compared: the radix store is nearly three times as fast in block (a). The probe count accounts for about two and a half of that. A bisection over the \num[group-separator={,},group-minimum-digits=4]{\dbstat{kmers}{strepto}} $k$-mers of \texttt{strepto} takes 29 probes, one over a bucket of them takes 12. The rest of the improvement is owed to memory access locality. A bucket holds some 34 kilobytes, while the sorted array walks an array of five gigabytes.

	\item With the filter switched on almost nothing of that is left, as block (b) shows. The filter answers most $k$-mers of a real read before any store is consulted. The radix store even pays for the indexed read that precedes its own filter query, which is why it falls slightly behind on the saliva run.

	\item The two blocks also state what the filter is worth. For the sorted array it raises the throughput elevenfold, which confirms the range of nine to eleven reported in \citep{genestrip-pub} already. For the radix store it is fourfold. The better layout needs the filter less.
\end{enumerate}

    Note that the figures from Table \ref{storeperf} stand against each other but not against Table \ref{matchperf}, which measures whole processes with \num{16} threads, loading included.
