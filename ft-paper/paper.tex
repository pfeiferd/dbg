    \section{Background}\label{background}

    \subsection{Introduction}
\label{problem}

$k$-mer based classification tools such as Kraken 1 \citep{kraken}, Kraken 2 \citep{kraken2}, KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq} and Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub} assign reads to taxa by matching their $k$-mers against a database that maps each $k$-mer to a node of a taxonomy tree. Since a $k$-mer may occur in the genomes of several organisms, a $k$-mer database is subjected to the \emph{lowest common ancestor update} (LCA update): originally introduced for metagenomic classification by MEGAN \citep{megan} and now applied by many $k$-mer database tools, it moves a $k$-mer occurring in the genome of more than one taxon up to the lowest node that holds all corresponding taxa as descendants. Later, at classification time, the LCA update prevents $k$-mers in reads from being falsely assigned to an overly low-ranked taxon, since genomes of other taxa such as siblings contain that $k$-mer too. Thus, essentially, the LCA update avoids a potentially false assignment of overly specific taxa to reads.

Unfortunately, the LCA update comes at a price that originates in the ever-growing number of genomes from underlying source databases such as RefSeq: as Nasko et al. \citep{Nasko:2018aa} have shown, it leads to a growing set of species and corresponding genomes under a single genus.
    Each newly added species closely related to an already covered one turns a $k$-mer that used to be specific to a single species into a shared one, and so the LCA update moves it up towards the genus. Consequently, with newer RefSeq versions \emph{more} reads are classified in total, but \emph{fewer} of them are classified on the species level \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. In other words, the resolution of $k$-mer databases deteriorates while the underlying genomic data deepens.
    We observed the same effect in our first study on Genestrip, where databases with a deeper genomic coverage for organisms of interest classified more reads but tended to accumulate them on higher ranks \citep{genestrip-pub}.

Nasko et al. put numbers to this. Bacterial RefSeq has doubled in size every 1.5 years since 2003, and its unique $31$-mers have grown at much the same rate; measured against nine of its versions, the share of reads classified at the species level peaked at version 30 and declined from there on, while genus-level assignments kept rising \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. Nothing has slowed since: the number of prokaryotic genomes has grown by a further factor of 5.8 \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038}, and in the two years to release 236 the number of covered organisms rose by about 20 \% from 152,668 to 182,465 \citep{refseqstats}.

Figure \ref{kmerongenusborrelia} illustrates the effect on the distribution of $k$-mers across ranks for two Genestrip databases, each covering twelve genera. For the bacterial one, a median of about 68 \% of a species' $k$-mers no longer reside at the species rank or below but somewhere between the genus and the species rank, whereas for the protozoan one a median of about 90 \% of them remain at the species rank or below.
    Both databases rest exclusively on RefSeq genomes, and bacteria are covered by far more close relatives than protozoa: release 236 comprises 109,133 bacterial but merely 944 protozoan species \citep{refseqstats}. The LCA update pushes the $k$-mers of a bacterial species up accordingly.

    At first glance, such $k$-mers appear to be lost for fine-grained classification, but Figure \ref{kmerviaranks} shows that it does not need to be that way: for the $k$-mers that ended up on a genus, we determined the number of the genus' children whose genomes actually contain them.
    A median of about 84 \% and 88 \% of them, for the bacterial and the protozoan database respectively, occur in just \emph{two} of the children, and the share drops steeply for higher numbers. So a $k$-mer sitting on a genus is typically not characteristic of the genus as a whole but just of a small group of species under it. The LCA update simply has no better place to put it, because the taxonomy is too coarse-grained. \emph{This is the potential that our approach exploits}: if the taxonomy is \emph{refined} by additional nodes, then the very same $k$-mers can potentially be pushed back down and become more informative again.

    To the best of our knowledge, none of the established $k$-mer based classification tools makes use of this potential. Reads whose $k$-mers would suffice to narrow the candidates down to two or three species are therefore often reported on the genus, even though the information about which species actually share these $k$-mers sits in the underlying genomes.

    \subsection{Related work}
    \label{relatedwork}

One route to improving the database is to correct the classification results afterwards. E.g., Bracken \citep{lu2017bracken} re-estimates species-level abundances from the output of a Kraken-style classification by redistributing the reads assigned to higher ranks according to a background distribution, which describes how the $k$-mers of each species in the database are expected to spread across the taxonomy. The approach differs from a refinement of the database itself: First, Bracken does not add information but reallocates counts on the basis of \emph{probabilistic assumptions} about which species are present and how their $k$-mers distribute. Its estimates degrade when a sample deviates from these assumptions, as for rare or unexpected organisms. Second, it operates on aggregated abundances of an entire sample rather than on individual reads, so that a single read remains classified on the genus even after the re-estimation. A refined database, in contrast, relies on \emph{hard data}: which species actually share a given $k$-mer is known exactly from the underlying genomes, and this knowledge is encoded in the database itself. Each read is then classified more specifically by construction rather than by estimation. 

HTTMM \citep{httmm} estimates an abundance for every node of the taxonomy, for the internal ones as well as for the genomes at the leaves, so that sequence shared among relatives is reported at the clade rather than pushed down to a species. Yet the probability that a node accounts for a read is assumed as under Bracken. However, a genome under a taxon $z$ with $r$ successors is given the fixed probability $1/r$, which is independent of the sequences and reflects nothing but the shape of the taxonomy. The nodes the reads are reported at are those of the given taxonomy. Within a node every genome is held equally likely to have produced a read, although some are far likelier than others --- a limitation the authors name themselves and leave to future work. In contrast, our approach adds new taxonomy nodes, and it replaces the assumed $1/r$ by the $k$-mers themselves. So like Bracken, HTTMM relies on probabilistic assumptions whereas our approach relies on hard data.

MetaPalette \citep{metapalette} is closer to the present work in one respect, in that it operates on
$k$-mers rather than on reads, at $k = 30$ and $50$. It fits the $k$-mer content of a
sample as a linear mixture of reference \enquote{palettes} by nonnegative least squares. It also
addresses a difficulty any such method faces, namely sequence from organisms the reference set does
not contain: reference palettes are degraded to a set similarity, so that a \enquote{hypothetical
organism} near a known one can absorb what the known one does not explain. A refinement has to answer
the same difficulty, and answers it from the genomes instead --- as Section \ref{methods} sets out ---
rather than by modelling what an absent relative might look like.

%... , and any subsequent analysis -- including an abundance estimation -- benefits from it.

    \subsection{Approach}
    \label{approach}
    Our contribution builds on the first study on Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub} by further improving precision of read classifications without sacrificing recall. Genestrip-FT refines the taxonomy tree of a $k$-mer database by grouping those taxa that share many $k$-mers, and it reassigns the affected $k$-mers to the newly added nodes. In doing so, a $k$-mer is only pushed down as far as it may still be attributed to all taxa whose genomes contain it, so that no read is ever classified below the level justified by the underlying genomes.

Figure \ref{ftsketch} captures our approach with regard to a simplistic $k$-mer database
with $k = 2$:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Species $X$, $Y$, $Z$ under a common genus $W$ have respective sequences associated. 
	\item After filling the database, the $2$-mers are assigned to their species. The figure simplifies the state of the database after this step.
	\item The LCA update pushes the $2$-mer \texttt{CT} up to $W$ because it occurs in the sequence of both $X$ and $Y$. However, it does not occur in $Z$. So, according to this database, a read \texttt{CT} would just be classified as $W$ although $Z$ is not even a candidate species for \texttt{CT}.
	\item Genestrip-FT's refinement adds an artificial node $h$ to the taxonomy with $X$ and $Y$ as children but not $Z$. Also, the refinement \emph{pushes down} \texttt{CT} from $W$ to $h$. According to this refined database, a read \texttt{CT} would be classified as $h$ and so $Z$ is excluded as a candidate species for \texttt{CT}.
\end{enumerate}
 So in general, this type of refinement potentially improves classification precision: E.g., if a read was classified under a genus taxon in an unrefined database, then \emph{the corresponding refined database may allow for narrowing down the candidate species to fewer than originally listed under that genus}.

Genestrip structures the complex computation of a $k$-mer database via goals as explained in \cite{genestrip-pub}. The goals form a dependency graph where a goal $g_1$ is executed only if its computational result is needed by a dependent goal $g_2$, and if $g_1$'s result has not been computed yet. Genestrip-FT is implemented as an extension of the original goal graph formed by Genestrip. Figure \ref{ftgoals} presents an extract of the additional goals, with most of Genestrip's original goals omitted. 

  \begin{figure}[!htbp]%
        \centering        
\includegraphics[width=8cm]{figs/ft-goals-extract.pdf}
         \caption{Extract of additional goals required by Genestrip-FT for the refinement of a corresponding $k$-mer database. The file goal \texttt{ftdb}, prefixed with \texttt{f:} stores the refined database as a file and depends on several, so-called object goals prefixed with \texttt{o:}. Genestrip-FT's goals also depend on some of Genestrip's original goals with the latter depicted at the top in dashed boxes.     \end{figure}   

In the following, we briefly explain the essential goals needed for the computation of a refined database:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \verb|kmerindexsize| estimates how many $k$-mer-to-species associations the Bloom filter of the following goal, \verb|kmerindexbloom|, will have to hold, so that the filter can be sized for them. It reads the same sequences but, instead of storing the associations, records a subsample of them in a HyperLogLog sketch \cite{Flajolet07hyperloglog}, whose estimate is accurate to within a fraction of a percent. The alternative is a conservative bound that assumes each $k$-mer on a genus to occur in the genome of every one of its species. The goal \verb|kmerindexsize| is optional --- it costs one additional pass over the sequences --- and may be skipped via a configuration setting. In the latter case, the conservative bound is used which may require considerably more memory due to an oversized Bloom filter.

	\item \verb|kmerindexbloom| associates each $k$-mer stored on the genus rank with the original species taxon of the sequence in which it occurred. To achieve this, the original sequences that were used to generate the unrefined database must all be reread. The mapping of a $k$-mer to its original species taxon is hashed; the resulting hash code is stored via a Bloom filter \cite{bloomfilter} in order to save main memory.\footnote{A Bloom filter is a probabilistic data structure for an efficient and compressed storing of values but also for a fast lookup of entered values. However, this comes at the expense of a configurable, usually low false positive rate at lookup time. Bloom filters and probabilistic filters in general \cite{10.1145/2674005.2674994,9920402,10.1145/3510449,10.1145/3376122} are widely used in the context of metagenomics tools \cite{biobloom, Piro:2020aa, 10.1093/nargab/lqaf094}.}
	
	\item \verb|intersectcount| computes the number of common $k$-mers $c(t_1, t_2)$ between any two species taxa $t_1$ and $t_2$ under the same genus. To do so, it consults the Bloom filter produced via the goal \verb|kmerindexbloom|. Based on $c(t_1, t_2)$, it also determines a Laplace-smoothed Jaccard index
	\[ J(t_1, t_2) = \frac{c(t_1, t_2) + 1}{c(t_1, t_1) + c(t_2, t_2) - c(t_1, t_2) + 1} \]
	\cite{jaccard} as a similarity measure of the two sets of $k$-mers belonging to $t_1$ and $t_2$ on the genus, respectively. The Laplace correction adds $1$ to both the intersection count (numerator) and each self-count (denominator), which avoids division by zero when both species share no $k$-mers on the genus and yields $J = 1$ in that degenerate case. Regarding our running example, Table \ref{intersectioncounts} shows the raw intersection counts whereas Table \ref{jaccard} states the resulting Jaccard indices. For simplicity, we skip the background of the column \enquote{OTHER} for now --- it will be explained at the end of this section.

	\item The goal \verb|dendrogram| from Figure \ref{ftgoals} computes a dendrogram using the standard agglomerative clustering algorithm \enquote{HAC} \cite{King01031967,1394399}, where the cluster distance is based on the Jaccard index $J$ from \verb|intersectcount|. Since the taxonomic refinement is governed by the aim of improving classification precision (see Section \ref{approach}), we chose \enquote{Single Linkage} \cite{King01031967} as the default linkage strategy. This linkage strategy is suitable because it merges clusters on the basis of the most similar $k$-mer sets they contain. Nevertheless, other linkage strategies such as UPGMA and WPGMA \cite{Sokal1958ASM} may be configured instead. Figure \ref{dendrogram} depicts the resulting dendrogram as computed on the basis of Table \ref{jaccard}.

	\item The goal \verb|ftupdatedb| reorganizes the database's taxonomy tree by adding new tree nodes under a genus as induced by the previous goal's dendrogram. Furthermore, $k$-mers are pushed down, i.e. reassigned, to the newly added nodes accordingly. In doing so, each $k$-mer is pushed down only as far as a new node $n$ such that all species with sequences containing that $k$-mer still remain descendants of $n$.

	\item Finally, \verb|ftdb| stores the refined database as a file.
\end{enumerate} 

	Figure \ref{simplerefdb} depicts the refined taxonomy for the Orthopoxvirus example and also states the number of $k$-mers as stored per node in the refined database. As can be seen, about 43 \% of the $k$-mers that originally resided on the genus Orthopoxvirus got pushed down to the newly added nodes, leaving the remaining 57 \% there (see Figure \ref{simplelcadb} for comparison). The new nodes obtain an artificial taxon prefixed with \enquote{000} to distinguish them from regular taxonomic entries, and their artificial rank is set to \enquote{REFINED}.

The Jaccard index over $k$-mer sets is not specific to Genestrip: it underlies a family of alignment-free tools for comparing sequence collections. Mash \cite{Ondov:2016aa} reduces each genome or read set to a MinHash sketch of a few thousand hash values, estimates $J$ from these sketches and converts it into a mutation distance. Skmer \cite{skmer} builds on such sketches for unassembled low-coverage \enquote{genome skims}, correcting the estimate for sequencing depth and error so that a sample can be identified without being assembled. Kmer-db \cite{kmerdb} forgoes sketching altogether and computes $J$ exactly over the complete $k$-mer sets, which it keeps as a compressed sparse binary matrix in order to obtain all pairwise distances among thousands of genomes at once.

What sets \verb|intersectcount| apart is its purpose: The tools above compare genomes or sequencing samples with one another, and the resulting distance is their end product. Here, $c(t_1, t_2)$ is computed over the $k$-mer sets that the database itself assigns to the species taxa under a common genus --- sets that already result from the LCA update and that include the placeholder \enquote{OTHER} discussed above. The index therefore does not serve the computation of an evolutionary distance, but is merely a means for refinement: it feeds the clustering of \verb|dendrogram|, which in turn restructures the taxonomy via \verb|ftupdatedb|.

\subsubsection{Data taxa and path correctness}\label{pathcorrectness}
    Let $t$ be a \emph{data taxon}, i.e., \emph{a taxon with a complete genome directly associated whereby the $k$-mers from that genome are also stored in $d$}. For example in RefSeq, complete genomes are often associated with taxa of the rank species or below. Moreover, let $\nu(a)$ denote the node at which a $k$-mer $a$ is stored in $d$; since each $k$-mer is stored exactly once, $\nu$ is well defined.

For a fair assessment of the measures below, we require that each data taxon be a leaf node in the taxonomy tree of database $d$. This can easily be enforced by a tree transformation explained via (3) and (4) in Figure \ref{fttrans}. Without it, a data taxon would compete against its own descendants.\footnote{Since the data taxa under $n$ include $n$ itself, a $k$-mer occurring in that taxon's genome alone is stored at its node, yet counted against every data taxon below it as well --- and those genomes have no reason to contain it. Its precision would fall short of one although no lower node could hold it, so the measure would penalise a placement that is in fact optimal, and the penalty would grow with the number of data taxa that happen to sit underneath (as in Figure \ref{fttrans} (1) and (2)).} Note that the described transformation leaves the phylogenetic structure of a given taxonomy intact.
Figure \ref{simplerefdbtrans} exemplifies the transformation when integrated into the refinement of the database from Figure \ref{simplelcadb}.\footnote{When carefully comparing the $k$-mer counts of similar nodes from Figures \ref{simplerefdb} and \ref{simplerefdbtrans}, one finds minor differences such as for \enquote{10245/OTHER}. Note that these differences originate in the use of a probabilistic data structure, i.e. a Bloom filter, during the refinement procedure according to Section \ref{dbrefinement}.}

    On this basis, we call a $k$-mer database $d$ \emph{path correct} if
for every $k$-mer $a$ stored in $d$ and every data taxon $t$ whose genome contains $a$, the node $\nu(a)$ is an ancestor of $t$ or $t$ itself. Equivalently, $\nu(a)$ lies on the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root for each such $t$.

    Both unrefined and refined databases are path correct by construction: The LCA update moves a $k$-mer up in the taxonomy just far enough that all data taxa whose genomes contain it become descendants of the resulting node. The refinement, in turn, may push a $k$-mer down only so far that the condition above is still maintained --- this is what keeps \verb|ftupdatedb| (see Section \ref{dbrefinement}) from placing a $k$-mer below the lowest common ancestor of the data taxa supporting it.\footnote{Due to its probabilistic nature, the Bloom filter used during the \texttt{kmerindexbloom} step (see Section~\ref{dbrefinement}) occasionally falsely attributes a species to a $k$-mer, which may prevent the $k$-mer from being pushed down to its lowest possible node. This leaves path correctness intact and only keeps the $k$-mer at a less specific node than necessary.}

\subsubsection{Node precision and subtree precision}\label{nodeprecision}
    Let $D_n$ denote the set of data taxa under a node $n$ including $n$ itself. For a $k$-mer $a$ stored in $d$ with its node $\nu(a)$ from Section \ref{pathcorrectness}, let $c(a)$ be the number of data taxa $t \in D_{\nu(a)}$ whose genomes contain $a$ at least once. We define the \emph{precision of the $k$-mer $a$} as
\[ \mathit{prec}(a) = \frac{c(a)}{|D_{\nu(a)}|} , \]
    i.e.\ as the fraction of the data taxa under $a$'s node whose genomes actually contain $a$. It is defined whenever $D_{\nu(a)} \neq \emptyset$, which excludes only the $k$-mers stored at artificial nodes carrying no data taxon at all.

%    The leaf requirement of Section \ref{pathcorrectness} matters here. Without it a data taxon competes against its own descendants.
%   Cut in the shortening pass (now the footnote in Section \ref{pathcorrectness}), kept to restore easily:
%  $D_n$ comprises the data taxa under $n$ including $n$ itself, so a $k$-mer that occurs in that taxon's genome alone is stored at its node but counted against every data taxon below it as well, and those genomes have no reason to contain it. Its precision would fall short of one even though no lower node could hold it. The measure would then penalise an optimal placement, and the penalty would grow with the number of data taxa that happen to sit underneath (Figure \ref{fttrans} (1) and (2)).
    
    Due to path correctness, every data taxon whose genome contains $a$ is subordinate to $\nu(a)$, i.e.\ the recall of every stored $k$-mer is one, and so the count $c(a)$ does not depend on where $a$ is stored: it is simply the number of data taxa in $d$ that support $a$. Only $|D_{\nu(a)}|$ varies with the placement, and it is smallest when $\nu(a)$ is the lowest common ancestor of the data taxa supporting $a$.
%   Cut in the shortening pass (said more precisely at the end of this section), kept to restore easily:
%   Note that raising $\mathit{prec}(a)$ is the very purpose of the refinement as it introduces new nodes and moves $k$-mers onto them.

    Averaging $\mathit{prec}$ over the $k$-mers stored at a single node $n$ yields \emph{node precision} $\mathit{nprec}(n)$. With $A_n$ denoting the set of $k$-mers associated with that node:
\[ \mathit{nprec}(n) = \frac{1}{|A_n|} \sum_{a \in A_n} \mathit{prec}(a). \]
    Averaging $\mathit{prec}$ over the $k$-mers of an entire subtree yields \emph{subtree precision} $\mathit{sprec}(m)$. Let $m$ be a node of $d$'s taxonomy tree and let
$\mathcal{A}_m = \bigcup_{n} A_n$ be the set of $k$-mers stored at the nodes $n$ of the subtree rooted at $m$ --- including $m$ itself --- with $D_n \neq \emptyset$.
    Then
\[ \mathit{sprec}(m) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_m|} \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}_m} \mathit{prec}(a) . \]
    For $m$ being the taxonomic root, $\mathcal{A}_m$ comprises all $k$-mers of $d$ that reside at a node with data taxa underneath, and $\mathit{sprec}(m)$ hence assesses the database as a whole; for $m$ being a genus, it assesses the refinement of that genus in isolation.
    The two averages are undefined when the set of $k$-mers they range over is empty, i.e.\ $\mathit{nprec}(n)$ for a node without $k$-mers and $\mathit{sprec}(m)$ for a subtree without any.
%   Cut in the shortening pass (the per-$k$-mer form follows in the next paragraph), kept to restore easily:
%   Moreover, given the transformation from Figure \ref{fttrans}, it follows by definition that $\mathit{nprec}(n) = 1$ for a node $n$ of a data taxon $t$ that stores $k$-mers at all.

    After the transformation of Section \ref{pathcorrectness}, a $k$-mer $a$ stored at a data taxon has $|D_{\nu(a)}| = 1$ and therefore $\mathit{prec}(a) = 1$ by construction. Such $k$-mers are the bulk of a database, as will be shown, so they dominate $\mathit{sprec}$ although they cannot be improved. We therefore report the \emph{restricted subtree precision} $\mathit{sprec}^{*}(m)$ alongside it. With $\mathcal{A}^{*}_m \subseteq \mathcal{A}_m$ denoting the $k$-mers of $\mathcal{A}_m$ that are \emph{not} stored at a data taxon --- the ones a refinement can still move ---
\[ \mathit{sprec}^{*}(m) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}^{*}_m|} \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}^{*}_m} \mathit{prec}(a) . \]

    In both averages, every stored $k$-mer contributes equally, regardless of how the taxonomy happens to distribute the $k$-mers over nodes. Moreover, inserting an inner node into the taxonomy changes neither $\nu(a)$ nor $|D_{\nu(a)}|$ nor $c(a)$ for any $k$-mer already stored, so refining the tree alone cannot improve $\mathit{nprec}$ or $\mathit{sprec}$. Both rise only when the added nodes cover a $k$-mer's supporting data taxa more tightly \emph{and} the $k$-mer is moved onto such a node.
    Note also that $\mathit{nprec}(n)$ may deteriorate if data taxa's genomes under $n$ are \emph{incomplete}, because in this case a $k$-mer $a$ at node $n$ might simply not be supported by such taxa, even though their completed genome would contain $a$.

    \subsubsection{Measuring classification quality}\label{classmeasures}

    Let $r$ be a read, let $\sigma(r)$ be the species taxon its underlying genome belongs to and let $\kappa(r)$ be the taxon the analysis assigns to it, which is undefined if $r$ remains unclassified. Reads whose $\sigma(r)$ cannot be determined are excluded from all counts below, as they can be neither right nor wrong.

    The customary way to score such an assignment is a \emph{boolean positive count}: $r$ counts as a hit if $\kappa(r)$ is equal to or below $\sigma(r)$, and as a miss otherwise, and likewise one rank higher with the genus in place of the species. Both variants, however, are blind to what a refinement does. A refinement introduces nodes \emph{between} a genus and its species and moves $k$-mers onto them, so a read that used to be classified to its genus now tends to end up at one of these new nodes. That is a substantial gain --- the read is narrowed down from all species of the genus to a handful --- yet the boolean count at the genus rank scores it as a hit in both cases and the one at the species rank as a miss in both cases. Neither of them registers the improvement at all.

    We therefore refine the count in proportion to how far the assignment narrows the species down. For a node $n$ of $d$'s taxonomy tree let
\[ \Sigma(n) = \{ s : s \text{ is a species taxon of } d \text{ and } s \text{ lies on a common path to the root with } n \} \]
    be the set of \emph{species in question} at $n$, i.e.\ the species that are still consistent with an assignment to $n$: those below $n$ and, if there are none because $n$ itself lies below the species rank, the one above it. The \emph{ratio-based positive count} of a read then is
\[ q(r) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{|\Sigma(\kappa(r))|} & \text{if } \sigma(r) \in \Sigma(\kappa(r)), \\[2ex] 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \]
    and $q(r) = 0$ for an unclassified read. So an assignment that pins the species down scores $1$, an assignment to a genus comprising $40$ species scores $1/40$, and one to a refined node covering $3$ of them scores $1/3$. In particular $q(r)$ agrees with the boolean count at the species rank whenever the species is actually determined, and it grades the cases in between that the boolean counts collapse.

    $\Sigma(n)$ is counted in two specific ways. First, the species are counted over the \emph{entire} subtree rooted at $n$, not among its children. A taxonomy may place intermediate nodes such as a subgenus between a genus and its species, and counting children would let such a node hide the species underneath it. Second, the counting happens in the taxonomy of the database at hand. The refinement adds nodes but neither adds nor removes species taxa, so $\Sigma(n)$ is the same in the refined and the unrefined database for every node $n$ that exists in both. What differs is $\kappa(r)$, the node a read is assigned to, and that is the effect we want to measure.

    From these counts we obtain precision, recall and the $F_1$-score in the usual way: with $R$ denoting the reads of a fastq file whose species is known and $R_c \subseteq R$ those that were classified at all,
\[ \mathit{prec} = \frac{\sum_{r \in R_c} q(r)}{|R_c|}, \qquad \mathit{rec} = \frac{\sum_{r \in R} q(r)}{|R|}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \mathit{prec} \cdot \mathit{rec}}{\mathit{prec} + \mathit{rec}} , \]
    and analogously for the boolean counts, which we report alongside for comparison with the literature.

    One further effect must be accounted for before these averages can be read as a statement about the refinement. They range over \emph{all} reads, yet the vast majority of them is already assigned to a single species by the unrefined database, and such a read scores $q(r) = 1$ before and after a refinement. It cannot improve, but it does enter the average and thereby dilutes whatever happens to the reads that can. A read can only profit from the refinement if the unrefined database classified it correctly down to its genus but no further --- correct at that rank, yet not specific enough to name the species.

    We therefore report the same measure a second time, restricted to exactly these reads. Let $\kappa_u$ and $\kappa_f$ denote the assignment of the unrefined and the refined database and let $q_u$ and $q_f$ be the corresponding counts. With
\[ R_g = \{ r \in R : \text{lca}(\sigma(r), \kappa_u(r)) \text{ lies at or below } \text{genus}(\sigma(r)) \text{ and above } \sigma(r) \} , \]
    where $\text{lca}$ denotes the lowest ranked common ancestor of two nodes and $\text{genus}(\sigma(r))$ the genus the read's own species belongs to, as the \emph{genus-only subset}, the \emph{restricted precision} of a variant $v \in \{u, f\}$ is
\[ \mathit{prec}_g(v) = \frac{1}{|R_g|} \sum_{r \in R_g} q_v(r) . \]
    The condition is a window of ranks rather than the genus alone, because a taxonomy places further ranks inside it: NCBI's \emph{subgenus} and \emph{species group} both lie between a genus and its species, and a read left at either is left with several species in question exactly as one left at the genus is. Restricting the subset to the genus proper would drop those reads although a refinement acts on them --- for \texttt{tb}, whose six species-group nodes carry the Rickettsia spotted fever, typhus, canis, belli and phagocytophilum groups, that is 13 \% of the improvable reads. Nodes carrying no rank of their own are excluded, which leaves a residue the measure does not see; determining the window by position in the tree rather than by rank would close it.

    Note that $R_g$ is determined by the unrefined run and then kept fixed, so that both variants are scored on the very same reads and the difference $\mathit{prec}_g(f) - \mathit{prec}_g(u)$ states the gain where a gain was possible at all. Note also that the denominator is $|R_g|$ rather than the number of classified reads among them, which makes no difference here: every read of $R_g$ was classified by construction, since an unclassified read has no common ancestor with its true taxon to begin with.

    \subsubsection{Estimating the gain without ground truth}\label{groundtruthfree}

    Both measures of Section \ref{classmeasures} rest on $\sigma(r)$, the species a read actually stems from. A simulation supplies it, a real sample does not. Of a real fastq file we can therefore still ask how far a classification narrows the species down --- only not whether it does so correctly. Dropping precisely that test from $q$ leaves
\[ q'(r) = \frac{1}{|\Sigma(\kappa(r))|} \]
    for a classified read, and $q'(r) = 0$ for an unclassified one. Since $q(r)$ is $q'(r)$ multiplied by the indicator of $\sigma(r) \in \Sigma(\kappa(r))$, we have $q(r) \le q'(r)$ read by read, and hence for every average over reads as well. This needs no distributional assumption: $q'$ bounds $q$ pointwise.

    Dropping the test from $q$ is only half of what a real sample forces, however. The subset $R_g$ is no more observable than $q$ is: it is defined through $\sigma(r)$, and asking whether the unrefined assignment sits below \emph{the read's own} genus presupposes the very answer the analysis is after. What can be observed is where the classification came to rest, and nothing else. The ungated measure is therefore taken over
\[ R'_g = \{ r \in R : \kappa_u(r) \text{ lies at or below a genus and above any species} \} , \]
    the reads the unrefined database placed no further than a genus --- right or wrong --- which is decided by $\kappa_u(r)$ alone. Writing
\[ \mathit{prec}'_g(v) = \frac{1}{|R'_g|} \sum_{r \in R'_g} q'_v(r) \]
    gives what we call the \emph{ungated precision}, against $\mathit{prec}_g$ of Section \ref{classmeasures} for the gated one. Neither its increase under a refinement nor that of $\mathit{prec}_g$ is given a name or a symbol: both are read off the four averages the tables report, and naming them would add notation without adding a quantity. A prime marks the ground-truth-free counterpart throughout, and the primes travel together: $\mathit{prec}'_g$ differs from $\mathit{prec}_g$ in the summand \emph{and} in the subset, because it is exactly those two substitutions together that remove every appeal to $\sigma(r)$ and leave a quantity a real fastq file can supply. A primed symbol is thus never simply its unprimed one relaxed --- $R'_g$ in particular is neither a subset nor a superset of $R_g$, as the next paragraph shows.

    The two subsets are close, but neither contains the other, and they part company in two opposite ways. $R'_g$ \emph{loses} the reads whose unrefined assignment named a \emph{sibling} species: their lowest common ancestor with $\sigma(r)$ is the genus, which puts them in $R_g$, while $\kappa_u(r)$ itself sits at a species, which keeps them out of $R'_g$. These are precisely the reads of $R_g$ that score zero under $q$. $R'_g$ \emph{gains} the reads stopped at a genus that is not theirs at all, which no ground truth vouches for and which $R_g$ therefore excludes. On the simulated data below --- drawn from the very genomes the database was built from --- the first effect dominates and the second is negligible: $R'_g$ falls short of $R_g$ by between $0$ and $8.6$ percent, of which the reads it gains account for at most $0.4$. On a real sample, which contains organisms no reference covers, there is no reason to expect the second effect to stay negligible, and that is the substitution's price.

    The increase in $\mathit{prec}'_g$ is not a bound on the increase in $\mathit{prec}_g$ in either direction. The two are increases of averages taken over different summands and different subsets, so the pointwise $q \le q'$ says nothing about how they compare, which is why the calibration below is a pair of factors on the two levels rather than one factor on their difference.

    $\mathit{prec}'_g$ registers how much more specific the refined answers are, not whether that specificity is warranted. A refinement that pushed every read onto an arbitrary single species would maximise it and destroy precision at the same time. It is informative here only because the refinement is conservative by construction: a $k$-mer moves only onto a node whose subtree still contains every species whose genomes carry it (Section \ref{dbrefinement}). For a read whose matching $k$-mers all stem from its own genome, $q_f(r) = q'_f(r)$. The two differ only where read errors produce matches that genome does not account for, and those reads can be counted. They are the reads of $R_g$ that score zero under $q$: for \texttt{cv}, 0.00 \%, 0.50 \% and 2.67 \% of it on the error-free, \enquote{HiSeq} and \enquote{MiSeq} sets, absent without sequencing errors and growing with them. They are also exactly the reads $R'_g$ omits; on every other read $q$ and $q'$ agree.

    The ratio of the two is what turns one into an estimate of the other. Writing
\[ \rho^u_{d,s} = \frac{\mathit{prec}_g(u)}{\mathit{prec}'_g(u)} , \qquad \rho^f_{d,s} = \frac{\mathit{prec}_g(f)}{\mathit{prec}'_g(f)} \]
    for the factors that turn an ungated precision into the gated one it stands for, both measured on the database $d$ and the simulated read set $s$. A real fastq file classified against the same database then yields the estimates $\rho^u_{d,s}\,\mathit{prec}'_g(u)$ and $\rho^f_{d,s}\,\mathit{prec}'_g(f)$ of a precision it cannot measure, and their difference is the estimated precision gain. That is what the whole construction is for.

    \section{Results}\label{results}

    Every measurement below compares a database against \emph{itself}: the same reads are classified twice, or the same database assessed twice, once before the refinement and once after it, with everything else held fixed. That is a deliberate choice of control, and since it means no other tool appears beside Genestrip anywhere in this section, it is worth saying why at the outset. A comparison across tools would vary the database and the classification algorithm along with the refinement, so a difference could no longer be attributed to the refinement alone --- whereas the comparison made here isolates it by construction. Nor is there a refined counterpart elsewhere to compare against: no other system performs a refinement, for reasons that differ from tool to tool and that Section \ref{relatedwork} sets out. Finally, the baseline is not an arbitrary one --- the first study on Genestrip established that it and KrakenUniq return identical $k$-mer and read counts on the same database \citep{genestrip-pub}, so the unrefined database is an externally corroborated reference point and what is measured against it carries over.

    \paragraph{What the taxonomy contributes to $\mathit{sprec}^{*}$.} A refinement can only move $k$-mers between
    nodes that exist, so what a database can reach depends on the tree it is given --- and the tree
    carries more than named taxa. Below a genus the NCBI taxonomy commonly places a bucket of genomes
    it cannot assign to a species, named \emph{unclassified} after that genus, and those genomes are
    not passive. Under the least common ancestor update a $k$-mer that a named species shares with one
    of them has its ancestor at the \emph{genus}, since the bucket hangs off the genus as a sibling of
    the whole lineage the species belongs to. The species loses the $k$-mer, and it loses it to a
    genome that carries no name to be told apart from.

    How much sits in such buckets varies widely across the databases of Table \ref{dbdesc}. In
    \texttt{tb} nine of them hold \num{349} species and \num{127243373} $k$-mers, \num{24}\,\% of
    that database, one under each of the genera the study is about --- \emph{Bartonella} alone
    contributes \num{225} unnamed species. In \texttt{protozoa} four hold \num{49297699} (\num{4}\,\%),
    in \texttt{parasites} one holds \num{9295}, and in \texttt{vineyard} none sits below a genus at
    all. Two kinds of node that look similar must be kept out of this count, and the criterion is what
    they contain rather than what they are called: an \emph{incertae sedis} node holds \emph{named}
    species whose placement is unsettled --- \emph{Trypanosoma theileri}, \emph{Plasmodium simium},
    and in \texttt{vineyard} the study organism \emph{Trichothecium roseum} --- and in \texttt{cv}
    every one of the \num{826} species below such buckets carries a name, because in virus taxonomy
    \emph{unclassified} usually marks a genus that is not yet subdivided. Excluding those would delete
    content, not filler.

    \subsection{Classification quality}\label{classificationquality}

    The measures of Section \ref{intrinsicqms} assess a database as such. We now turn to what the refinement means for the analysis of actual reads, using simulated fastq files whose ground truth is known by construction. The measures themselves are those of Section \ref{classmeasures}, together with the estimate of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} that a sample \emph{without} known ground truth still admits; Sections \ref{simreads} and \ref{realreads} describe the simulated data and the real sequencing runs they are applied to.

%

    \subsubsection{Simulated read data}\label{simreads}

    Following the first study on Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub}, the simulated reads are produced with InSilicoSeq \citep{10.1093/bioinformatics/bty630}. Its two Illumina error models \enquote{MiSeq} and \enquote{HiSeq} are applied to \emph{all} RefSeq genomes of the category that the database under consideration draws from, yielding one pair of paired-end fastq files with one million reads per model. The models differ in read length as well as in error profile: InSilicoSeq emits 301 base pairs per mate for \enquote{MiSeq} and 126 for \enquote{HiSeq}.

    The ground truth follows from how InSilicoSeq names its reads: each read carries the accession of the fasta record it was drawn from, which Genestrip's accession map resolves to a taxon. A read whose accession cannot be resolved is reported as \emph{unresolved} and excluded from every count, in line with Section \ref{classmeasures}; a number substantially above zero would point at a mismatch between the reads and the accession map rather than at a property of the database, which is why we report it in Table \ref{simdata}.

    Since the reads are drawn from an entire RefSeq category, they need not fall within what a database was built to cover. We therefore count a read towards $R$ only if its true taxon lies at or below a taxon that the database explicitly requests. A read outside that scope can still be classified into it, and it then counts as a false positive, i.e.\ towards the classified reads but not towards $R$. Consequently the number of classified reads may exceed $|R|$, as it does for \texttt{tb} in Table \ref{simdata}. Where a database requests an entire category, as \texttt{cv} does, every read is in scope anyway and the restriction changes nothing.

    Besides \texttt{cv} we use \texttt{tb} from Table \ref{dbdesc} as the second database here. The two complement each other in the respect the refinement is about: \texttt{cv} is large and taxonomically broad, whereas \texttt{tb} is dense with close relatives --- twelve bacterial genera holding 1,120 taxa at the species rank and below --- which is the regime in which the LCA update strands the most $k$-mers above the species and a refinement therefore has the most to recover.

    Table \ref{genusonly} puts the two calibration factors of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} at every pair the simulations provide, and both are close to one throughout. On \texttt{cv} they run from $0.9729$ to $1.0000$ and from $0.9756$ to $1.0000$; on the error-free reads both are exactly one, because there $R'_g$ is $R_g$ and the two blocks coincide read for read. Two regularities hold across every pair reported here: neither factor exceeds one, and $\rho^f_{d,s}$ is the larger of the two. The first says an ungated precision never understates its gated counterpart, the second that the gap is narrower after a refinement than before it --- which is the sibling reads of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} being brought back into question, visible now as a systematic difference between two columns rather than as a single ratio crossing one.

    Neither regularity is automatic, and the first at least has an exact criterion. Write $Z$ for the reads $R'_g$ omits --- the sibling-named ones, which score zero under $q$ --- and $W$ for those it admits instead, placed at a genus that is not the read's own; the two subsets thus pull in opposite directions, $Z$ raising the ungated average by removing zeros and $W$ lowering it by adding near-zeros. With $A = \sum_{r \in R_g \cap R'_g} q_u(r)$ and $B = \sum_{r \in W} q'_u(r)$, a line of algebra gives
\[ \mathit{prec}_g(u) \le \mathit{prec}'_g(u) \iff \big(|W| - |Z|\big)\, A \le |R_g|\, B , \]
    and since $B \ge 0$ the condition $|W| \le |Z|$ is sufficient on its own. It is comfortably met here: $|Z|$ exceeds $|W|$ by between 15 and 53 times on every read set. That is not luck. Reads drawn from the reference genomes themselves are seldom assigned to a foreign genus, so $W$ stays in the tens, whereas sibling-named reads are exactly what sequencing error produces and $Z$ runs to thousands. A real sample carries no such guarantee --- its off-target content is bounded by nothing --- so the ordering should be read as a property of these simulations rather than as a law.

    On \texttt{tb} the eight tick simulations give $\rho^u_{\texttt{tb},s}$ between $0.9057$ and $0.9660$ and $\rho^f_{\texttt{tb},s}$ between $0.9258$ and $0.9703$, across $s = \text{tick}_1 \dots \text{tick}_8$, means $0.9232$ and $0.9443$. The departure from one is larger than on \texttt{cv}, and it comes from where one would expect: at a per-base error of $11.25$ \% the sibling-named reads that separate $R'_g$ from $R_g$ reach $3.3$ to $8.6$ percent of the subset, against $0$ to $2.6$ percent for the Illumina sets, so the two averages are taken over populations that agree less well. Even so the eight samples agree to within six percent among themselves --- a spread narrow enough for one pair of figures per database to be worth quoting, and wide enough that quoting one pair for \emph{all} databases would not be.

    Across all twelve pairs the factors lie between $0.9057$ and $1.0000$, and that is the result this section is after. Uncalibrated, an ungated precision overstates its gated counterpart by at most nine percent, and by under three percent on every \texttt{cv} set; multiplied by its factor it reproduces it exactly. Either way the construction of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} does what it was built for: a precision that can be \emph{measured} only where the truth is known can be \emph{estimated}, to two decimal places, where it is not --- which is the case for every real sample in Section \ref{realreads}.



    Table \ref{classquality} confirms empirically what Section \ref{classmeasures} argued on principle: the boolean counts hardly register the refinement. At the genus rank the $F_1$-score is unchanged to ten decimal places for \texttt{cv} under every one of the three read sets; at the species rank the largest change is $0.0001$. This is not a shortcoming of the refinement but of the measure --- a read moved from a genus to a node grouping three of its species has not reached its species, so the species count still scores it as a miss, and it has not left the genus subtree either, so the genus count scored it as a hit all along.

    The candidate-weighted measure, in contrast, does register it: precision rises by between $0.0080$ and $0.0097$ for \texttt{cv}, consistently across all three read sets. These figures are small in absolute terms, and Table \ref{genusonly} shows why. Only between 7.4\,\% and 10.3\,\% of the reads can be improved on at all --- for the remainder the assignment is either already at the species or wrong above the genus, and in neither case can a refinement change anything. Restricted to those reads, the same measure roughly \emph{doubles}: from $0.104$ to $0.208$ under \enquote{MiSeq} and from $0.106$ to $0.200$ under \enquote{HiSeq}, a gain of $0.104$ and $0.094$. In other words, the refinement halves the number of species left in question for a read that the unrefined database could not resolve.

    The ungated columns of the same table say what an analysis without ground truth would have seen: gains of $0.105$ and $0.094$ for the two Illumina models, against $0.106$ and $0.094$ measured against the truth. The estimate of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} would thus have been right to the third decimal place without knowing $\sigma(r)$ for a single read. How far that carries is read off the error-free row, where $R'_g = R_g$ and both factors are exactly one: over the range these three sets span, the estimate is as good as the measurement it stands in for.

    A small part of the gain even reaches the species rank: of the genus-only reads $R_g$, the refined database pins $0.15$\,\% down to a single species under \enquote{MiSeq}, where the unrefined one pins none by construction. The bulk of the effect, however, is the narrowing that the boolean counts cannot see.

    The subset is larger under \enquote{HiSeq} than under \enquote{MiSeq} --- $10.3$\,\% against $7.4$\,\%. The two models differ in read length and in error profile at once, so this contrast alone cannot attribute the effect to either: a shorter read carries fewer $k$-mers and a more error-prone one carries fewer \emph{matching} $k$-mers, and both make a classification come to rest higher up in the taxonomy. The error-free runs separate the two, since InSilicoSeq's \enquote{perfect} mode keeps the read length and introduces no errors at all; they also bound what the refinement can achieve when nothing but the taxonomy limits the classification.

    The measure of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} exists so that a refinement can be assessed on data that no simulation produced. All experiments reported in Section \ref{results} were performed on a machine with \sysRamGB{} GB of RAM, \sysCores{} CPU cores and a \sysDiskSize{} \sysDiskType{} disk.\footnote{We ensured that as few competing processes as possible were running during the experiments, and OS disk swapping was \sysSwap. \subsubsection{The radix $k$-mer store}\label{radixstore}

Both figures below rest on a $k$-mer store that has been replaced since \citep{genestrip-pub}, and the change is worth stating because it affects every number in this section. The store described there kept all $k$-mers in one sorted array and answered a lookup by binary search over it. It is now a \emph{radix store}: the low $r$ bits of a $k$-mer --- the encoding of its trailing bases, $r = 17$ by default --- select one of $2^{r}$ buckets, and only the remaining bits are searched within that bucket. Five things follow, of which the first two govern lookup and the rest the construction and the size of a database.

First, the $r$ leading comparisons of the search are replaced by a single indexed read. A binary search over $n$ $k$-mers costs $\log_2 n$ probes; resolving $r$ bits by address arithmetic leaves a bucket of $n/2^{r}$ entries and hence $\log_2 n - r$ of them. For a database of a billion $k$-mers that is thirty probes reduced to thirteen. Second, and more important than the count, those probes are cheaper. The probes a global binary search makes are spread over the whole array and each of the first ones is a cache miss on a different page, whereas a bucket of a few dozen entries occupies one or two cache lines; the radix table itself is $2^{r}$ references, one megabyte at $r = 17$, so it stays resident in the last-level cache and the indexed read that starts every lookup is not a memory stall. A bucket that is empty --- no $k$-mer of the database ends in those bases --- is a \texttt{null} that ends the lookup before the probabilistic pre-filter is even consulted.

Third, the buckets give write access a natural granularity of locking. Filling a database is a parallel read of the reference sequences, and with one array there is one lock; with $2^{r}$ buckets there are $2^{r}$, each guarding the few dozen entries that share a $k$-mer suffix. Two threads inserting $k$-mers that differ in their trailing bases --- which is to say almost any two --- do not contend at all, so the fill and the lowest-common-ancestor update scale with the cores available rather than serialising on the store.

Fourth, the store is more compact, and by construction rather than by compression. The $r$ bits that selected the bucket need not be stored in it: they are the bucket's address. Each entry is a single 64-bit word holding the remaining $2k - r$ bits of the $k$-mer, and the bits thus freed carry the index of the taxon the $k$-mer is assigned to and one bit marking a $k$-mer as already counted while a sample is classified. A separate array of taxon indices, which the previous store required, is therefore not needed at all, and a wider radix directly raises the number of distinct taxa a database can hold.

Fifth, a technicality of the implementation language disappears. A Java array is indexed by a signed \texttt{int}, so a single array cannot hold more than $2^{31}$ entries and a store of more $k$-mers than that had to be built from the segmented \enquote{big array} constructions of the collections library, at a cost on every access. A bucket holds $n/2^{r}$ entries, so for any database that fits in memory at all it is an ordinary \texttt{int}-indexed array, and the segmentation is gone along with the indirection it cost.

    \subsubsection{Database refinement}\label{refinementperformance}

Since a refined database is always generated \emph{on top of} an existing, unrefined one, the relevant cost of the refinement is its \emph{additional} resource consumption. Table \ref{ftdbperf} therefore reports the wall time and the maximum RAM of the unrefined database generation in absolute terms, but the corresponding figures of the subsequent refinement relative to them.

As can be seen, the refinement typically costs less than the generation of the unrefined database it builds upon: for \texttt{protozoa} and \texttt{parasites} it stays below two thirds of the wall time, and for \texttt{tb} and \texttt{vineyard} below 85 \%. The database \texttt{cv} forms an exception with 174 \%, i.e. the refinement takes about three quarters longer than the original database generation. This is because the refinement's runtime is essentially driven by the number of $k$-mers residing on refinement ranks and by the number of subtrees each of them must be tested against, whereas the generation time of the unrefined database is dominated by reading and hashing the underlying genomic sequences. With 14,988 species and below (see Table \ref{dbdesc}), \texttt{cv} is by far the most species-rich database considered here, so that testing a single $k$-mer becomes correspondingly expensive.
Regarding main memory, the ratio does not follow the size of a database, and it cannot, since its two halves are driven by different quantities. The generation of an unrefined database is dominated by the genomic sequences it reads, whereas the refinement holds the $k$-mer store and the Bloom filter of the goal \verb|kmerindexbloom| at the same time, the latter growing with the number of $k$-mers on refinement ranks and the number of taxa they must be attributed to --- the same product that governs the refinement's runtime. The refinement accordingly stays below the generation wherever voluminous genomes were read for comparatively few species: \texttt{parasites} with 81 \% and \texttt{protozoa} with 95 \%, the latter two holding above a billion $k$-mers for fewer than 110 species each. It exceeds the generation where that peak was modest by comparison: \texttt{vineyard} with 106 \%, the smallest database in our collection, \texttt{tb} with 123 \%, whose refinement has the largest memory footprint of all at 1,120 species, and \texttt{cv} with 134 \%, whose viral genomes make for a particularly lean generation while its \dbstat{species}{viral} species make for a particularly large mapping.
Most notably, the refinement is almost for free in terms of disk space: a refined database is at most 2.6 \% larger than the corresponding unrefined one. This is because the refinement does not add any $k$-mers to the database --- it only reassigns them and adds a comparatively small number of new tree nodes.

    \subsubsection{Clinical background}
    Bacterial lower respiratory infection is diagnosed by culture. Culture is insensitive, and it is
    \enquote{too slow to guide early, targeted antimicrobial therapy} \citep{lrinanopore}, so the first days of treatment
    are empirical and broad-spectrum. Nanopore metagenomics shortens that wait: \citet{lrinanopore} report a result six
    hours after the sample arrives. It is also a setting where a $k$-mer classifier has to name an organism straight from a
    clinical specimen.

    In the respiratory tract, naming the genus is rarely enough. \emph{S.~pneumoniae} is a leading cause of
    community-acquired pneumonia and is treated as one. \emph{S.~oralis}, \emph{S.~mitis}, \emph{S.~infantis} and
    \emph{S.~pseudopneumoniae} are oral commensals; healthy people carry them, and saliva brings them into any respiratory
    specimen. An answer of \enquote{Streptococcus} therefore says nothing clinically, because it fits the pathogen and the
    flora equally well. The distinction that matters is between species, and these species have genomes close enough to make
    it hard. \citet{lrinanopore} were $96.6$\,\% sensitive but only $41.7$\,\% specific against culture, and reached full
    specificity only after adding \emph{pathobiont-specific gene analyses} --- a second, organism-specific assay run beside
    the metagenomic classification.

    The reference taxonomy is what creates the difficulty. NCBI places \emph{S.~pneumoniae}, \emph{S.~pseudopneumoniae},
    \emph{S.~oralis}, \emph{S.~infantis}, \emph{S.~sanguinis}, \emph{S.~cristatus} and \emph{S.~gordonii} as direct
    children of the genus. Of the biological mitis group only \emph{S.~mitis} and \emph{S.~parasanguinis} sit under the
    node named after it, and \emph{S.~parasanguinis} through a nested \emph{Streptococcus parasanguinis group} of its own.
    A $k$-mer that these species share has no common ancestor below \emph{Streptococcus}, so the LCA update leaves it
    there. The genomes do offer something finer. The tree has nowhere to put it.

    \subsubsection{Application of refined $k$-mer databases}
    The database \texttt{strepto} of Table \ref{dbdesc} holds the genus with at most fifty genomes per species. Above the
    data taxa its genus subtree stores \num{250398829} $k$-mers, and before any clustering runs those sit in exactly three
    kinds of place: $47.99$\,\% at a species node or below it, $2.37$\,\% at one of the five \emph{species group} nodes
    NCBI supplies, and $49.64$\,\% at the genus itself. There is nothing in between. The five species groups hold
    \num{5924657} $k$-mers together against \num{124302068} at the genus, one part in twenty of the material whose
    placement is at issue.

    \todo{The branching-degree figures that stood here came from \texttt{streptococcus\_branchhistocsv.csv} of
    2026-08-22, whose genus row totals \num{222207845} $k$-mers against the \num{124302068} of the current build. Rerun
    the histogram and restate them, including how many of the degree-two $k$-mers the clustering actually places. The old
    figures cannot be renumbered: they describe a database that still held the unnamed branches and was built under the
    contig cap.}

    The refinement moves that material a long way down. The share of it at a node leaving more than a hundred species open
    falls from $49.64$\,\% to $35.80$\,\%, and the share at a node naming ten species or fewer rises from $50.36$\,\% to
    $60.35$\,\% --- \num{25014786} $k$-mers newly placed there, against the \num{5924657} of NCBI's own \emph{species
    group} nodes, a factor of $4.2$. It also builds a node the taxonomy does not. One refined node holds the five species
    of NCBI's own \emph{Streptococcus mitis group} --- \emph{S.~mitis}, \emph{S.~gwangjuensis}, \emph{S.~humanilactis},
    \emph{S.~chosunensis} and \emph{S.~toyakuensis} --- together with \emph{S.~pneumoniae}, \emph{S.~oralis} and
    \emph{S.~pseudopneumoniae}, which that group leaves outside and which hang off the genus instead. Those three are the
    clinically decisive ones, the organisms a pneumococcus is confused with. Before the refinement a $k$-mer shared
    between \emph{S.~pneumoniae} and \emph{S.~mitis} could rest nowhere but the genus, since NCBI's group is not an
    ancestor of the pneumococcus; afterwards it rests at a node leaving eight species open instead of \num{157}.

    That node has exactly two children, and the split is the point. One is \emph{S.~pneumoniae}, whose subtree holds
    \num{1729440} $k$-mers. The other holds the seven commensals and \num{27408759} $k$-mers, and the pneumococcus is not
    below it. Between the two, \num{707491} $k$-mers stay at the node itself, shared and undecidable --- $2.4$\,\% of the
    \num{29845690} that reach the mitis complex at all. So the refined database can also answer \emph{a mitis-group
    commensal, and not the pneumococcus} --- a negative answer on the pathogen, which is the distinction
    \citet{lrinanopore} had to settle in the laboratory. The unrefined database cannot give it at all: the \num{895103}
    $k$-mers that carry it sat at the genus, where they named all \num{157} species.

    Two qualifications keep that from being more than it is. The node is not the mitis group of the clinical literature:
    \emph{S.~infantis}, \emph{S.~cristatus} and \emph{S.~sanguinis} sit elsewhere in the refined tree, because the
    clustering groups by shared $k$-mers and not by nomenclature. And the \num{707491} $k$-mers at the node itself stay
    undecidable between the pathogen and its neighbours; that is the residue \citet{lrinanopore} needed a
    \emph{ply}-specific assay for, and no refinement can move it. The assay is still required. What the refined database
    adds is that it now says which reads require it.

    \paragraph{What a refinement cannot repair.} The pneumococcus is nameable in this database, and the credit belongs to
    the database rather than to the refinement. \emph{S.~pneumoniae} keeps \num{1273715} $k$-mers of its own and
    \num{1729440} counting its whole subtree, and those are in the unrefined variant too. An earlier build left it
    \num{656}; that figure was an artefact of a per-species cap that counted fasta entries instead of genomes and so gave
    the best-sequenced species of the genus two thirds of one assembly. It was not a fact about the mitis complex.

    What the refinement adds to the pneumococcus itself is nothing, and the quality file says so plainly. Its $\mathit{sprec}$ goes
    from $0.26794262$ to $0.26793533$ and its node precision from $0.00652790$ to $0.00652994$. The refinement does move
    \num{1174677} of its $k$-mers into a strain cluster below the species, but that cluster's node precision is
    $0.00652806$, which is $1/153$: the $k$-mers are carried by essentially every one of the \num{153} pneumococcal data
    taxa. They are core genome, and Section \ref{dbrefinement} predicts that core cannot move.

    This is also where $\mathit{sprec}$ stops tracking the clinical question. Its per-$k$-mer precision is $1/|D(a)|$ over data taxa,
    and data taxa are genomes, not species: \emph{S.~gordonii} has two of them and a node precision of $0.4997$,
    \emph{S.~pneumoniae} has \num{153} and $0.006528$. A $k$-mer that names the pneumococcus and nothing else therefore
    scores $1/153$, and the refined mitis node, which separates the pathogen from seven commensal species, scores
    $0.0060$. Neither $\mathit{sprec}$ nor $\mathit{sprec}^{*}$ can express the distinction this case study is about. The read-level $\rho$ of
    Section \ref{groundtruthfree} has to carry it.

    Over the whole database the refinement raises $\mathit{sprec}$ from $0.6040$ to $0.6216$ and $\mathit{sprec}^{*}$ from $0.2046$ to $0.2401$, a
    relative gain of $17.4$\,\% and the smallest of the databases in Table \ref{dbsp}. The gain is the right kind even so.
    A refinement never moves a $k$-mer across the species boundary, so the $\mathit{sprec}^{*}$ numerator splits exactly by position
    in the tree: \num{153303860} $k$-mers sit above the species rank in both variants, and their mean precision rises from
    $0.0074$ to $0.0535$, a factor of $7.2$. That is $73$\,\% of the gain. The remaining $27$\,\% is strain clustering
    inside species. The aggregate is small because half the material above the data taxa already sits at a species node,
    not because the work between species failed.

    The genus supplies its own control for what the neighbourhood costs. \emph{S.~mutans}, which has no close relative
    here, keeps \num{4118045} $k$-mers in its subtree, \emph{S.~uberis} \num{4251160} and \emph{S.~suis} \num{3847928}.
    The \num{1729440} of \emph{S.~pneumoniae} is the poorest of the genus's well-sequenced species, but of the same order,
    not the three orders the earlier build suggested. Part of the remaining gap is the cap and not the biology: it admits
    fifty of the \num{9263} pneumococcal assemblies, against fifty of \num{259} for \emph{S.~oralis} and fifty of
    \num{242} for \emph{S.~mitis}, so the pneumococcal accessory genome is by far the most thinly sampled of the three.
    The figure is a floor.

    The two unnamed branches of the genus --- \emph{unclassified Streptococcus}, with its \num{468} genomes, and
    \emph{environmental samples} --- are struck out of \texttt{strepto}, and \texttt{refseq.updateScope=allButExcluded}
    keeps them out of the LCA update as well. Under that update a genome the taxonomy cannot place is a sink: a $k$-mer
    shared between \emph{S.~pneumoniae} and an unnamed draft has its lowest common ancestor at the genus, so one such
    genome lifts a species' $k$-mers clear out of it. This is why the database holds \num{157} species where the release
    has \num{626}. The experiment that ran as a separate project \texttt{streptonamed} is folded into \texttt{strepto} and
    is no longer reported on its own.

    That exclusion makes a trade, and it is the failure mode this section is about: a $k$-mer that besides a kept species
    only an excluded genome carries now stays claimed for that species, so where the excluded genome was in truth a
    different streptococcus, a commensal read could be reported as the pathogen. The six extraction negatives of the
    study --- the same protocol with no sample --- are what tests it. Across all six, both variants and every ranked
    position, \emph{S.~pneumoniae} does not appear. The fourteen taxa that do are oral and environmental commensals:
    \emph{S.~mitis}, \emph{S.~oralis}, \emph{S.~gordonii}, \emph{S.~parasanguinis}, \emph{S.~salivarius} and their
    like. The trade did not produce a false pathogen. Two limits keep that from being more than it is: the negatives
    carry \num{176} classified reads between them, so the test has little power, and one of them ranks
    \emph{S.~pyogenes} first --- a pathogen, on a control with eleven placed reads spread over seven nodes.

    The read-level results of Section \ref{streptoreads} were remade on this build. An earlier run of them, on a
    database with \num{455031852} stored $k$-mers and a pneumococcus holding \num{656} of its own, found that across the
    \num{83} samples no classification ever landed below the genus. That finding does not survive the rebuild, and it
    was a statement about the cap rather than about the genus: with \num{1729440} $k$-mers below it, the pneumococcus is
    now the top call in four of the samples.

    \subsubsection{The reference standard, and what it can decide}
    Tables 1 and 2 of \citet{lrinanopore} give, per sample, the organism routine microbiology grew and the organism their own
    pipeline reported. We transcribed them into \texttt{data/projects/strepto/ground\_truth.csv} of the experiment project:
    \num{108} organism rows over \num{81} samples. This is a standard on the sample, not on the read. The measures of
    Section \ref{classmeasures} need $\sigma(r)$ and cannot use it, but the estimator of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} needs
    no ground truth and applies unchanged.

    A sample-level standard can still decide the question this genus poses. Six samples grew \emph{S.~pneumoniae} (P3, P28,
    P30, P33, P36, P40), and in six others the $k$-mer pipeline reported it where culture did not (S8, S10, S11, S15, S21,
    S28). The second group is not simply a list of errors. \citet{lrinanopore} took all \num{18} samples positive for a
    pathobiont --- their term for \emph{H.~influenzae} and \emph{S.~pneumoniae}, \enquote{potentially pathogenic organisms
    which may reside as commensals in the lung} --- and ran a species-specific gene assay on each, \emph{ply} for the
    pneumococcus and \emph{siaT} for \emph{H.~influenzae}. In five of them the organism was absent. Their explanation states
    the problem of this section from the outside:

    \begin{quote}
    \enquote{These false positive detections can be caused by misclassification of reads by WIMP, as $k$-mer based read
    classification can be unreliable at the species level, particularly where species in a genus are highly related or share
    genes.} \citep{lrinanopore}
    \end{quote}

    Their remedy was a second, organism-specific assay beside the classifier. A refinement does not remove the need for it,
    and we should say so plainly: the assay answers whether the pneumococcus is present, and no rearrangement of a taxonomy
    can answer that from $k$-mers the species do not differ in. What a refinement removes is the false confidence that made
    the assay the only way to find out. $K$-mers on which \emph{S.~pneumoniae} and its commensal neighbours agree move onto
    a node that names the group, and only $k$-mers that genuinely separate the species can still carry a species-level
    call --- so a classification either names a species and means it, or names the group and says that it cannot go
    further.

    Three classifiers fail here in three different ways, and only one of them is the baseline the rest of this paper measures
    against. WIMP is a front end for Centrifuge \citep{centrifuge}, which does not take the lowest common ancestor over its
    index the way Kraken does. It scores candidate genomes by $\sum_{\mathit{hit}}(\mathit{length}(\mathit{hit})-15)^2$ and
    reports the best-scoring labels, up to five per read, climbing the tree only to keep that number down. Nothing there
    stops an ambiguous read from being called to a species, and which species wins depends on the composition of the index:
    RefSeq holds \num{9263} genomes of \emph{S.~pneumoniae} against \num{242} of \emph{S.~mitis}.

    \subsubsection{What we measure, and what we do not}
    A reference standard stated per sample invites a classifier stated per sample, and we deliberately do not build one. A
    sputum sample is not one organism: it carries a community of streptococci, of which a pneumococcus is at best one
    member, and the standard itself names several organisms for nine of the thirteen samples it decides. Any rule that
    crowns a single taxon per sample answers a question the material does not pose, and its answer would be an artefact of
    the rule rather than of the database. The comparison this paper is about --- refined against unrefined --- does not
    need one either.

    What we report instead is the measure of Section \ref{groundtruthfree}, unchanged and per read: over the reads the
    unrefined database left at a genus, the reciprocal of the number of species its assignment still leaves open, before and
    after the refinement. It needs no $\sigma(r)$, it is the same quantity already read on the saliva runs and the tick
    samples, and it rests on \dbstat{rg}{strepto/reads} reads rather than on thirteen samples. Table \ref{realgain} carries the figures
    beside those two.

    The refinement raises it from \dbstat{rg}{strepto/precu} to \dbstat{rg}{strepto/precf}, from
    \dbstat{rg}{strepto/openu} species still open to \dbstat{rg}{strepto/openf}. That is
    \dbstat{rg}{strepto/gain}\,\%, over \dbstat{rg}{strepto/subset} reads in
    \dbstat{rg}{strepto/samples} of the \dbstat{nn}{strepto/samples} samples --- the rest left no read at the genus at all.
    \dbstat{rg}{strepto/improved} samples improve, \dbstat{rg}{strepto/flat} are unchanged and
    \dbstat{rg}{strepto/worse} gets worse. The two database variants classify the same reads: the number a sample has
    classified is identical in every row of \texttt{strepto\_lri\_specificity.csv}, which is what makes the pair
    comparable at all.

    Set that against the $17.4$\,\% by which the same refinement raises $\mathit{sprec}^{*}$. The two measures are averages of
    different things over different sets and neither bounds the other, but the direction of the difference is the one
    Section \ref{dbrefinement} predicts: $\mathit{sprec}^{*}$ divides by data taxa, which are genomes, and fifty near-identical
    pneumococcal genomes hold it near $1/153$ whatever the refinement does. The read-level measure divides by the species
    a classification leaves open, which is the quantity a clinician reads, and on it the same refinement is worth a factor
    of $2.3$.

    Where the pneumococcus actually is, it is worth more. Splitting the samples by what the reference standard says about
    it gives the three blocks of Table \ref{realgain}: \dbstat{rg}{strepto/cultgain}\,\% where culture grew
    \emph{S.~pneumoniae}, \dbstat{rg}{strepto/pipegain}\,\% where only the source study's pipeline called it, and
    \dbstat{rg}{strepto/nonegain}\,\% where neither named it. All six culture-confirmed samples improve, between
    $76$\,\% and $454$\,\%.

    The relative ordering has a cause worth stating, because it is not that the refinement works better on some samples
    than on others. Unrefined, a sample with the pneumococcus in it is scored barely half as specifically as one without
    --- \dbstat{rg}{strepto/cultprecu} against \dbstat{rg}{strepto/noneprecu} --- and it strands twice as large a share
    of its classified reads at the genus, \dbstat{rg}{strepto/cultgroupshare}\,\% against
    \dbstat{rg}{strepto/nonegroupshare}\,\%. That deficit is the mitis complex, and it is exactly what Section
    \ref{streptocase} predicts from the tree. After the refinement the two are \dbstat{rg}{strepto/cultprecf} and
    \dbstat{rg}{strepto/noneprecf}. The refinement does not favour the pneumococcus; it removes the penalty the
    pneumococcus's own neighbourhood imposes.

    The last two columns of Table \ref{realgain} stay empty for \texttt{strepto}. Carrying an ungated precision to the
    gated one it stands for needs $\rho^u$ and $\rho^f$ from simulated reads of this same database, and those do not
    exist yet. \todo{Generate them --- \texttt{make\_fastqs.sh strepto} and the \texttt{strepto} target of
    \texttt{run\_classification\_exps.sh} --- and check first whether the extracted genomes under
    \texttt{data/projects/strepto/fasta/} predate the rebuild of 2026-08-26, since the per-species selection changed with
    it. Borrowing another database's calibration would be worse than leaving the columns empty.}

    \paragraph{What the sample-level standard says.} The reference standard also allows the cruder comparison, and it is
    worth reporting because it comes out flat. Naming one taxon per sample below the genus, from
    \texttt{strepto\_lri\_taxoncall.csv}, the top call is identical between the two variants in all
    \dbstat{nn}{strepto/topsame} samples, for each of the \dbstat{nn}{strepto/currencies} currencies the report offers
    --- reads, $k$-mers and unique $k$-mers. Not one sample changes. \emph{S.~pneumoniae} is the top call in
    \dbstat{nn}{strepto/topnamed} of them, \dbstat{nn}{strepto/topnamedlist}, all of them culture-confirmed, and it is
    the same set before and after. The \dbstat{nn}{strepto/topmissed} culture-confirmed samples it misses are called
    \dbstat{nn}{strepto/topmissedlist} --- both mitis-complex neighbours, and both missed by the refined variant too. That is the \dbstat{nn}{strepto/parentkmers} undecidable $k$-mers of the mitis node appearing as clinical error, and no refinement
    can move them.

    Two things follow. The pneumococcus is named by the unrefined database already, which settles where the credit for
    that belongs: the per-species genome cap, not the refinement. And a classifier stated per sample cannot see what a
    refinement does, since the finer node a read reaches carries no name in the reference taxonomy and so cannot be
    reported as a taxon at all. The decision not to build one is therefore not only a judgement about what a sputum
    sample is; it is what the measurement shows.

    Table \ref{nearestnode} reads the same file the other way round. Rather than name a taxon, it asks
    how far towards \emph{S.~pneumoniae} each sample's evidence reached and how many species are still
    in question there --- which is the only way a refined node can be described at all, since it bears
    no name of the reference taxonomy. The two unrefined rows have nothing in their middle columns,
    and that is structural rather than a finding: with the pneumococcus a direct child of the genus,
    an unrefined database has exactly two answers available to it, the species or the genus. The
    refinement fills the gap between them.

    What it does not establish is how much of a sample went there. The median share of the in-genus
    evidence sitting at the reported node is \dbstat{nn}{strepto/reads/f/medianshareat} in reads and
    \dbstat{nn}{strepto/kmers/f/medianshareat} in $k$-mers, so for many samples the depth reached is
    the depth one read or one $k$-mer reached. The share at or below the node is far larger ---
    \dbstat{nn}{strepto/reads/f/mediansharebelow} and \dbstat{nn}{strepto/kmers/f/mediansharebelow}
    --- but that counts the commensal siblings of the same clade and says nothing about the
    pneumococcal lineage. Read as a statement about what the tree makes available, the table is sound;
    read as a statement about what a sample supports, it is not, and the two must not be conflated.
    None of the samples in those middle columns is one culture confirmed for the pneumococcus: all six
    of those already reach the species in both variants.

    two corrections. Empty \emph{OTHER} buckets were counted as a candidate species, which added one

    One column of that file invites a wrong reading. Its \emph{unplaced} count is the reads not placed at a \emph{named}
    taxon below the genus, and it rises under the refinement, from \dbstat{nn}{strepto/reads/u/unplaced} to \dbstat{nn}{strepto/reads/f/unplaced}. This is not a regression.
    A read that lands on a refined node has no taxon name to report and counts as unplaced even where that node sits
    below the species; the same reads are simultaneously more specific, which is what the
    \dbstat{rg}{strepto/precu}~$\rightarrow$~\dbstat{rg}{strepto/precf} above measures. The number of contributing nodes
    rises with it, from \dbstat{nn}{strepto/reads/u/contribnodes} to \dbstat{nn}{strepto/reads/f/contribnodes}.

    \subsubsection{Read data}\label{streptoreads}
    The clinical data are the \num{83} nanopore runs of \citet{lrinanopore}, deposited under ENA study PRJEB30781: forty
    samples processed with that study's pilot protocol and forty-three with its optimized one, \num{2271441} reads of about
    $2.7$\,kbp mean length. They were sequenced shotgun after saponin-based depletion of human DNA, not by amplicon, and that
    matters more here than anywhere else in this paper: the $16S$ genes of \emph{S.~pneumoniae} and \emph{S.~mitis} are
    almost identical, so an amplicon run carries hardly a $k$-mer that separates them and could not show a refinement gain
    even in principle.

    The study's six process extraction negatives are analysed beside them as a specificity control. They went through the
    same protocol with no sample and hold \num{510} reads in total, so anything the refined database reports in them is
    reagent contamination or a false positive.

    The human genome is declared to the database through \texttt{additional.txt} without being one of the requested taxa, as
    it is for \texttt{cv} and its saliva runs. That declaration lets the LCA update account for the host: a $k$-mer that a
    streptococcus shares with \emph{Homo sapiens} is raised above it instead of staying under the streptococcus, where it
    would turn human reads into hits. The saponin depletion of \citet{lrinanopore} reduces host DNA but does not remove it,
    and in a respiratory specimen what remains is still the most common kind of read in the file.

\subsection{Path precision}\label{apppathprecision}

Section \ref{nodeprecision} assesses refinement quality per node. It can equivalently be expressed by counting $k$-mers per data taxon, which yields a more conventional precision. We develop it here for reference and to relate it to the measures used in the main text.

Let $t$ be a data taxon of a database $d$, as defined in Section \ref{pathcorrectness}. The following counts assess the quality of the taxonomic refinement per data taxon $t$ in $d$:
\begin{description}
%   Cut in the shortening pass along with path recall, kept to restore easily:
%   \item \textbf{Positive counts $P$ per data taxon $t$ in database $d$:} The number of unique $k$-mers in $d$ that occur in the genome associated with $t$.

    \item \textbf{True positive counts $TP$ per data taxon $t$ in database $d$:} The number of unique $k$-mers in $d$ that occur in the genome associated with $t$ where in $d$, such $k$-mers are associated with some taxon on the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root including $t$ itself.

    \item \textbf{True positive plus false positive counts $TP + FP$ per data taxon $t$ in database $d$:} The number of unique $k$-mers in $d$ assigned to any taxon on the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root including $t$ itself.
\end{description}

   These counts allow for defining \emph{path precision} as $TP / (TP + FP)$.

   As an example, consider Figure \ref{ftsketch} from above: In (3), the path precision of $Z$ is $2/3$ because there are three $2$-mers on the path from $Z$ to the root $W$ but one of them, \texttt{CT}, is not in $W$'s sequence. In (3), $X$ and $Y$ both have a path precision of $1$. The refined database in (4) has a precision of 1 for $X$, $Y$ \emph{and} $Z$.

%   Cut in the shortening pass (now named in Section \ref{nodeprecision}), kept to restore easily:
%   Path recall is the counting counterpart of path correctness: a $k$-mer $a$ that occurs in the genome of $t$ contributes to $P$ in any case, but to $TP$ only if $\nu(a)$ lies on the path from $t$ to the root. Hence $TP = P$ for a data taxon $t$ exactly if the condition of Section \ref{pathcorrectness} holds for every $k$-mer supported by $t$, and \emph{a database is path correct if and only if its path recall equals one for every data taxon}. Since both the LCA update and the refinement establish that condition by construction, path recall carries no information beyond the boolean statement, which is why the main text uses the latter.

    Path precision is a genuine quality measure and an alternative to node precision. We report unweighted\footnote{The unweighted average considers every data taxon as equally relevant.} average path precision by averaging over all data taxa in $d$ or subsets thereof, in particular over all taxa under the same species or the same genus. E.g., in (3) of Figure \ref{ftsketch}, the average path precision of the genus $W$ is 8/9.

    The counts of a data taxon $t$ above refer to the entire path from $t$ to the taxonomic root. As for node precision, it is useful to relativize them to a subtree: let $m$ be a node of $d$'s taxonomy tree and let $D_m$ be the set of data taxa under $m$ including $m$ itself. For a data taxon $t \in D_m$, let $TP_m(t)$ and $(TP + FP)_m(t)$ be defined as above, but with respect to the path from $t$ to $m$ -- including $t$ and $m$ themselves -- instead of the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root. Besides the unweighted average, we then consider the \emph{weighted} average path precision of the subtree rooted at $m$,\footnote{We deliberately refrain from smoothing $\overline{pp}_w(m)$ -- or $\mathit{nprec}(n)$ from Section \ref{nodeprecision} -- by a Laplace correction, i.e.\ from adding a virtual $k$-mer per node that counts as a true positive by definition. Such a correction contributes the same amount to numerator and denominator, and since the ratio is at most one, every contribution of this kind strictly raises it -- without a single $k$-mer of $d$ supporting the increase. Its total size grows with the number of nodes of the subtree, and the refinement is precisely a procedure that \emph{adds} nodes to the taxonomy. A corrected measure would therefore reward a refined tree over an unrefined one for its mere node count, and it would do so the more the deeper the refined subtrees become, i.e.\ exactly along the axis the measure is meant to assess.}
\[ \overline{pp}_w(m) = \frac{\sum_{t \in D_m} TP_m(t)}{\sum_{t \in D_m} (TP + FP)_m(t)} , \]
    which relates the pooled counts instead of averaging the per-taxon ratios. For $m$ being the taxonomic root, it ranges over all data taxa of $d$ and hence assesses the database as a whole. The measure is undefined if no $k$-mer at all resides in the subtree rooted at $m$.

    Note that for a taxonomy tree with a branching factor $\geq 2$ (which is the standard case) a high average path precision ensures that a $k$-mer $a$ in $d$ is located as closely as possible above a data taxon or right at a data taxon whose genome(s) contain $a$. Otherwise, if $a$ was located higher up in the taxonomy, then $a$ would also be on the path to the root starting from a data taxon $t$ whose genome does not contain $a$. But this would increase the $FP$-count of $t$ and in turn would lower the average path precision.

    Grouping the pooled counts by the node holding each $k$-mer rather than by data taxon shows how $\overline{pp}_w(m)$ relates to the subtree precision $\mathit{sprec}(m)$ of Section \ref{nodeprecision}. Both average the per-$k$-mer precision $\mathit{prec}(a)$ over the $k$-mers of the subtree, but $\overline{pp}_w(m)$ weights each $k$-mer by $|D_{\nu(a)}|$, i.e.\ by the number of data taxa its node covers, whereas $\mathit{sprec}(m)$ weights all $k$-mers equally. Path precision therefore lets $k$-mers sitting high in the taxonomy dominate the value, while subtree precision does not, which is the reason the main text builds on the latter.
