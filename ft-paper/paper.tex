    \section{Background}\label{background}

    \subsection{Introduction}
\label{problem}

$k$-mer based classification tools such as Kraken 1 \citep{kraken}, Kraken 2 \citep{kraken2}, KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq} and Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub} assign reads to taxa by matching their $k$-mers against a database that maps each $k$-mer to a node of a taxonomy tree. Since a $k$-mer may occur in the genomes of several organisms, a $k$-mer database is subjected to the \emph{lowest common ancestor update} (LCA update): a $k$-mer occurring in the genome of more than one taxon is moved up to the lowest node that holds all corresponding taxa as descendants. Later, at classification time, the LCA update prevents $k$-mers in reads from being falsely assigned to an overly low-ranked taxon, since genomes of other taxa such as siblings contain that $k$-mer too. Thus, essentially, the LCA update avoids a potentially false assignment of overly specific taxa to reads.

Unfortunately, the LCA update comes at a price that originates in the ever-growing number of genomes from underlying source databases such as RefSeq: as Nasko et al. \citep{Nasko:2018aa} have shown, that it leads to a growing set of species and corresponding genomes under a single genus.
    Each newly added species closely related to an already covered one turns a $k$-mer that used to be specific to a single species into a shared one, and so the LCA update moves it up towards the genus. Consequently, with newer RefSeq versions \emph{more} reads are classified in total, but \emph{fewer} of them are classified on the species level \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. In other words, the resolution of $k$-mer databases deteriorates while the underlying genomic data deepens.
    We observed the same effect in our first study on Genestrip, where databases with a deeper genomic coverage for organisms of interest classified more reads but tended to accumulate them on higher ranks \citep{genestrip-pub}.

Regarding RefSeq, the expansion happens at a remarkable pace: between its releases 66 (July 2014) and 71 (July 2015) alone, the number of bacterial organisms grew by 40 \% to 39,660, so that release 71 covered more than 55,000 organisms in total \citep{refseq}. A few years later, Nasko et al. quantified what this influx does to $k$-mer based classification, and since their study the expansion has continued unabated: the number of prokaryotic genomes in RefSeq alone has increased by a further factor of 5.8 \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038}.
    The trend persists to this day: in the two years from release 225 (July 2024) to release 236 the number of covered organisms grew by about 20 \% from 152,668 to 182,465 \citep{refseqstats}.

Figure \ref{kmerongenusborrelia} illustrates the effect on the distribution of $k$-mers across ranks for two Genestrip databases: regarding the bacterial database \texttt{tb}, a median of about 68 \% of a species' $k$-mers no longer reside at the species rank or below but somewhere between the genus and the species rank, whereas for \texttt{protozoa} the picture is almost reversed, with a median of about 90 \% of a species' $k$-mers remaining at the species rank or below.
    Both databases rest exclusively on RefSeq genomes, and bacteria are covered by far more close relatives than protozoa: release 236 comprises 109,133 bacterial but merely 944 protozoan species \citep{refseqstats}. The $k$-mers of a bacterial species are pushed up accordingly. A second effect works alongside it and in the opposite direction: where several genomes of one species are available, they enlarge that species' own set of specific $k$-mers without adding to what it shares with its relatives. The share of $k$-mers remaining at the species rank therefore grows with how deeply a species is covered, just as it shrinks with the number of its close relatives.

    At first glance, such $k$-mers appear to be lost for fine-grained classification, but Figure \ref{kmerviaranks} shows that this is not the case: for the $k$-mers that ended up on a genus, we determined the number of the genus' children whose genomes actually contain them.
    A median of about 84 \% (\texttt{tb}) and 88 \% (\texttt{protozoa}) of them occur in just \emph{two} of the children, and the share drops steeply for higher numbers. So a $k$-mer sitting on a genus is typically not characteristic of the genus as a whole but just of a small group of species under it. The LCA update simply has no place to put it, because the taxonomy offers no node that represents exactly this group. \emph{This is the potential that our approach exploits}: if the taxonomy is refined by additional nodes that do represent such groups, then the very same $k$-mers can potentially be pushed back down and become informative again.

    To the best of our knowledge, none of the established $k$-mer based classification tools makes use of this potential. Reads whose $k$-mers would suffice to narrow the candidates down to two or three species are therefore often reported on the genus, even though the information about which species actually share these $k$-mers is fully contained in the underlying genomes.

One route to improving the database is to correct the classification results afterwards. E.g., Bracken \citep{lu2017bracken} re-estimates species-level abundances from the output of a Kraken-style classification by redistributing the reads assigned to higher ranks according to a background distribution, which describes how the $k$-mers of each species in the database are expected to spread across the taxonomy. Nasko et al. \citep{Nasko:2018aa} confirm that this mitigates the shift described above, but also that it struggles with novel genomes. The approach differs from a refinement of the database itself: First, Bracken does not add information but reallocates counts on the basis of \emph{probabilistic assumptions} about which species are present and how their $k$-mers distribute. Its estimates degrade when a sample deviates from these assumptions such as for rare or unexpected organisms. Second, it operates on aggregated abundances of an entire sample rather than on individual reads, so that a single read remains classified on the genus even after the re-estimation. A refined database, in contrast, relies on \emph{hard data}: which species actually share a given $k$-mer is known exactly from the underlying genomes, and this knowledge is encoded in the database itself. Each read is then classified more specifically by construction rather than by estimation.
%... , and any subsequent analysis -- including an abundance estimation -- benefits from it.

    \subsection{Approach}
    \label{approach}
    Our contribution builds on the first study on Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub} by further improving precision of read classifications without sacrificing recall. Genestrip-FT refines the taxonomy tree of a $k$-mer database by grouping those taxa that share many $k$-mers, and it reassigns the affected $k$-mers to the newly added nodes. Hereby, a $k$-mer is only pushed down as far as it may still be attributed to all taxa whose genomes contain it, so that no read is ever classified below the level justified by the underlying genomes.

Figure \ref{ftsketch} captures our approach with regard to a simplistic $k$-mer database
with $k = 2$:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Species $X$, $Y$, $Z$ under a common genus $W$ have respective sequences associated. 
	\item After filling the database, the $2$-mers are assigned to their species. The figure simplifies the state of the database after this step.
	\item The lowest common ancestor update (LCA update) pushes the $2$-mer \texttt{CT} up to $W$ because it occurs in the sequence of both $X$ and $Y$. However, it does not occur in $Z$. So, according to this database a read \texttt{CT} would just be classified as $W$ although $Z$ is not even a candidate species for \texttt{CT}.
	\item Genestrip-FT's refinement adds an artificial node $h$ to the taxonomy with $X$ and $Y$ as children but not $Z$. Also, the refinement \emph{pushes down} \texttt{CT} from $W$ to $h$. According to this refined database, a read \texttt{CT} would be classified as $h$ and so, $Z$ is excluded as candidate species for \texttt{CT}.
\end{enumerate}
 So in general, this type of refinement potentially improves classification precision: E.g., if a read was classified under a genus taxon in a unrefined database, then \emph{the corresponding refined database may allow for narrowing down the candidate species to fewer species than originally listed under that genus}.

\subsection{Refinement procedure}
\label{dbrefinement}
   
We explain the system's refinement procedure by means of a small and initially unrefined example database including three species under the genus Orthopoxvirus. The structure of the unrefined database is shown in Figure \ref{simplelcadb}. For the sake of simplicity, we restrict the refinement to the rank genus only, although \emph{it is normally performed simultaneously on all levels of the database's taxonomy} but its leaves.

Genestrip structures the complex computation of a $k$-mer database via goals as explained in \cite{genestrip-pub}. The goals form a dependency graph where a goal $g_1$ is executed only if its computational result is needed by a dependent goal $g_2$, and if $g_1$'s result has not been computed yet. Genestrip-FT is implemented as an extension of the original goal graph formed by Genestrip. Figure \ref{ftgoals} presents an extract of the additional goals, with most of Genestrip's original goals omitted. 

  \begin{figure}[!htbp]%
        \centering        
\includegraphics[width=8cm]{figs/ft-goals-extract.pdf}
         \caption{Extract of additional goals required by Genestrip-FT for the refinement of a corresponding $k$-mer database. The file goal \texttt{ftdb}, prefixed with \texttt{f:} stores the refined database as a file and depends on several, so-called object goals prefixed with \texttt{o:}. Genestrip-FT's goals also depend on some of Genestrip's original goals with the latter depicted at the top in dashed boxes. An extract of Genestrip's original goals can be found in the Appendix of \cite{genestrip-pub}. The different types of dependencies and goals (such as object goals or file goals) are explained there too.}\label{ftgoals}
    \end{figure}   

In the following, we briefly explain the essential goals needed for the computation of a refined database:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \verb|kmerindexbloom| associates each $k$-mer stored on the genus rank with the original species taxon of the sequence in which it occurred. To achieve this, the original sequences that were used to generate the unrefined database must all be reread. The mapping of a $k$-mer to its original species taxon is hashed; the resulting hash code is stored via a Bloom filter \cite{bloomfilter} in order to save main memory.\footnote{A Bloom filter is a probabilistic data structure for an efficient and compressed storing of values but also for a fast lookup of entered values. However, this comes at the expense of a configurable, usually low false positive rate at lookup time. Bloom filters and probabilistic filters in general \cite{10.1145/2674005.2674994,9920402,10.1145/3510449,10.1145/3376122} are widely used in the context of metagenomics tools \cite{biobloom, Piro:2020aa, 10.1093/nargab/lqaf094}.}
	
	\item \verb|intersectcount| computes the number of common $k$-mers $c(t_1, t_2)$ between any two species taxa $t_1$ and $t_2$ under the same genus. To do so, it consults the Bloom filter produced via the goal \verb|kmerindexbloom|. Based on $c(t_1, t_2)$, it also determines a Laplace-smoothed Jaccard index
	\[ J(t_1, t_2) = \frac{c(t_1, t_2) + 1}{c(t_1, t_1) + c(t_2, t_2) - c(t_1, t_2) + 1} \]
	\cite{jaccard} as a similarity measure of the two sets of $k$-mers belonging to $t_1$ and $t_2$ on the genus, respectively. The Laplace correction adds $1$ to both the intersection count (numerator) and each self-count (denominator), which avoids division by zero when both species share no $k$-mers on the genus and yields $J = 1$ in that degenerate case. Regarding our running example, Table \ref{intersectioncounts} shows the raw intersection counts whereas Table \ref{jaccard} states the resulting Jaccard indices. For simplicity, we skip the background of the column \enquote{OTHER} for now --- it will be explained at the end of this section.

	\item The goal \verb|dendrogram| from Figure \ref{ftgoals} computes a dendrogram using the standard agglomerative clustering algorithm \enquote{HAC} \cite{King01031967,1394399}, where the cluster distance is based on the Jaccard index $J$ from \verb|intersectcount|. Since the taxonomic refinement is governed by the aim of improving classification precision (see Section \ref{approach}), we chose \enquote{Single Linkage} \cite{King01031967} as the default linkage strategy. This linkage strategy is suitable because it merges clusters on the basis of their most similar $k$-mer sets they contain. Nevertheless, other linkage strategies such as UPGMA and WPGMA \cite{Sokal1958ASM} may be configured instead. Figure \ref{dendrogram} depicts the resulting dendrogram as computed on the basis of Table \ref{jaccard}.

	\item The goal \verb|ftupdatedb| reorganizes the database's taxonomy tree by adding new tree nodes under a genus as induced by the previous goal's dendrogram. Furthermore, $k$-mers are pushed down, i.e. reassigned, to the newly added nodes accordingly. Hereby, each $k$-mer is pushed down as far to a new node $n$, such that all species with sequences containing that $k$-mer still remain descendants of $n$.

	\item Finally, \verb|ftdb| stores the refined databases as a file.
\end{enumerate} 

	Figure \ref{simplerefdb} depicts the refined taxonomy for the Orthopoxvirus example and also states the number of $k$-mers as stored per node in the refined database. As can be seen, about 56 \% of the $k$-mers that originally resided on the genus Orthopoxvirus got pushed down to the newly added nodes (see Figure \ref{simplelcadb} for comparison). The new nodes obtain an artificial taxon prefixed with \enquote{000} to distinguish them from regular taxonomic entries, and their artificial rank is set to \enquote{REFINED}.

\subsection{Quality measurements}

Two kinds of measure are needed, and both are implemented in Genestrip rather than derived afterwards from its output. The first assess a database as such, without reference to any reads: how specifically the $k$-mers it stores are placed in its taxonomy. The second assess what that placement means for the classification of reads, and come in two forms --- one that scores a classification against a known ground truth, and one that does without, so that a refinement can also be judged on real data.
\label{intrinsicqms}    
    In this section, we consider new quality measures regarding $k$-mer databases irrespective of read analysis and read classification results.
    For this purpose, we establish \emph{path correctness} as a prerequisite and the precision of a single $k$-mer as the actual quality measure, from which \emph{node precision} and \emph{subtree precision} follow by averaging.
    The three measures generally apply to any $k$-mer database $d$ that has a taxonomy tree associated and where stored $k$-mers have taxa, i.e., nodes, assigned from that tree.
    In the results section, we use the new precision measures along with well-established classification-based measures to assess refinement quality. \todo{As will be shown, these new intrinsic measures correlate well with the established, classification-based measures.}

\subsubsection{Data taxa and path correctness}\label{pathcorrectness}
    Let $t$ be a \emph{data taxon}, i.e., \emph{a taxon with a complete genome directly associated whereby the $k$-mers from that genome are also stored in $d$}. For example in RefSeq, complete genomes are often associated with taxa of the rank species or below. Moreover, let $\nu(a)$ denote the node at which a $k$-mer $a$ is stored in $d$; since each $k$-mer is stored exactly once, $\nu$ is well defined.

For a fair assessment of the measures below, we require that each data taxon must be a leaf node in the taxonomy tree of database $d$. This can easily be enforced by a tree transformation explained via (3) and (4) in Figure \ref{fttrans}. Without it, a data taxon would compete against its own descendants: since $D_n$ comprises the data taxa under $n$ including $n$ itself, a $k$-mer occurring in that taxon's genome alone is stored at its node, yet counted against every data taxon below it as well --- and those genomes have no reason to contain it. Its precision would fall short of one although no lower node could hold it, so the measure would penalise a placement that is in fact optimal, and the penalty would grow with the number of data taxa that happen to sit underneath (as in Figure \ref{fttrans} (1) and (2)). Note that the described transformation leaves the phylogenetic structure of a given taxonomy intact.
Figure \ref{simplerefdbtrans} exemplifies the transformation when integrated into the refinement of the database from Figure \ref{simplelcadb}.\footnote{When carefully comparing the $k$-mer counts of similar nodes from Figures \ref{simplerefdb} and \ref{simplerefdbtrans}, one finds minor differences such as for \enquote{10245/OTHER}. Note that these differences originate in the use of a probabilistic data structure, i.e. a Bloom filter, during the refinement procedure according to Section \ref{dbrefinement}.}

    On this basis, we call a $k$-mer database $d$ \emph{path correct} if
for every $k$-mer $a$ stored in $d$ and every data taxon $t$ whose genome contains $a$, the node $\nu(a)$ is an ancestor of $t$ or $t$ itself. Equivalently, $\nu(a)$ lies on the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root for each such $t$.

    Both unrefined and refined databases are path correct by construction. The LCA update moves a $k$-mer up in the taxonomy just far enough that all data taxa whose genomes contain it become descendants of the resulting node. The refinement, in turn, may push a $k$-mer down only so far that the condition above is still maintained --- this is what keeps \verb|ftupdatedb| from placing a $k$-mer below the lowest common ancestor of the data taxa supporting it.\footnote{Due to its probabilistic nature, the Bloom filter used during the \texttt{kmerindexbloom} step (see Section~\ref{dbrefinement}) occasionally falsely attributes a species to a $k$-mer, which may prevent the $k$-mer from being pushed down to its lowest possible node. This leaves path correctness intact and only keeps the $k$-mer at a less specific node than necessary.}

\subsubsection{Node precision and subtree precision}\label{nodeprecision}
Given path correctness, every data taxon under a $k$-mer's node is a candidate carrier of that $k$-mer, and how many of them actually carry it indicates how well the $k$-mer is placed.

    Let $D_n$ denote the set of data taxa under a node $n$ including $n$ itself. For a $k$-mer $a$ stored in $d$ with its node $\nu(a)$ from Section \ref{pathcorrectness}, let $c(a)$ be the number of data taxa $t \in D_{\nu(a)}$ whose genomes contain $a$ at least once. We define the \emph{precision of the $k$-mer $a$} as
\[ p(a) = \frac{c(a)}{|D_{\nu(a)}|} , \]
    i.e.\ as the fraction of the data taxa under $a$'s node whose genomes actually contain $a$. It is defined whenever $D_{\nu(a)} \neq \emptyset$, which excludes only the $k$-mers stored at artificial nodes carrying no data taxon at all.

    This is where the leaf requirement of Section \ref{pathcorrectness} earns its keep. Without it, a data taxon would compete against its own descendants: since $D_n$ comprises the data taxa under $n$ including $n$ itself, a $k$-mer occurring in that taxon's genome alone is stored at its node, yet counted against every data taxon below it as well --- and those genomes have no reason to contain it. Its precision would fall short of one although no lower node could hold it, so the measure would penalise a placement that is in fact optimal, and the penalty would grow with the number of data taxa that happen to sit underneath (as in Figure \ref{fttrans} (1) and (2)).

    Due to path correctness, every data taxon whose genome contains $a$ is subordinate to $\nu(a)$, and so the count $c(a)$ does not depend on where $a$ is stored: it is simply the number of data taxa in $d$ that support $a$. Only $|D_{\nu(a)}|$ varies with the placement, and it is smallest when $\nu(a)$ is the lowest common ancestor of the data taxa supporting $a$.
    Note that raising $p(a)$ is the very purpose of the refinement as it introduces new nodes and moves $k$-mers onto them.

    Averaging $p$ over the $k$-mers stored at a single node $n$ yields \emph{node precision} $np(n)$. With $A_n$ denoting the set of $k$-mers associated with that node:
\[ np(n) = \frac{1}{|A_n|} \sum_{a \in A_n} p(a). \]
    Averaging $p$ over the $k$-mers of an entire subtree yields \emph{subtree precision} $sp(m)$. Let $m$ be a node of $d$'s taxonomy tree and let
$A^{*}_m = \bigcup_{n} A_n$ be the set of $k$-mers stored at the nodes $n$ of the subtree rooted at $m$ --- including $m$ itself --- with $D_n \neq \emptyset$.
    Then
\[ sp(m) = \frac{1}{|A^{*}_m|} \sum_{a \in A^{*}_m} p(a) . \]
    For $m$ being the taxonomic root, $A^{*}_m$ comprises all $k$-mers of $d$ that reside at a node with data taxa underneath, and $sp(m)$ hence assesses the database as a whole; for $m$ being a genus, it assesses the refinement of that genus in isolation.
    The two averages are undefined when the set of $k$-mers they range over is empty, i.e.\ $np(n)$ for a node without $k$-mers and $sp(m)$ for a subtree without any.
    Moreover, given the transformation from Figure \ref{fttrans}, it follows by definition that $np(n) = 1$ for a node $n$ of a data taxon $t$ that stores $k$-mers at all.

    A $k$-mer stored at a data taxon has $|D_{\nu(a)}| = 1$ after the transformation of Section \ref{pathcorrectness} and therefore $p(a) = 1$ by construction. Such $k$-mers make up the bulk of a database --- between 62\,\% and 99\,\% of those considered here --- so they dominate $sp$ while being incapable of improvement, and we report alongside it the \emph{restricted subtree precision} $sp^{*}(m)$, the same average taken over those $k$-mers of the subtree for which $p$ is defined and which are \emph{not} stored at a data taxon --- the ones a refinement can still move.

    In both averages, every stored $k$-mer contributes equally, regardless of how the taxonomy happens to distribute the $k$-mers over nodes. In particular, inserting an inner node into the taxonomy changes neither $\nu(a)$ nor $|D_{\nu(a)}|$ nor $c(a)$ for any $k$-mer already stored, so neither $np$ nor $sp$ can be improved by refining the tree alone.
    Note also that $np(n)$ may deteriorate if data taxa's genomes under $n$ are \emph{incomplete}, because in this case a $k$-mer $a$ at node $n$ might simply not be supported by such taxa, even though their completed genome would contain $a$.

    \subsubsection{Measuring classification quality}\label{classmeasures}

    Let $r$ be a read, let $\sigma(r)$ be the species taxon its underlying genome belongs to and let $\kappa(r)$ be the taxon the analysis assigns to it, which is undefined if $r$ remains unclassified. Reads whose $\sigma(r)$ cannot be determined are excluded from all counts below, as they can be neither right nor wrong.

    The customary way to score such an assignment is a \emph{boolean positive count}: $r$ counts as a hit if $\kappa(r)$ is equal to or below $\sigma(r)$, and as a miss otherwise, and likewise one rank higher with the genus in place of the species. Both variants, however, are blind to what a refinement does. A refinement introduces nodes \emph{between} a genus and its species and moves $k$-mers onto them, so a read that used to be classified to its genus now tends to end up at one of these new nodes. That is a substantial gain --- the read is narrowed down from all species of the genus to a handful --- yet the boolean count at the genus rank scores it as a hit in both cases and the one at the species rank as a miss in both cases. Neither of them registers the improvement at all.

    We therefore refine the count in proportion to how far the assignment narrows the species down. For a node $n$ of $d$'s taxonomy tree let
\[ \Sigma(n) = \{ s : s \text{ is a species taxon of } d \text{ and } s \text{ lies on a common path to the root with } n \} \]
    be the set of \emph{species in question} at $n$, i.e.\ the species that are still consistent with an assignment to $n$: those below $n$ and, if there are none because $n$ itself lies below the species rank, the one above it. The \emph{ratio-based positive count} of a read then is
\[ q(r) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{|\Sigma(\kappa(r))|} & \text{if } \sigma(r) \in \Sigma(\kappa(r)), \\[2ex] 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \]
    and $q(r) = 0$ for an unclassified read. So an assignment that pins the species down scores $1$, an assignment to a genus comprising $40$ species scores $1/40$, and one to a refined node covering $3$ of them scores $1/3$. In particular $q(r)$ agrees with the boolean count at the species rank whenever the species is actually determined, and it grades the cases in between that the boolean counts collapse.

    Two points deserve attention when $\Sigma(n)$ is determined. First, the species are counted over the \emph{entire} subtree rooted at $n$ rather than among its children, because a taxonomy may well place intermediate nodes such as a subgenus between a genus and its species; counting children would let such a node hide the species underneath it. Second, the counting takes place in the taxonomy of the database at hand. Since the refinement adds nodes but neither adds nor removes species taxa, $\Sigma(n)$ is the same in the refined and the unrefined database for every node $n$ that exists in both. What differs between the two is $\kappa(r)$ --- the node a read is assigned to --- which is precisely the effect to be measured.

    From these counts we obtain precision, recall and the $F_1$-score in the usual way: with $R$ denoting the reads of a fastq file whose species is known and $R_c \subseteq R$ those that were classified at all,
\[ \mathit{prec} = \frac{\sum_{r \in R_c} q(r)}{|R_c|}, \qquad \mathit{rec} = \frac{\sum_{r \in R} q(r)}{|R|}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \mathit{prec} \cdot \mathit{rec}}{\mathit{prec} + \mathit{rec}} , \]
    and analogously for the boolean counts, which we report alongside for comparison with the literature.

    One further effect must be accounted for before these averages can be read as a statement about the refinement. They range over \emph{all} reads, yet the vast majority of them is already assigned to a single species by the unrefined database, and such a read scores $q(r) = 1$ before and after a refinement. It cannot improve, but it does enter the average and thereby dilutes whatever happens to the reads that can. A read can only profit from the refinement if the unrefined database classified it correctly down to its genus but no further --- correct at that rank, yet not specific enough to name the species.

    We therefore report the same measure a second time, restricted to exactly these reads. Let $\kappa_u$ and $\kappa_f$ denote the assignment of the unrefined and the refined database and let $q_u$ and $q_f$ be the corresponding counts. With
\[ R_g = \{ r \in R : \text{lca}(\sigma(r), \kappa_u(r)) \text{ lies at or below } \text{genus}(\sigma(r)) \text{ and above } \sigma(r) \} , \]
    where $\text{lca}$ denotes the lowest ranked common ancestor of two nodes and $\text{genus}(\sigma(r))$ the genus the read's own species belongs to, as the \emph{genus-only subset}, the \emph{restricted precision} of a variant $v \in \{u, f\}$ is
\[ \mathit{prec}_g(v) = \frac{1}{|R_g|} \sum_{r \in R_g} q_v(r) . \]
    The condition is a window of ranks rather than the genus alone, because a taxonomy places further ranks inside it: NCBI's \emph{subgenus} and \emph{species group} both lie between a genus and its species, and a read left at either is left with several species in question exactly as one left at the genus is. Restricting the subset to the genus proper would drop those reads although a refinement acts on them --- for \texttt{tb}, whose six species-group nodes carry the Rickettsia spotted fever, typhus, canis, belli and phagocytophilum groups, that is 13 \% of the improvable reads. Nodes carrying no rank of their own are excluded, which leaves a residue the measure does not see; determining the window by position in the tree rather than by rank would close it.

    Note that $R_g$ is determined by the unrefined run and then kept fixed, so that both variants are scored on the very same reads and the difference $\mathit{prec}_g(f) - \mathit{prec}_g(u)$ states the gain where a gain was possible at all. Note also that the denominator is $|R_g|$ rather than the number of classified reads among them, which makes no difference here: every read of $R_g$ was classified by construction, since an unclassified read has no common ancestor with its true taxon to begin with.

    \subsubsection{Estimating the gain without ground truth}\label{groundtruthfree}

    Both measures of Section \ref{classmeasures} rest on $\sigma(r)$, the species a read actually stems from. A simulation supplies it, a real sample does not. Of a real fastq file we can therefore still ask how far a classification narrows the species down --- only not whether it does so correctly. Dropping precisely that test from $q$ leaves
\[ q'(r) = \frac{1}{|\Sigma(\kappa(r))|} \]
    for a classified read, and $q'(r) = 0$ for an unclassified one. Since $q(r)$ is $q'(r)$ multiplied by the indicator of $\sigma(r) \in \Sigma(\kappa(r))$, we have $q(r) \le q'(r)$ read by read, and hence for every average over reads as well. This needs no distributional assumption: $q'$ bounds $q$ pointwise.

    Dropping the test from $q$ is only half of what a real sample forces, however. The subset $R_g$ is no more observable than $q$ is: it is defined through $\sigma(r)$, and asking whether the unrefined assignment sits below \emph{the read's own} genus presupposes the very answer the analysis is after. What can be observed is where the classification came to rest, and nothing else. The ungated measure is therefore taken over
\[ R'_g = \{ r \in R : \kappa_u(r) \text{ lies at or below a genus and above any species} \} , \]
    the reads the unrefined database placed no further than a genus --- right or wrong --- which is decided by $\kappa_u(r)$ alone. Writing
\[ \mathit{prec}'_g(v) = \frac{1}{|R'_g|} \sum_{r \in R'_g} q'_v(r) \]
    gives what we call the \emph{ungated precision}, against $\mathit{prec}_g$ of Section \ref{classmeasures} for the gated one. Neither its increase under a refinement nor that of $\mathit{prec}_g$ is given a name or a symbol: both are read off the four averages the tables report, and naming them would add notation without adding a quantity. A prime marks the ground-truth-free counterpart throughout, and the primes travel together: $\mathit{prec}'_g$ differs from $\mathit{prec}_g$ in the summand \emph{and} in the subset, because it is exactly those two substitutions together that remove every appeal to $\sigma(r)$ and leave a quantity a real fastq file can supply. A primed symbol is thus never simply its unprimed one relaxed --- $R'_g$ in particular is neither a subset nor a superset of $R_g$, as the next paragraph shows.

    The two subsets are close, but neither contains the other, and they part company in two opposite ways. $R'_g$ \emph{loses} the reads whose unrefined assignment named a \emph{sibling} species: their lowest common ancestor with $\sigma(r)$ is the genus, which puts them in $R_g$, while $\kappa_u(r)$ itself sits at a species, which keeps them out of $R'_g$. These are precisely the reads of $R_g$ that score zero under $q$. $R'_g$ \emph{gains} the reads stopped at a genus that is not theirs at all, which no ground truth vouches for and which $R_g$ therefore excludes. On the simulated data below --- drawn from the very genomes the database was built from --- the first effect dominates and the second is negligible: $R'_g$ falls short of $R_g$ by between $0$ and $8.6$ percent, of which the reads it gains account for at most $0.4$. On a real sample, which contains organisms no reference covers, there is no reason to expect the second effect to stay negligible, and that is the substitution's price.

    The increase in $\mathit{prec}'_g$ is not a bound on the increase in $\mathit{prec}_g$ in either direction. The two are increases of averages taken over different summands and different subsets, so the pointwise $q \le q'$ says nothing about how they compare, which is why the calibration below is a pair of factors on the two levels rather than one factor on their difference.

    What $\mathit{prec}'_g$ registers is how much more specific the refined answers are, not whether that specificity is warranted: a refinement pushing every read onto an arbitrary single species would maximise it while destroying precision. It is informative here only because the refinement is conservative by construction --- a $k$-mer moves only onto a node whose subtree still contains every species whose genomes carry it (Section \ref{dbrefinement}) --- so that for a read whose matching $k$-mers all stem from its own genome $q_f(r) = q'_f(r)$. The two part company only where read errors produce matches that genome does not account for, and those reads can be counted: they are the reads of $R_g$ scoring zero under $q$, which for \texttt{cv} are 0.00 \%, 0.50 \% and 2.67 \% of it on the error-free, \enquote{HiSeq} and \enquote{MiSeq} sets --- absent without sequencing errors, and growing with them. They are also exactly the reads $R'_g$ omits; on every other read $q$ and $q'$ agree.

    The ratio of the two is what turns one into an estimate of the other. Writing
\[ \rho^u_{d,s} = \frac{\mathit{prec}_g(u)}{\mathit{prec}'_g(u)} , \qquad \rho^f_{d,s} = \frac{\mathit{prec}_g(f)}{\mathit{prec}'_g(f)} \]
    for the factors that turn an ungated precision into the gated one it stands for, both measured on the database $d$ and the simulated read set $s$. A real fastq file classified against the same database then yields the estimates $\rho^u_{d,s}\,\mathit{prec}'_g(u)$ and $\rho^f_{d,s}\,\mathit{prec}'_g(f)$ of a precision it cannot measure, and their difference is the estimated precision gain. That is what the whole construction is for.

    \section{Results}\label{results}

    Every measurement below compares a database against \emph{itself}: the same reads are classified twice, or the same database assessed twice, once before the refinement and once after it, with everything else held fixed. That is a deliberate choice of control, and since it means no other tool appears beside Genestrip anywhere in this section, it is worth saying why at the outset. A comparison across tools would vary the database and the classification algorithm along with the refinement, so a difference could no longer be attributed to the refinement alone --- whereas the comparison made here isolates it by construction. Nor is there a refined counterpart elsewhere to compare against: no other system performs a refinement, for reasons that differ from tool to tool and that Section \ref{discussion} sets out. Finally, the baseline is not an arbitrary one --- the first study on Genestrip established that it and KrakenUniq return identical $k$-mer and read counts on the same database \citep{genestrip-pub}, so the unrefined database is an externally corroborated reference point and what is measured against it carries over.

    \subsection{Classification quality}\label{classificationquality}

    The measures of Section \ref{intrinsicqms} assess a database as such. We now turn to what the refinement means for the analysis of actual reads, using simulated fastq files whose ground truth is known by construction. The measures themselves are those of Section \ref{classmeasures}, together with the estimate of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} that a sample \emph{without} known ground truth still admits; Sections \ref{simreads} and \ref{realreads} describe the simulated data and the real sequencing runs they are applied to.

%

    \subsubsection{Simulated read data}\label{simreads}

    Following the first study on Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub}, the simulated reads are produced with InSilicoSeq \citep{10.1093/bioinformatics/bty630}. Its two Illumina error models \enquote{MiSeq} and \enquote{HiSeq} are applied to \emph{all} RefSeq genomes of the category that the database under consideration draws from, yielding one pair of paired-end fastq files with one million reads per model. The models differ in read length as well as in error profile: InSilicoSeq emits 301 base pairs per mate for \enquote{MiSeq} and 126 for \enquote{HiSeq}.

    The ground truth follows from how InSilicoSeq names its reads: each read carries the accession of the fasta record it was drawn from, which Genestrip's accession map resolves to a taxon. A read whose accession cannot be resolved is reported as \emph{unresolved} and excluded from every count, in line with Section \ref{classmeasures}; a number substantially above zero would point at a mismatch between the reads and the accession map rather than at a property of the database, which is why we report it in Table \ref{simdata}.

    Since the reads are drawn from an entire RefSeq category, they need not fall within what a database was built to cover. We therefore count a read towards $R$ only if its true taxon lies at or below a taxon that the database explicitly requests. A read outside that scope can still be classified into it, and it then counts as a false positive, i.e.\ towards the classified reads but not towards $R$. Consequently the number of classified reads may exceed $|R|$, as it does for \texttt{tb} in Table \ref{simdata}. Where a database requests an entire category, as \texttt{cv} does, every read is in scope anyway and the restriction changes nothing.

    Besides \texttt{cv} we use \texttt{tb} from Table \ref{dbdesc} as the second database here. The two complement each other in the respect the refinement is about: \texttt{cv} is large and taxonomically broad, whereas \texttt{tb} is dense with close relatives --- twelve bacterial genera holding 1,120 taxa at the species rank and below --- which is the regime in which the LCA update strands the most $k$-mers above the species and a refinement therefore has the most to recover.

    Table \ref{genusonly} puts the two calibration factors of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} at every pair the simulations provide, and both are close to one throughout. On \texttt{cv} they run from $0.9729$ to $1.0000$ and from $0.9756$ to $1.0000$; on the error-free reads both are exactly one, because there $R'_g$ is $R_g$ and the two blocks coincide read for read. Two regularities hold across every pair reported here: neither factor exceeds one, and $\rho^f_{d,s}$ is the larger of the two. The first says an ungated precision never understates its gated counterpart, the second that the gap is narrower after a refinement than before it --- which is the sibling reads of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} being brought back into question, visible now as a systematic difference between two columns rather than as a single ratio crossing one.

    Neither regularity is automatic, and the first at least has an exact criterion. Write $Z$ for the reads $R'_g$ omits --- the sibling-named ones, which score zero under $q$ --- and $W$ for those it admits instead, placed at a genus that is not the read's own; the two subsets thus pull in opposite directions, $Z$ raising the ungated average by removing zeros and $W$ lowering it by adding near-zeros. With $A = \sum_{r \in R_g \cap R'_g} q_u(r)$ and $B = \sum_{r \in W} q'_u(r)$, a line of algebra gives
\[ \mathit{prec}_g(u) \le \mathit{prec}'_g(u) \iff \big(|W| - |Z|\big)\, A \le |R_g|\, B , \]
    and since $B \ge 0$ the condition $|W| \le |Z|$ is sufficient on its own. It is comfortably met here: $|Z|$ exceeds $|W|$ by between 15 and 53 times on every read set. That is not luck. Reads drawn from the reference genomes themselves are seldom assigned to a foreign genus, so $W$ stays in the tens, whereas sibling-named reads are exactly what sequencing error produces and $Z$ runs to thousands. A real sample carries no such guarantee --- its off-target content is bounded by nothing --- so the ordering should be read as a property of these simulations rather than as a law.

    On \texttt{tb} the eight tick simulations give $\rho^u_{\texttt{tb},s}$ between $0.9057$ and $0.9660$ and $\rho^f_{\texttt{tb},s}$ between $0.9258$ and $0.9703$, across $s = \text{tick}_1 \dots \text{tick}_8$, means $0.9232$ and $0.9443$. The departure from one is larger than on \texttt{cv}, and it comes from where one would expect: at a per-base error of $11.25$ \% the sibling-named reads that separate $R'_g$ from $R_g$ reach $3.3$ to $8.6$ percent of the subset, against $0$ to $2.6$ percent for the Illumina sets, so the two averages are taken over populations that agree less well. Even so the eight samples agree to within six percent among themselves --- a spread narrow enough for one pair of figures per database to be worth quoting, and wide enough that quoting one pair for \emph{all} databases would not be.

    Across all twelve pairs the factors lie between $0.9057$ and $1.0000$, and that is the result this section is after. Uncalibrated, an ungated precision overstates its gated counterpart by at most nine percent, and by under three percent on every \texttt{cv} set; multiplied by its factor it reproduces it exactly. Either way the construction of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} does what it was built for: a precision that can be \emph{measured} only where the truth is known can be \emph{estimated}, to two decimal places, where it is not --- which is the case for every real sample in Section \ref{realreads}.



    Table \ref{classquality} confirms empirically what Section \ref{classmeasures} argued on principle: the boolean counts hardly register the refinement. At the genus rank the $F_1$-score is unchanged to ten decimal places for \texttt{cv} under every one of the three read sets; at the species rank the largest change is $0.0001$. This is not a shortcoming of the refinement but of the measure --- a read moved from a genus to a node grouping three of its species has not reached its species, so the species count still scores it as a miss, and it has not left the genus subtree either, so the genus count scored it as a hit all along.

    The candidate-weighted measure, in contrast, does register it: precision rises by between $0.0080$ and $0.0097$ for \texttt{cv}, consistently across all three read sets. These figures are small in absolute terms, and Table \ref{genusonly} shows why. Only between 7.4\,\% and 10.3\,\% of the reads can be improved on at all --- for the remainder the assignment is either already at the species or wrong above the genus, and in neither case can a refinement change anything. Restricted to those reads, the same measure roughly \emph{doubles}: from $0.104$ to $0.208$ under \enquote{MiSeq} and from $0.106$ to $0.200$ under \enquote{HiSeq}, a gain of $0.104$ and $0.094$. In other words, the refinement halves the number of species left in question for a read that the unrefined database could not resolve.

    The ungated columns of the same table say what an analysis without ground truth would have seen: gains of $0.105$ and $0.094$ for the two Illumina models, against $0.106$ and $0.094$ measured against the truth. The estimate of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} would thus have been right to the third decimal place without knowing $\sigma(r)$ for a single read. How far that carries is read off the error-free row, where $R'_g = R_g$ and both factors are exactly one: over the range these three sets span, the estimate is as good as the measurement it stands in for.

    A small part of the gain even reaches the species rank: of the genus-only reads $R_g$, the refined database pins $0.15$\,\% down to a single species under \enquote{MiSeq}, where the unrefined one pins none by construction. The bulk of the effect, however, is the narrowing that the boolean counts cannot see.

    The subset is larger under \enquote{HiSeq} than under \enquote{MiSeq} --- $10.3$\,\% against $7.4$\,\%. The two models differ in read length and in error profile at once, so this contrast alone cannot attribute the effect to either: a shorter read carries fewer $k$-mers and a more error-prone one carries fewer \emph{matching} $k$-mers, and both make a classification come to rest higher up in the taxonomy. The error-free runs separate the two, since InSilicoSeq's \enquote{perfect} mode keeps the read length and introduces no errors at all; they also bound what the refinement can achieve when nothing but the taxonomy limits the classification.

    \subsubsection{Real read data}\label{realreads}

    The measure of Section \ref{groundtruthfree} exists so that a refinement can be assessed on data that no simulation produced. We therefore apply it to the two collections of real sequencing data of the first study on Genestrip \citep{genestrip-pub}, so that the databases and the samples are the ones that study already characterised.

    For \texttt{cv} these are the runs from DNA sequencing human saliva, deposited in the Sequence Read Archive \citep{10.1093/nar/gkab1053}: deep metagenomic paired-end data of 605 to 981 million read pairs each, which the first study used to show that a database of narrow coverage provokes false positives that a comprehensive one avoids. For \texttt{tb} these are the Nanopore runs of the tick surveillance study \citep{nanoporeticks} --- the very samples the simulated reads of Section \ref{simreads} are modelled on, so that the simulated and the real case can be read against each other for one and the same database.

    Neither collection admits the measures of Section \ref{classmeasures}: the organisms in a saliva sample or a field-collected tick are what the analysis is meant to find out, so there is no $\sigma(r)$ to score against. What Table \ref{realgain} reports is therefore the two ungated precisions and the size of the observable subset they are averaged over, together with the estimated gated precisions $\rho^u_{d,s}\,\mathit{prec}'_g(u)$ and $\rho^f_{d,s}\,\mathit{prec}'_g(f)$ that follow from the calibration of Table \ref{genusonly}; their difference is the estimated precision gain. The reads carry their own error rate in their quality strings, which is what selects the read set $s$ whose calibration is borrowed.

    The first of the saliva runs has been measured once, and has to be measured again: that run predates the correction of the genus-only subset set out in Section \ref{classmeasures}, and \texttt{cv} carries 77 subgenus nodes and some thirteen thousand unranked ones, every one of which the earlier subset passed over. What it did establish is the scale. Of the 981 million reads the unrefined database classifies just under two percent, and of those only about one in a thousand comes to rest at a genus --- so the measure rests on a few tens of thousands of reads out of a billion, and the table reports $|R'_g|$ beside the precisions for that reason.

    The gain came out below the $0.094$ to $0.105$ the simulated reads of Table \ref{genusonly} show, and the reason is visible in the reads themselves: measured from their quality strings they carry a mean per-base error of $2.07$\,\% at 101 base pairs, three times the error of the most error-prone simulated set. The subset they can improve is correspondingly small.

    It also puts the sample outside the range of error rates the stock InSilicoSeq models span, which is $0.01$\,\% to $0.73$\,\%. That is why Table \ref{simdata} carries a fourth \texttt{cv} read set: rather than extrapolate the factors beyond their calibration, the \enquote{saliva-like} set reproduces the runs' own parameters --- 101 base pairs at $1.9953$\,\% --- so that the factor applied in Table \ref{realgain} is one measured at the error rate it is used at. The saliva runs are Illumina HiSeq 2000, so the alternative would have been to borrow the \enquote{HiSeq} factors on the strength of the platform name; that the stock \enquote{HiSeq} model is an order of magnitude cleaner than the instrument is exactly the trap this avoids.

    \subsection{Performance}\label{performance}

All experiments reported in Section \ref{results} were performed on a machine with \sysRamGB{} GB of RAM, \sysCores{} CPU cores and a \sysDiskSize{} \sysDiskType{} disk.\footnote{We ensured that as few competing processes as possible were running during the experiments, and OS disk swapping was \sysSwap.     \subsubsection{Database refinement}\label{refinementperformance}

Since a refined database is always generated \emph{on top of} an existing, unrefined one, the relevant cost of the refinement is its \emph{additional} resource consumption. Table \ref{ftdbperf} therefore reports the wall time and the maximum RAM of the unrefined database generation in absolute terms, but the corresponding figures of the subsequent refinement relative to them.

As can be seen, the refinement typically costs less than the generation of the unrefined database it builds upon: for \texttt{protozoa} and \texttt{parasites} it stays below two thirds of the wall time, for \texttt{tb} below 85 \% and for \texttt{vineyard} still below 100 \%. The database \texttt{cv} forms an exception with 184 \%, i.e. the refinement takes almost twice as long as the original database generation. This is because the refinement's runtime is essentially driven by the number of $k$-mers residing on refinement ranks and by the number of subtrees each of them must be tested against, whereas the generation time of the unrefined database is dominated by reading and hashing the underlying genomic sequences. With 14,988 species and below (see Table \ref{dbdesc}), \texttt{cv} is by far the most species-rich database considered here, so that testing a single $k$-mer becomes correspondingly expensive.
Regarding main memory, the refinement stays below the unrefined database generation for the larger databases, since it does not need to hold the sequence-related data structures of the latter. Only for \texttt{vineyard}, the smallest database in our collection, it exceeds them slightly with 111 \%, because the refinement must keep the $k$-mer store and the Bloom filter of the goal \verb|kmerindexbloom| in memory at the same time, which for such a small database outweighs what its generation ever allocated.
Most notably, the refinement is almost for free in terms of disk space: a refined database is at most 2.5 \% larger than the corresponding unrefined one. This is because the refinement does not add any $k$-mers to the database --- it only reassigns them and adds a comparatively small number of new tree nodes.

    \section{Conclusions}\label{discussion}

% TODO (added 2026-08-02): argument recorded for later polishing.
A further reason why a refinement does not carry over to Kraken 1 or KrakenUniq is more fundamental than the effort of the refinement itself: the \emph{lossless} broad coverage database that it would have to build upon can hardly be generated in the first place. Already in 2018, producing a database for Kraken 1 --- and thus equivalently for KrakenUniq --- that merely covers the bacterial genomes from RefSeq \enquote{required ca. 2.5 TB of RAM and ca. 11 days} on a suitable computer \citep{Nasko:2018aa}, and RefSeq has expanded considerably ever since, as laid out in Section \ref{problem}. The first study on Genestrip therefore concluded that $k$-mer databases with very broad and comprehensive coverage have reached a \enquote{dead-end} when combined with a lossless storing of $k$-mers \citep{genestrip-pub}. A refinement inherits this verdict, since it presupposes precisely such a lossless database, as we detail at the end of this section. On top of that, it would add its own effort, which is essentially governed by the number of $k$-mers residing on refinement ranks \emph{multiplied by} the number of subtrees each of them must be tested against. Both factors grow with the taxonomic breadth of the reference set, and our own measurements already reflect this: for \texttt{cv}, by far the most species-rich database considered here with 14,988 species, the refinement takes almost twice as long as the generation of the unrefined database, whereas it stays well below that for all smaller ones (Table \ref{ftdbperf}). Kraken 2 and Ganon do evade this memory barrier --- but only by compressing $k$-mers, which forfeits the very property a refinement relies on. Taken together, the two options thus close in on each other: stored losslessly, a broad coverage database can barely be built, and compressed, it can no longer be refined.

Simply shrinking such a database is not a way out either, and this is where Genestrip's particular design becomes essential. As the first study on Genestrip has shown, a database restricted to just the genomes of the organisms of interest provokes many false positives: a large fraction of a species' $k$-mers are unspecific in the sense that they also occur in genomes of organisms \emph{not} contained in the database, so that a read composed of such $k$-mers is falsely attributed to a species that happens to be present \citep{genestrip-pub}. Genestrip escapes this dilemma because its LCA update is \emph{comprehensive}: it takes all taxa and associated genomes under the relevant RefSeq categories into account, not merely those whose $k$-mers are eventually stored (see Section \ref{dbrefinement} and the placeholder \enquote{OTHER} in Figure \ref{ftother}). A Genestrip database may therefore remain small \emph{and} precise at the same time --- and it is exactly this combination that renders a refinement computationally affordable to begin with.

Finally, a refinement presupposes that a database can still say, per $k$-mer, which species carry it. Genestrip stores its $k$-mers uncompressed and individually identifiable, so it can \citep{genestrip-pub}, and the price for keeping them that way is low: the refinement itself increases the size of a database by at most 2.5 \% (see Table \ref{ftdbperf}). The other systems stand in different relations to that requirement, and Table \ref{othertools} separates them rather than filing them all under \enquote{compression}. Three kinds of obstacle appear. For the filter-based indices it is one of principle --- there is no per-$k$-mer entry to carry a taxon, so a refinement has no object to act on. For Kraken 2 it is one of collision risk and resolution: a refinement is conceivable at build time, but the stored representation cannot always tell two $k$-mers apart, and pushing a merged entry downwards would produce exactly the false specificity that an LCA update exists to prevent. For a third group the question simply does not arise, because they never collapse a $k$-mer to a single taxon in the first place and so have nothing to recover --- at the price of a database that grows with the number of species carrying each $k$-mer, which is the very cost the LCA update avoids and a refinement pays back only in part.

\subsection{Path precision and path recall}\label{apppathprecision}

Section \ref{pathcorrectness} states path correctness as a boolean property and Section \ref{nodeprecision} assesses refinement quality per node. Both can equivalently be expressed by counting $k$-mers per data taxon, which yields the more conventional pair of precision and recall. We develop it here for reference and to relate it to the measures used in the main text.

Let $t$ be a data taxon of a database $d$, as defined in Section \ref{pathcorrectness}. The following counts assess the quality of the taxonomic refinement per data taxon $t$ in $d$:
\begin{description}
    \item \textbf{Positive counts $P$ per data taxon $t$ in database $d$:} The number of unique $k$-mers in $d$ that occur in the genome associated with $t$.

    \item \textbf{True positive counts $TP$ per data taxon $t$ in database $d$:} The number of unique $k$-mers in $d$ that occur in the genome associated with $t$ where in $d$, such $k$-mers are associated with some taxon on the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root including $t$ itself.

    \item \textbf{True positive plus false positive counts $TP + FP$ per data taxon $t$ in database $d$:} The number of unique $k$-mers in $d$ assigned to any taxon on the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root including $t$ itself.
\end{description}

   These counts allow for defining \emph{path precision} as $TP / (TP + FP)$ and \emph{path recall} as $TP / P$.

   As an example, consider Figure \ref{ftsketch} from above: In (3), the path precision of $Z$ is $2/3$ because there are three $2$-mers on the path from $Z$ to the root $W$ but one of them, \texttt{CT}, is not in $W$'s sequence. In (3), $X$ and $Y$ both have a path precision of $1$. The refined database in (4) has a precision of 1 for $X$, $Y$ \emph{and} $Z$. Path recall is $1$ for all of the cases in (3) and (4).

    Path recall is the counting counterpart of path correctness: a $k$-mer $a$ that occurs in the genome of $t$ contributes to $P$ in any case, but to $TP$ only if $\nu(a)$ lies on the path from $t$ to the root. Hence $TP = P$ for a data taxon $t$ exactly if the condition of Section \ref{pathcorrectness} holds for every $k$-mer supported by $t$, and \emph{a database is path correct if and only if its path recall equals one for every data taxon}. Since both the LCA update and the refinement establish that condition by construction, path recall carries no information beyond the boolean statement, which is why the main text uses the latter.

    Path precision, in contrast, is a genuine quality measure and an alternative to node precision. We report unweighted\footnote{The unweighted average considers every data taxon as equally relevant.} average path precision by averaging over all data taxa in $d$ or subsets thereof, in particular over all taxa under the same species or the same genus. E.g., in (3) of Figure \ref{ftsketch}, the average path precision of the genus $W$ is 8/9.

    The counts of a data taxon $t$ above refer to the entire path from $t$ to the taxonomic root. As for node precision, it is useful to relativize them to a subtree: let $m$ be a node of $d$'s taxonomy tree and let $D_m$ be the set of data taxa under $m$ including $m$ itself. For a data taxon $t \in D_m$, let $TP_m(t)$ and $(TP + FP)_m(t)$ be defined as above, but with respect to the path from $t$ to $m$ -- including $t$ and $m$ themselves -- instead of the path from $t$ to the taxonomic root. Besides the unweighted average, we then consider the \emph{weighted} average path precision of the subtree rooted at $m$,\footnote{We deliberately refrain from smoothing $\overline{pp}_w(m)$ -- or $np(n)$ from Section \ref{nodeprecision} -- by a Laplace correction, i.e.\ from adding a virtual $k$-mer per node that counts as a true positive by definition. Such a correction contributes the same amount to numerator and denominator, and since the ratio is at most one, every contribution of this kind strictly raises it -- without a single $k$-mer of $d$ supporting the increase. Its total size grows with the number of nodes of the subtree, and the refinement is precisely a procedure that \emph{adds} nodes to the taxonomy. A corrected measure would therefore reward a refined tree over an unrefined one for its mere node count, and it would do so the more the deeper the refined subtrees become, i.e.\ exactly along the axis the measure is meant to assess.}
\[ \overline{pp}_w(m) = \frac{\sum_{t \in D_m} TP_m(t)}{\sum_{t \in D_m} (TP + FP)_m(t)} , \]
    which relates the pooled counts instead of averaging the per-taxon ratios. For $m$ being the taxonomic root, it ranges over all data taxa of $d$ and hence assesses the database as a whole. The measure is undefined if no $k$-mer at all resides in the subtree rooted at $m$.

    Note that for a taxonomy tree with a branching factor $\geq 2$ (which is the standard case) a high average path precision ensures that a $k$-mer $a$ in $d$ is located as closely as possible above a data taxon or right at a data taxon whose genome(s) contain $a$. Otherwise, if $a$ was located higher up in the taxonomy, then $a$ would also be on the path to the root starting from a data taxon $t$ whose genome does not contain $a$. But this would increase the $FP$-count of $t$ and in turn would lower the average path precision.

    Grouping the pooled counts by the node holding each $k$-mer rather than by data taxon shows how $\overline{pp}_w(m)$ relates to the subtree precision $sp(m)$ of Section \ref{nodeprecision}. Both average the per-$k$-mer precision $p(a)$ over the $k$-mers of the subtree, but $\overline{pp}_w(m)$ weights each $k$-mer by $|D_{\nu(a)}|$, i.e.\ by the number of data taxa its node covers, whereas $sp(m)$ weights all $k$-mers equally. Path precision therefore lets $k$-mers sitting high in the taxonomy dominate the value, while subtree precision does not, which is the reason the main text builds on the latter.
