% Enter abstract:
The consumption of main memory resources is a significant burden in modern metagenomic analysis when performing read classification and (unique) $k$-mer-counting. Genestrip addresses this issue by focusing on small but freely configurable groups of organisms. Regarding the selected organisms, it produces databases and results comparable to those of Kraken 1 and KrakenUniq but at a fraction of their required memory resources. Thus, database creation and read classification may be performed even on small-sized PCs or edge machines. Moreover, Genestrip can be used to extremely reduce the size of sequence files for downstream steps without loss of relevant data regarding the selected organisms. 

	We assess the correctness, performance and usefulness of Genestrip in different contexts and show that it ascertains high read classification accuracy even though a corresponding database only covers the genomes of the selected organisms. Due to Genestrip's particular way of updating the $k$-mers' least common ancestors stored in a database, both database production and its use can be realized with little memory. This empowers users to flexibly create and apply such databases potentially with a very deep genomic coverage for the selected organisms.

\section{Background}\label{background}

To analyze large sets of reads, researchers use software tools based on $k$-mer matching and counting such as Kraken 1 \citep{kraken}, Kraken 2 \citep{kraken2} or KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq}. With regard to pathogen detection KrakenUniq is particularly suited because of its very low false positive rate but also because its efficiency, precision and sensitivity \citep{krakenUniq}.

In order to avoid false positive classifications of reads or mismatches of $k$-mers, all of these tools resort to very large databases, containing millions of encoded $k$-mers along with their respective taxons. A database is usually loaded entirely into main memory and consumes tens of gigabytes of space \footnote{See \url{https://benlangmead.github.io/aws-indexes/k2} for related Databases}. This requirement mandates specialized and expensive compute servers with very large main memory.

In recognition of this problem, KrakenUniq has been extended for database processing in chunks \citep{lowmemkraken}. However, database creation still mandates a very large main memory. This may force users to resort to ready-made standard database even though they do not cover an organism of interest at the necessary genomic depth although respective genomes are potentially available for example in Genbank \citep{genbank}.

If only a smaller group of a few 100 species or strains is under consideration, then it seems promising to simply \emph{generate a smaller database that contains just the $k$-mers from respective genomes in depth}. \emph{However, this very likely leads to many false positives} even when using KrakenUniq. The reason for this is that due to the evolutionary relationship of organisms, \emph{a large fraction of $k$-mers that belong to the genome of a species are unspecific} in a sense that they can as well be found in the genome other species not considered for analysis. Thus, when using too small a database, a read may be falsely assigned to a species because it consists of $k$-mers that \emph{also} belong to another species' genome that was not included in the database.
 
In order to enable a smaller database with no false positives emerging from this problem, Genestrip employs a \emph{two-phase} database generation process: During the \emph{entry phase}, all $k$-mers of genomes from selected organsisms are entered into the database on the basis of the RefSeq \citep{refseq} and optionally GenBank \citep{genbank}. During the \emph{update phase}, all stored $k$-mers from the database are checked against the genomes of all other organisms from the RefSeq belonging to the same (super-)kingdom. If a stored $k$-mer also occurs in another organism which is not part of the database, the $k$-mer's taxonomic identifier gets updated in the database and the least common ancestor's taxonomic identifier is entered instead. This prevents $k$-mers from being associated with one of selected organisms even though they are unspecific.

Based on this approach, Genestrip achieves low memory footprint during database creation as well as at analysis time for reads. Moreover, it offers competitive $k$-mer matching and read classification performance. Together, these qualities empower users to create and use respective databases on machines with regular main memory size. 

Genestrip's $k$-mer counting results are similar to those of KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq} with regard to the selected organisms. Much as KrakenUniq, Genestrip supports unique $k$-mer counting with exact counts but with low memory overhead.

In contrast to Genestrip, databases for KrakenUniq or Kraken 1 offer but also require large databases in order avoid false positives with regard to unspecific $k$-mers. E.g., a bacterial KrakenUniq database that covers the RefSeq on the assembly quality level "complete genome" has a disk size of ?? GB \cite{?}. 
In order to produce this database, a large memory machine must with almost 1TB of memory must be provided. Nevertheless, the database is restricted to a just an extract of RefSeq's bacterial genomes containing no scaffolds and no non-standard genomes. Unfortunately, this may affect the discovery of an organisms a user may be particularly interested in as deep genomic coverage even with additional genomes from Genbank and other source is often needed for this purpose \cite{?}.

Note that also Kraken 2 is no suitable alternative in fields such as medical diagnostics where classification accuracy is paramount: Although Kraken 2 produces relatively smaller databases, it compresses stored $k$-mers under loss of information therefore potentially permitting false positive $k$-mer counts \cite{?}. 

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{System Structure}

Genestrip's database generation mandates the setting of groups of organisms whose genomes will be exclusively represented in a resulting database. Users choose desired genuses, species or strains via a configuration file by listing respective taxonomic identifiers. If higher ranked taxonomic identifiers are entered, then \emph{all} of the descending organisms will be included and $k$-mers of their genomes will be added to the database accordingly. 

Genestrip generates a database for a selected group of organisms primarily via 
genomes from the RefSeq \citep{refseq} but genomes from Genbank or own genomic files may be included on top.
To better handle the complexity of the generation process, we employ a goal-oriented approach in similarity to Make \citep{feldman1979make}: Upon access, each goal returns a file or an object in main memory. A goal is only executed if its result is not yet available. Goal execution may involve the access of other goals which turn, may trigger their execution. Respective goal dependencies are explicitly specified in terms of a directed acyclic graph. 

Figure \ref{f:goalgraph} depicts an extract of Genestrip's goal graph: Several goals (prefixed with \verb|d:|) revolve around downloading files from the RefSeq, Genbank and other data from the NCBI. Several object goals (prefixed with \verb|o:|) load files and represent them as data structures in main memory for further processing. Most file goals (prefixed with \verb|f:|) write computational results to files.
Boxes with edged corners depict goals that can be executed directly via the command line. E.g., 
the goals \verb|match| and \verb|matchlr| are for fastq file analysis but they would trigger a respective database generation if the database file was missing.

\subsection{Database and Filter Generation}

Genestrip stores a database primarily as a large array \verb|S| of sorted long values. Each long value encodes a $k$-mer from a reference genome of a selected organism. The default for $k$ is $31$ but other values are possible. A second array of the same size as \verb|S| encodes taxonomy identifiers for each $k$-mer in \verb|S|. During fastq file analysis, lookups in \verb|S| are performed via a binary search.

At analysis time, a bit vector with same size as \verb|S| indicates which $k$-mers from \verb|S| were found at least once. This is sufficient for exact unique $k$-mer counting and is space-efficient, as it requires only about 1.6\% of additional memory with respect to \verb|S|.
An optional Bloom filter \citep{bloomfilter} also contains all stored $k$-mers. It is used by default at analysis time to check whether a certain $k$-mer is stored in \verb|S|. A negative result avoids a more expensive binary search in \verb|S| and therefore saves overall execution cost. 
Moreover, the parts of the NCBI taxonomy tree \citep{taxonomy} required at analysis time are included in 
the database. The taxonomy extract comprises entries for the selected organisms and all of its ancestors.

Besides $k$-mer matching and read classification, Genestrip also supports filtering of fastq files via a Bloom filter in similarity to \citep{biobloom}. When generated via the goal \verb|index|, the Bloom filter gets stored in separate file. The goal \verb|filter| loads the filter and processes fastq files accordingly. The standard configuration for the filtering process rewrites a read to the output if the read contains at least one $k$-mer that is recognized by the filter and thus, the read may belong to a selected organism.

\subsection{Analysis Reports for Reads} \label{measures}

Similarly $u_{t,d}$ is the number of (unique) $k$-mers for $t$ stored in $d$.

Table \ref{csvcolumns} describes the most important reported columns. Additional columns exist to  accumulate $k$-mer counts and read counts etc. upwards to higher ranks in the taxonomy. Notably, Genestrip introduces a novel, derived statistical measure -- the \emph{$k$-mer consistency ratio} as explained in the following: Given $k_{t,f,d}$, the a-priori probability $p(t\ |\ k_{t,f,d})$ of a unique $k$-mer from $d$ belonging to $t$ to occur \emph{at least once} in $f$\ is approximately:\footnote{The approximation is based on the simplifying assumption that all $k$-mers in $d$ belonging to $t$ are equally likely to be found in $f$ \cite{ticks}. Still, this may be countered by special DNA library preparation protocols like 16S amplification \cite{?}.}
\[ p(t\ |\ k_{t,f,d}) = 1 - (1 - 1/u_{t,d})^{k_{t,f,d}}\] 

The corresponding \emph{expected number of unique $k$-mers} $E$ is (\cite{Mendelson2016WhatIT}):
\[ E(k_{t,f,d},u_{t,d}) = u_{t,d} \cdot p(t\ |\ k_{t,f,d})\]
It is useful to compare $E$ with the actual count $u_{t,f,d}$: If these quantities divert too much from each other, then the consistency of the $k$-mer counting results for $t$ in $f$ should be questioned. If, on the other hand, these quantities are close, then this supports the consistency of the counting results. This justifies the following ratio as the \emph{$k$-mer consistency score}, which ideally is close to $1$:\footnote{A mathematically sounder approach is to consider the probability distribution 
$q(u_{t,f,d}\ |\ k_{t,f,d})$, i.e. the probability to obtain exactly $u_{t,f,d}$ unique $k$-mers for a given (non-unique) $k$-mer count of $k_{t,f,d}$ with  sampling with replacement as the underlying random process.
Interestingly, this distribution is non-trivial and has been investigated in \cite{Mendelson2016WhatIT}. The authors have shown that the distribution can be approximated by a normal distribution under most practically relevant conditions. Moreover, they confirm the expectation $E$ from above and give a formula for the variance $\sigma^2_q$ which in our case depends on $k_{t,f,d}$ and $u_{t,d}$. 
So, as an alternative consistency score one could set $r'(k_{t,f,d}, u_{t,f,d}) := f(u_{t,f,d}) / f(E)$,
where $f$ is the density of the normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(E,\sigma^2_q)$.
This relative density $r'$ ranges between $0$ and $1$ where $1$ is best and implies $u_{t,f,d} = E$.
Although mathematically sound, $r'$ did not appear useful with respect to $k$-mer counts because $\mathcal{N}(E,\sigma^2_q)$ tends to be extremely peaked and so, even small deviations from $E$ result in a $r' \approx 0$.}

%Expectation accounting for duplicate kmers in DB:
%Let $k_{t,d}(i)$ be the number of times a unique kmer $i$ occurs in the genome of $t$ with $1 \leq i \leq u_{t,d}$ and $\vec{k}_{t,d}$ the corresponding vector:
%\[ E_q(k_{t,f,d},\vec{k}_{t,d}) = \sum_{i = 1}^{u_{t,d}} k_{t,d}(i) \cdot (1 - (1 - 1/k_{t,d}(i))^{k_{t,f,d}}) \]
\[ r(k_{t,f,d}, u_{t,f,d}) := u_{t,f,d} / E(k_{t,f,d},u_{t,d})\]

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Correctness and Accuracy}

To assess Genestrips's correctness with regard to $k$-mer counting and read classification, we compared its databases and respective analysis results with those from KrakenUniq.

As a first test, we created database contents for both systems that were as identical as possible in terms and their genomic basis and their stored $k$-mers. 
We then applied the created databases to a suitable fastq file in order to compare the results between both systems. Highly similar results from this check mutually solidify the correctness of both systems since they had been developed completely independently.

In this context, we chose all RefSeq genomes from the viral super-kingdom on the assembly level "complete genome" (RefSeq Release 229). Figure \ref{viraldbscatter} compares the structure of the resulting two databases from KrakenUniq and Genestrip, respectively: They are almost perfectly identical with regard to the number of $k$-mers stored per taxonomic identifier with 15 genomes and respective taxonomic identifiers occurring in KrakenUniq but not Genestrip and 33 genomes vice versa.\footnote{The differences between the sets selected genomes are caused by different approaches on how the genomes are preselected and downloaded by KrakenUniq and Genestrip, respectively. They do not constitute erroneous behavior of either system.} The slightly differing sets of genomes result in 158 differences in $k$-mers stored per taxonomic identifier due to effects of least common ancestor updates for stored $k$-mers.

\subsection{Relevance of the Update Phase}

In order to validate the relevance of Genestrip's update phase we compared the contents of variants of the same Genestrip database. (See Section \ref{background} and Table \ref{goals} for background on the update phase.) In particular, we assessed, how the number of $k$-mers associated with each taxonomic identifier changes depending on the spectrum of genomes considered for the least common ancestor update. The database variants differed by having either
\begin{enumerate}
	\item no update phase (but just the entry phase), \label{noupdate}
	\item an update phase based \emph{exclusively} on the genomes from the entry phase, \label{minupdate}
	\item an update phase based on \emph{all} of the genomes of the corresponding taxonomic (super-)kingdom from the RefSeq.\label{completeupdate} 
\end{enumerate}
Table \ref{updaterelevance} documents respective results for 4 different databases: Perhaps not surprisingly, the average percentage of $k$-mers that moved to higher taxonomic ranks is large when comparing variant \ref{noupdate} against variant \ref{minupdate} (see Column ($\bullet$)). However, in three out of four databases, a considerable amount of $k$-mers was also moved up when comparing variant \ref{minupdate} against variant \ref{completeupdate} (see Column($\circ$)). This indicates the importance of the update phase according to variant \ref{completeupdate} as otherwise, $k$-mer matches may lead to false positives at analysis time.

Figure \ref{tickchangekmers1} compares the results of fastq file analyses based on the \enquote{Tick Borne} database using variant \ref{minupdate} versus variant \ref{completeupdate}. We used the fastq files from \cite{nanoporeticks} and show the results for Tick 3 and Tick 6 as representative examples. The considerable changes in unique $k$-mer counts confirm that variant \ref{minupdate} is inferior for reliable $k$-mer counting due to false positives. Table \ref{topresultsticks} shows how the top ten hits under variant \ref{completeupdate} have changed under \ref{minupdate}: Major differences of unique $k$-mer counts and of resulting frequency ranks solidify the design choice for Genestrip to employ an update phase according to \ref{completeupdate} as its default. Noteworthy, the results from Table \ref{topresultsticks} are also in line with the findings from \cite{nanoporeticks}.

\subsection{Abundance Estimation via the Normalized $k$-mer Count}

We assess the quality of an abundance estimation via the 
normalized $k$-mer count $k'_{t,f,d}$ from Section \ref{measures}.
Our assessment is based on three well-defined, sequenced mock community datasets named \enquote{PB\_Zymo}, \enquote{ONT\_Zymo} and \enquote{PB\_ATCC} as used and described in \cite{classifier_eval}.
The datasets' real species abundance is known and stated by corresponding producers. To determine the relative estimated abundance $A_t$ with Genestrip, we compute
\[ A_{t,f,d} = k'_{t,f,d} / \sum_{s \in S_d} k'_{s,f,d} \]
where $S_d$ is the list of all species-level taxonomic identifiers in the respective Genestrip database $d$.

We created three Genestrip databases where each contains just the taxonomic identifiers of all bacterial species as present in a corresponding dataset from above. While this is not a realistic scenario (as ground truth is partially used with respect to the present species), it still allows for a quality assessment of the abundance estimation itself. Note that successful abundance estimation depends on both, the use of species-level $k$-mer counting for this purpose \emph{and} the use of $k'_{t,f,d}$ as a suitable abundance measure. Regarding a dataset (i.e. fastq file) $f$ and a database $d$, we use the averaged relative distance to the real abundances on the species level as the error quantification:
\[ err_{f,d} := 1 / | S_d | \cdot \sum_{t \in S_d} |a_{f,t} -  A_{t,f,d}| / a_{f,t} \]
with $a_{f,t}$ being the true relative abundance of $t$ in $f$.
