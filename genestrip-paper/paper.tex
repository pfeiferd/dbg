% Enter abstract:
The consumption of main memory resources is a significant burden in modern metagenomic analysis when creating analysis databases as well as performing read classification and (unique) $k$-mer-counting. Genestrip addresses this issue by focusing on small but freely configurable groups of organisms. Regarding the selected organisms, it produces databases and results comparable to those of KrakenUniq but at a fraction of its required memory resources. So, both database creation and read classification may be performed even on small-sized PCs or edge machines. Moreover, Genestrip can be used to extremely reduce the size of sequence files for downstream steps without loss of relevant data regarding the selected organisms. 

	We assess the correctness, usefulness and performance of Genestrip in different contexts and show that it ascertains high read classification accuracy even though a corresponding database only covers the genomes of the selected organisms. Due to Genestrip's particular way of updating the $k$-mers' lowest common ancestors stored in a database, both database creation and its use can be realized with little memory. This empowers users to flexibly create and apply such databases potentially with a very deep genomic coverage for the chosen organisms.

\section{Background}\label{background}

With the availability of low cost and small-scale sequencing devices, metagenomic analysis will likely replace many established technologies for the detection and surveillance of specific groups of microorganisms. Related applications may affect various areas such as farming, ecology, food processing and medicine. While sequencing devices have become ready for ubiquitous use, software tools for metagenomics still tend to be resource intensive and too hard to use for end users.

To analyze large sets of reads, software tools based on $k$-mer matching and counting such as Kraken 1 \citep{kraken}, Kraken 2 \citep{kraken2} or KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq} are at hand. With regard to pathogen detection, KrakenUniq is particularly suited because of its very low false positive rate but also because its efficiency and high sensitivity \citep{krakenUniq}.

In order to avoid false positive classifications of reads or mismatches of $k$-mers, all of these tools resort to very large databases, containing millions of encoded $k$-mers along with their respective \taxon. A database is usually loaded entirely into main memory and consumes tens of gigabytes of space.\footnote{See \url{https://benlangmead.github.io/aws-indexes/k2} for related Databases.} This requirement mandates specialized and expensive compute servers with very large main memory.

In recognition of this problem, KrakenUniq has been extended for database processing in chunks at read analysis time \citep{lowmemkraken}. However, database creation still mandates a very large main memory: E.g., a "standard database" for KrakenUniq that just covers the standard genomes of archaea, bacteria, viruses and the human genome from the RefSeq \citep{refseq} requires over 370 GB of main memory to be produced \citep{lowmemkraken}. Still related organisms are often not covered below the species rank, whereby genomes below the quality level "complete genome" are not even included. 

Despite the fact that these databases may not cover organisms of interest at the necessary genomic depth, users tend to apply them because otherwise, creating even larger databases would require even more main memory. So, although a much richer set of genomes for organisms interest may be available in principle -- e.g. via Genbank \citep{genbank} -- these genomes cannot easily be leveraged for metagenomic analysis.

Although Kraken 2 delivers more memory efficient databases, it is not a suitable alternative in fields such as medical diagnostics where read classification accuracy is paramount \citep{lowmemkraken}. Kraken 2 produces smaller databases than KrakenUniq but it compresses stored $k$-mers under loss of information, therefore enabling false positive $k$-mer counts \citep{kraken2, lowmemkraken}. 

If only a smaller group of a few 100 species or strains is under consideration, then it seems promising to simply \emph{generate a smaller database that contains just the $k$-mers from respective genomes in depth}. \emph{However, this very likely leads to many false positives}: The reason for this is that due to the evolutionary relationship of organisms, \emph{a large fraction of $k$-mers that belong to the genome of a species are unspecific} in a sense that they can as well be found in the genome other species not considered for analysis. Thus, when using too small a database, a read may be falsely assigned to a species because it consists of $k$-mers that \emph{also} belong to another species' genome that was not included in the database.
  
In order to enable a smaller database with no false positives emerging from this problem, we introduce Genestrip, as new metagenomic software tool based on $k$-mer matching and filtering.

Genestrip employs a \emph{two-phase} database generation process: During the \emph{entry phase}, all $k$-mers of genomes from selected organisms are entered into the database on the basis of the RefSeq \citep{refseq} and optionally GenBank \citep{genbank}. During the \emph{update phase}, all stored $k$-mers from the database are checked against the genomes of \emph{all} other organisms from the RefSeq belonging to the same (super-)kingdom. So, if a stored $k$-mer also occurs in another organism which is not part of the database, the $k$-mer's \taxon\ still gets updated in the database and the lowest common ancestor's \taxon\ is entered instead. This prevents a $k$-mer from being falsely associated with one of selected organisms if it is too unspecific for that.

Based on this approach, Genestrip achieves low memory footprint during database creation as well as at read analysis time. Moreover, it offers reasonable $k$-mer matching and read classification performance. Together, these qualities empower users to create and use respective databases on machines with regular main memory size. Genestrip's $k$-mer counting results are similar to those of KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq} with regard to the selected organisms. Genestrip also supports unique $k$-mer counting with exact counts and with low memory overhead. 

Genestrip is based on Java, has a small code base and supports command-line-based as well as API-based usage. It can be executed on all modern operating systems.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{System Structure}

Genestrip's database generation mandates the setting of groups of organisms whose genomes will be exclusively represented in a resulting database. Users choose desired genuses, species or strains via a configuration file by listing respective \taxon s. If higher ranked \taxon s are entered, then by default, \emph{all} of the descending \taxon s will be included and $k$-mers of their genomes will be added to the database accordingly. 

Genestrip generates a database for a selected group of organisms primarily via 
genomes from the RefSeq \citep{refseq} but genomes from Genbank and additional genomic files may be included on top or instead.
To better handle the complexity of the generation process, it employs a goal-oriented approach in similarity to Make \citep{feldman1979make}: Upon access, each goal returns a file or an object in main memory. A goal is only executed if its result is not yet available. Goal execution may involve the access of other goals which turn, may trigger their execution. Respective goal dependencies are explicitly specified in terms of a directed acyclic graph. 

Figure \ref{f:goalgraph} depicts Genestrip's goal dependency graph: Several goals (prefixed with \verb|d:|) revolve around downloading files from the RefSeq, Genbank and other data from the NCBI. Several object goals (prefixed with \verb|o:|) load files and represent them as data structures in main memory for further processing. Most file goals (prefixed with \verb|f:|) write computational results to files.
Boxes with edged corners depict goals that are meant to be executed directly via the command line. E.g., 
the goals \verb|match| and \verb|matchlr| are for fastq file analysis but they would trigger a respective database generation goal if the corresponding database file was missing.

Solid edges symbolize "eager" dependencies where the existence of a dependency's result is ensured prior to the execution of the depending goal. In contrast, dotted edges symbolize "lazy" dependencies where the existence of a dependency's result is ensured just when first accessed at the execution of the depending goal.

\subsection{Database and Filter Generation}
\label{database}

Genestrip stores a database primarily as a large array \verb|S| of sorted long values. Each long value encodes a $k$-mer from a genome of a selected organism. The default for $k$ is $31$ but other values are possible. 
To handle reverse complements of reads later at read analysis time, stored $k$-mers are standardized in the following way: Each $k$-mer is encoded twice as a long value -- once in its regular version and once via its reverse complement -- but only the larger of the two encoded values is stored in the database. 

A second array of the same size as \verb|S| encodes \taxon s for each $k$-mer in \verb|S|. At read analysis time, lookups in \verb|S| are performed via a binary search. However, an additional Bloom filter \citep{bloomfilter} also contains all stored $k$-mers. By default, it is consulted upfront to check whether a certain $k$-mer might be in \verb|S|. A negative result avoids a more expensive binary search in \verb|S| and therefore saves overall execution cost.
 
Moreover, all parts of the NCBI taxonomy tree \citep{taxonomy} which are required at analysis time are also included in  the database. The taxonomy extract comprises entries for the selected organisms and all of its ancestors. At analysis time, a bit vector with same size as \verb|S| indicates which $k$-mers from \verb|S| were found at least once. This is sufficient for exact unique $k$-mer counting and is space-efficient, as it requires only about 1.6\% of additional memory with respect to \verb|S|.

Table \ref{goals} describes the most relevant goals for database generation in their typical execution order. All of the described goals are multi-threading enabled for faster database generation.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{l|p{10cm}}
 Goal Name & Description \\
  \hline

 \verb|tempindex| & Creates a Bloom filter filled with all $k$-mers of genomes of selected organisms. The Bloom filter's size is initially set via the result from \verb|fillsize|. As the Bloom filter avoids double counting, its final number of entries can serve as a more exact basis for the actual database size.\\
 \verb|filldb| & This \emph{entry phase} populates the database with all $k$-mers of genomes from selected organisms. The appropriate size of the database is a close but conservative estimate based on \verb|tempindex|.
  Duplicate $k$-mer entries are avoided via a Boom filter that tracks the already stored $k$-mers. Finally, the stored $k$-mers are sorted in order to enable binary search at analysis time but also for the goal \verb|updatedb| from below.\\
 \verb|updatedb| & This \emph{update phase} updates the populated database to account for common $k$-mers between organisms.
 \emph{All} genomes from the RefSeq belonging to a set of pre-configurable taxonomic (super-)kingdoms will be considered. This step ensures that a $k$-mer from a selected organism obtains the correct \taxon\ as the $k$-mer may also occur in the genome of another organism (which might be represented in database or not). Concerning the finished database, the respective $k$-mer will be associated with the lowest common taxonomic ancestor of all organisms whose genomes contain the $k$-mer.\\
\end{tabular}

Additional genomes can manually be added either to create deeper databases or else, to filter out contaminating sequences that mistakenly have been included in the genome of an organism of interest. E.g. \cite{Breitwieser:2019aa} report that human DNA fragments often  contaminate bacterial genomes for various reasons.

Besides $k$-mer matching and read classification, Genestrip supports filtering of fastq files via a Bloom filter in similarity to \citep{biobloom}. When generated via the goal \verb|index|, the Bloom filter gets stored in separate file. The goal \verb|filter| loads the filter and processes fastq files accordingly. The standard configuration for the filtering process rewrites a read to the output if the read contains at least one $k$-mer that is recognized by the filter and thus, the read may belong to a selected organism.

Regarding database and Bloom filter generation, $k$-mers from contaminating sequences can be identified during the update phase: E.g., the human genome can be included via configuration in the update phase and so, a $k$-mer from the human genome that is also found in the contaminated genome will be pushed to the lowest common taxonomic ancestor of both species. Still, if the human genome is employed during the update phase only, a resulting database does not include respective $k$-mers (other than the one from contaminating sequences). This may ensure a small-sized database.

Of note, Genestrip supports the filtering of $k$-mers with low complexity known as genomic dust \citep{doi:10.1089/cmb.2006.13.1028}: As part of the entry phase, each $k$-mers's repetitiveness in terms of base pairs is measured via a fibonacci-based function. If the corresponding value exceeds a configurable threshold then the $k$-mer is dropped.

\subsection{Read Classification}

The principal read classification algorithm is similar to the one from  Kraken 1 \citep{kraken}. When iterating over a read's $k$-mers, each $k$-mer is encoded as a long once by considering its regular order and once via its reverse complement order. For efficiency, only the larger of the two encoded values is looked up in the database. This is sufficient since, if at all, then only just the larger of two values got stored in the first place during database generation (see Section \ref{database}). 

Two types of error thresholds can be configured for read classification:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The ratio or number of $k$-mers per read that must at least be found in the database and
  \item the ratio or number of $k$-mers per read that must be consistent the read's designated class taxon. A $k$-mer is consistent with a read's class taxon if the $k$-mer's taxon as stored in the database is the same or if it is a taxonomic ancestor of class taxon.
\end{enumerate}

Genestrip implements a multi-threading enabled classification procedure following a producer consumer pattern, where a producer thread reads a fastq file and puts reads into an in-memory queue while consumer threads pick reads from the queue in order to classify them.

 Genestrip introduces a novel, derived statistical measure -- the \emph{$k$-mer consistency ratio} -- as explained in the following: Given $k_{t,f,d}$, the a-priori probability $p(t\ |\ k_{t,f,d})$ of a unique $k$-mer from $d$ belonging to $t$ to occur \emph{at least once} in $f$\ is approximately:\footnote{The approximation is based on the simplifying assumption that all $k$-mers in $d$ belonging to $t$ are equally likely to be found in $f$ (such as reported in \citep{ticks}). Still, this may be countered by special DNA library preparation protocols like 16S amplification \citep{?}.}
\[ p(t\ |\ k_{t,f,d}) = 1 - (1 - 1/u_{t,d})^{k_{t,f,d}}\] 

The corresponding \emph{expected number of unique $k$-mers} $E$ is:
\[ E(k_{t,f,d},u_{t,d}) = u_{t,d} \cdot p(t\ |\ k_{t,f,d})\]
It is useful to compare $E$ with the actual count $u_{t,f,d}$: If these quantities divert too much from each other, then the consistency of the $k$-mer counting results for $t$ in $f$ should be questioned. If, on the other hand, these quantities are close, then this supports the consistency of the counting results. This justifies the following ratio as the \emph{$k$-mer consistency score}, which ideally is close to $1$:\footnote{A mathematically sounder approach is to consider the probability distribution 
$q(u_{t,f,d}\ |\ k_{t,f,d})$, i.e. the probability to obtain exactly $u_{t,f,d}$ unique $k$-mers for a given (non-unique) $k$-mer count of $k_{t,f,d}$ with  sampling with replacement as the underlying random process.
Interestingly, this distribution is non-trivial and has been investigated in \citep{Mendelson2016WhatIT}. The authors have shown that the distribution can be approximated by a normal distribution under most practically relevant conditions. Moreover, they confirm the expectation $E$ from above and give a formula for the variance $\sigma^2_q$ which in our case depends on $k_{t,f,d}$ and $u_{t,d}$. 
So, as an alternative consistency score one could set $r'(k_{t,f,d}, u_{t,f,d}) := f(u_{t,f,d}) / f(E)$,
where $f$ is the density of the normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(E,\sigma^2_q)$.
This relative density $r'$ ranges between $0$ and $1$ where $1$ is best and implies $u_{t,f,d} = E$.
Although mathematically sound, $r'$ did not appear useful with respect to $k$-mer counts because $\mathcal{N}(E,\sigma^2_q)$ tends to be extremely peaked and so, even small deviations from $E$ result in a $r' \approx 0$.}

%Expectation accounting for duplicate kmers in DB:

%\[ E_q(k_{t,f,d},\vec{k}_{t,d}) = \sum_{i = 1}^{u_{t,d}} k_{t,d}(i) \cdot (1 - (1 - 1/k_{t,d}(i))^{k_{t,f,d}}) \]
\[ r(k_{t,f,d}, u_{t,f,d}) := u_{t,f,d} / E(k_{t,f,d},u_{t,d})\]

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Correctness}
\label{correctness}

To assess Genestrips's correctness with regard to $k$-mer counting and read classification, we compared its databases and respective analysis results with those from KrakenUniq. We consulted KrakenUniq because Genestrip aims to provide a similar $k$-mer based functionality while also enabling small databases without loss of accuracy. Moreover, KrakenUniq is trusted by a large user base in bioinformatics and therefore applied in highly regarded research publications \citep{??}.

As part of our test procedure, we created database contents under both systems that were as identical as possible in terms of their genomic basis and their stored $k$-mers. 
We then applied the created databases to suitable fastq files in order to compare the results between both systems. Highly similar results from this check mutually solidify the correctness of both systems since they had been developed completely independently. Besides, Genestrip's correctness is also supported via extensive functional tests with high test coverage.

To create the databases, we chose all RefSeq genomes from the viral super-kingdom on the assembly level "complete genome". Figure \ref{viraldbscatter} compares the structure of the two databases produced with KrakenUniq (with 373,250,262 $k$-mers in total) and Genestrip (with 373,989,857 $k$-mers in total). The databases are nearly identical with regard to the number of $k$-mers stored per \taxon\ with 15 genomes and respective \taxon s occurring in KrakenUniq but not in Genestrip and 33 genomes vice versa.\footnote{The differences between the sets of selected genomes are caused by different approaches on how the genomes are preselected and downloaded by KrakenUniq and Genestrip, respectively. They do not constitute erroneous behavior of either system.} The slightly differing sets of genomes result in 158 differences in $k$-mers stored per \taxon . A large part of the differences is a consequence of the necessary lowest common ancestor update on stored $k$-mers (see \cite{kraken} and Section \ref{background}). In the following, we name the two databases \texttt{cv\_ku} and \texttt{cv\_g} with regard to KrakenUniq and Genestrip, respectively.

As real-world examples, we applied the two databases to six short-read fastq files from DNA-sequencing human saliva of four donors. We measured the mean absolute percentage error of $k$-mer counts for these "human saliva" fastq files according to the following error criterion: 
Let $S_d$ be the list of all \taxon s in a database $d$.
The mean absolute percentage error $err_{f,d}(e)$ for a database $e$ with respect to fastq file $f$ and database $d$ is\footnote{The term "$k_{t,f,d} + 1$" in the formula includes a Laplace correction to avoid a potential division by zero.}
\begin{equation}
\label{err}
err_{f,d}(e) := 100 / | S_{d} \cap S_{e} | \cdot \sum_{t \in S_{d} \cap S_{e}} |k_{t,f,d} - k_{t,f,e}| / (k_{t,f,d} + 1)	
\end{equation}

\subsection{Accuracy in the Context of Smaller Databases}

The following results highlight the importance of the update phase according to Section \ref{background} in the context of smaller databases.

As a first example, we created two small viral databases based on the RefSeq covering \emph{human viruses only}\footnote{The list of human viruses originates from \url{https://viralzone.expasy.org/678}. Some human viruses known to be missing were manually added. All \taxon s were chosen on the species rank.} -- again under both KrakenUniq and Genestrip.  In the following, we name the two databases \texttt{hv\_ku} and \texttt{hv\_g} with regard to KrakenUniq and Genestrip, respectively. We again assured that the databases were as identical as possible in terms of their genomic basis and their stored $k$-mers.
Figure \ref{viraldbscomp} compares the viral database contents for the databases from Section \ref{correctness} with the respective "human virus only" database for (a) Genestrip and (b) KrakenUniq. The plots depict entries of the 407 \taxon s referenced in both databases.

Regarding Genestrip's databases from Figure \ref{viraldbscomp} (a), all $k$-mer counts on the species rank and below are identical per \taxon\ as an effect of the update phase (as discussed in Section \ref{background}). This is an important precondition met by Genestrip's \texttt{hv\_g} database to avoid false positives at read classification time. Nevertheless, data points from higher taxonomic ranks scatter because the complete viral database  \texttt{cv\_g} contains $k$-mers from many more non-human viruses, and these $k$-mers were pushed to higher ranks due to the least common ancestor update. Obviously, these additional $k$-mers are not required to accurately classify reads with respect to human viruses. The database \texttt{hv\_g} contains only 4,175,715 $k$-mers with a disk size of just about 60 MB, whereas \texttt{cv\_g} has as disk size of 5.4 GB with almost 90 times as many $k$-mers.

KrakenUniq's corresponding $k$-mer counts from Figure \ref{viraldbscomp} (b) are not identical on the species rank and below. Although visually not well obvious in (b), this accounts for 158 scattering data points on the species rank and below. These differences offer potential for lower accuracy and in particular false positives at read classification time as will be exemplified next.
