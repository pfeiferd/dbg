    \abstract{\textbf{Background:} The consumption of main memory resources is a significant burden in $k$-mer-based metagenomic analysis when creating related databases but also when performing (unique) $k$-mer-counting and read classification. Genestrip addresses this issue by focusing on small but freely configurable groups of organisms.

    \textbf{Results:}  Regarding the selected organisms, Genestrip produces databases and results comparable to those of KrakenUniq but at a fraction of its required memory resources. So both, database creation \emph{and} read classification may be performed even on small-sized PCs or edge machines. Moreover, Genestrip can be used to extremely reduce the size of sequence files for downstream steps without loss of relevant data regarding the selected organisms.

    \textbf{Conclusions:} We assessed the correctness, usefulness and performance of Genestrip in different contexts and showed that it ascertains high read classification recall and precision for organisms whose genomes were included in a corresponding database. Due to Genestrip's particular way of updating the $k$-mers' lowest common ancestors stored in a database, both database creation and its use can be realized with little memory. This empowers users to flexibly create and apply such databases potentially with a very deep genomic coverage for the chosen organisms.}

    \keywords{Metagenomics, $k$-mer counting, read classification, small $k$-mer database, fastq file filtering, high accuracy, high performance}

    \section{Background}\label{background}

    \subsection{Introduction}

    With the availability of low-cost and small-scale sequencing devices, metagenomic analysis will likely replace many established technologies for the detection and surveillance of specific groups of microorganisms. Related applications may affect various areas such as farming, ecology, food processing and medicine. While sequencing devices have become ready for ubiquitous use, software tools for metagenomics still tend to be resource intensive and too hard to use for end users.

    To analyze large sets of reads, software tools based on $k$-mer matching and counting such as Kraken 1 \citep{kraken}, Kraken 2 \citep{kraken2} or KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq} are at hand. With regard to pathogen detection, KrakenUniq is particularly suited because of its very low false positive rate but also because of its efficiency and high sensitivity \citep{krakenUniq}.

    \subsection{Problem statement}
    \label{problem}

    In order to avoid false positive classifications of reads, $k$-mer counting tools resort to very large databases, containing millions of encoded $k$-mers along with their respective taxa. A database is usually loaded entirely into main memory and often consumes tens of gigabytes of space. This requirement mandates specialized and expensive compute servers with very large main memory.

    In recognition of this problem, KrakenUniq has been extended for database processing in chunks at read analysis time \citep{lowmemkraken}. However, database creation still mandates ultralarge main memory: For example, in 2018 the production of a database for Kraken 1 and, equivalently, KrakenUniq that just covers the bacterial genomes from the RefSeq \citep{refseq} ``required ca. 2.5 TB of RAM and ca. 11 days" on a suitable computer \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. However, since then, the RefSeq's number of prokaryotic genomes alone has increased by a factor of 4.5 to over 38 Mio. \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038}.
    Therefore, related organisms are often not covered below the species rank and also genomes below the quality level ``complete genome" are usually not included.

    Despite the fact that these databases may not cover organisms of interest at the necessary genomic depth, users tend to apply them because otherwise, creating even larger databases would require even more main memory \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. So, although a much richer set of genomes for organisms of interest may be available in principle -- for example via recent versions of the RefSeq or in addition via Genbank \citep{genbank} -- these genomes cannot easily be leveraged for metagenomic analysis \citep{Cuesta-Zuluaga:2020aa,Youngblut:2021aa}.

    Even though Kraken 2 offers more memory efficient $k$-mer databases, \emph{it is not a suitable alternative in fields such as medical diagnostics where precision of read classification is paramount} \citep{lowmemkraken}. This is because in comparison with Kraken 1 and KrakenUniq, Kraken 2 produces smaller databases but it compresses stored $k$-mers under loss of information, therefore facilitating false positive $k$-mer counts \citep{kraken2, lowmemkraken}.

    \subsection{Approach}

    If only a smaller group of a few dozen genera or a few 100 species is under consideration, then it seems promising to simply \emph{generate a smaller database that contains just the $k$-mers from respective genomes}. \emph{However, without additional measures, this very likely leads to many false positives}: The reason for this is that owing to the evolutionary relationship of organisms, \emph{a large fraction of $k$-mers that belong to the genome of a species are unspecific} in the sense that they can as well be found in the genomes other species or genera not considered for analysis. Thus, when using too small a database, a read may be falsely assigned to a species because it consists of $k$-mers that \emph{also} belong to another species' genome that was not included in the database.

    In order to enable a smaller database with no false positives emerging from this problem, we introduce Genestrip, as new metagenomic software tool supporting (unique) $k$-mer counting, read classification and read filtering.

    In principle, Genestrip employs a \emph{two-phase} database generation process: During the \emph{entry phase}, all $k$-mers of genomes from selected organisms are entered into the database on the basis of the RefSeq \citep{refseq} and optionally GenBank \citep{genbank} and additional sources. During the \emph{update phase}, all stored $k$-mers from the database are checked against the genomes of \emph{all} other organisms from the RefSeq belonging to the same \emph{category}\footnote{By standard, the RefSeq is structurally divided into 14 categories including ``Bacteria", ``Fungi", ``Invertebrate", ``Protozoa", ``Viral" and others.}. So, if a stored $k$-mer also occurs in another organism which is not part of the database, the $k$-mer's \taxon\ still gets updated in the database and the lowest common ancestor's \taxon\ (known as LCA) is entered instead \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. This prevents a $k$-mer from being falsely associated with one of the selected organisms given that it is too unspecific.

    Based on this approach, Genestrip achieves a low memory footprint during database creation as well as at read analysis time. Moreover, it offers competitive to highly favorable runtimes for $k$-mer matching and read classification. Together, these qualities empower users to create and use respective databases on machines with regular main memory size. Genestrip's $k$-mer counting results are similar to those of KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq} with respect to the selected organisms. Genestrip also supports unique $k$-mer counting with exact counts and with low memory overhead.

    Genestrip is written in Java, has a small code base and supports command-line-based as well as API-based usage. Based on the Java Virtual Machine, it can be executed on  modern operating systems including Linux, Windows and macOS.

    \section{Implementation}\label{methods}

    \subsection{System structure}

    Genestrip's database generation mandates the setting of groups of organisms whose genomes will be exclusively represented in a resulting database. Users choose the desired genera, species or strains via a configuration file by listing the corresponding taxa. If higher ranked taxa are entered, then by default, \emph{all} of the descending taxa will be included and $k$-mers of their genomes will be added to the database accordingly.

    Genestrip generates a database for a selected group of organisms primarily via
    genomes from the RefSeq \citep{refseq} but genomes from Genbank and additional genomic files may be included on top or instead.
    To better handle the complexity of the generation process, it employs a goal-oriented approach similar to Make \citep{feldman1979make}: Upon access, each goal returns a file or an object in main memory. A goal is executed only if its result is not yet available. Goal execution may involve the access of other goals which in turn, may trigger their execution. Respective goal dependencies are explicitly specified in terms of a directed acyclic graph.

    Figure \ref{goalgraph} depicts an extract of Genestrip's goal dependency graph: Several goals (prefixed with \verb|d:|) revolve around automatically downloading all necessary files of the RefSeq, Genbank and other data from the NCBI's Web site \citep{Sayers:2025aa}.
    When required for database generation, the goal \verb|refseqfna| downloads the RefSeq genomes belonging to a matching category as a collection of zip files. The collection is one of NCBI's  standard offerings. By consulting the goal \verb|checksummap|, \verb|refseqfna| also performs an MD5 fingerprint check after each zip file download to ensure data integrity and reattempts the download in case the initial file appears to be corrupted.

    Several object goals (prefixed with \verb|o:|) load files and create data structures or compute results in main memory for further processing. Most file goals (prefixed with \verb|f:|) write computational results to files.
    Boxes with edged corners depict goals that are meant to be executed directly via the command line. For example,
    the goal \verb|match| is for fastq file analysis but it would trigger a respective database generation goal if the corresponding database file was missing.

    Solid edges symbolize ``eager" dependencies where the existence of a dependency's result is ensured prior to the execution of the dependent goal. In contrast, dotted edges symbolize ``lazy" dependencies where the existence of a dependency's result is ensured when first accessed during the execution of the dependent goal.

    \subsection{Database and filter generation}
    \label{database}

    Genestrip stores a database primarily as a large array \verb|S| of sorted long values. Each long value encodes a $k$-mer from the genome of a selected organism. The default for $k$ is $31$ but smaller values and $k=32$ are possible.
    In order to handle reverse complements of reads later at read analysis time, stored $k$-mers are standardized in the following way: Each $k$-mer is encoded twice as a long value --- once in its regular version and once via its reverse complement --- but only the larger of the two encoded values is stored in the database.

    A second array of the same size as \verb|S| encodes taxa for each $k$-mer in \verb|S|. At read analysis time, lookups in \verb|S| are performed via a binary search. However, an additional Bloom filter \citep{bloomfilter} also contains all stored $k$-mers. By default, it is consulted upfront to check whether a certain $k$-mer might be in \verb|S|. A negative result avoids a more expensive binary search in \verb|S| and therefore saves overall execution cost.

    Moreover, all parts of the NCBI taxonomy tree \citep{taxonomy} required at analysis time are also included in  the database. The taxonomy extract comprises entries for the selected organisms and all of their ancestors. At analysis time, a bit vector with the same size as \verb|S| indicates which $k$-mers from \verb|S| were found at least once. This is sufficient for exact unique $k$-mer counting and is space-efficient, as it requires only about 1.6\% of additional main memory with respect to \verb|S|.

    Table \ref{goals} describes the most relevant goals for database generation in their typical execution order. All of these goals are multithreading enabled for faster database generation.

    \begin{table}[h]

            Goal name & Description \\
            \hline
            \verb|fillsize| & Determines the maximum size of the database in terms of number of $k$-mers by reading the respective reference genome files and including the genomes of selected organisms. This results in a conservative estimate where duplicates are double counted. \\
            \verb|tempindex| & Creates a Bloom filter filled with all $k$-mers of the genomes of selected organisms. The Bloom filter's size is initially set via the result from \verb|fillsize|. As the Bloom filter avoids double counting, its final number of entries serves as a more exact basis for the actual database size.\\
            \verb|filldb| & This \emph{entry phase} populates the database with all $k$-mers of genomes from selected organisms. The appropriate size of the database is a close but conservative estimate based on \verb|tempindex|.
            Duplicate $k$-mer entries are avoided via a Boom filter that tracks the already stored $k$-mers. Finally, the stored $k$-mers are sorted in order to enable binary search at analysis time but also for the goal \verb|updatedb| from below.\\
            \verb|updatedb| & This \emph{update phase} updates the populated database to account for common $k$-mers between organisms.
            \emph{All} genomes from the RefSeq belonging to a set of preconfigurable taxonomic categories are considered during this phase. This ensures that a $k$-mer from a selected organism obtains the correct \taxon\ as the $k$-mer may also occur in the genome of another organism (which might be represented in the database or not). In the finished database, the respective $k$-mer is associated with the lowest common ancestor taxon as computed via all considered genomes that contain the $k$-mer.\\

    Additional genomes can be manually added either to create deeper databases or, else, to filter out contaminating sequences that are mistakenly included in the genome of an organism of interest. For example, \cite{Breitwieser:2019aa} reported that human DNA fragments often  contaminate bacterial genomes for various reasons whereas \cite{Lu:2018aa} discussed a pipeline to remove contaminants from genomes of eukaryotic pathogens.

    Regarding database generation, $k$-mers from contaminating sequences can be identified during the update phase: For example, the human genome can be included via configuration in the update phase only; thus, a $k$-mer from the human genome that is also found in the contaminated genome will be pushed to the lowest common ancestor taxon of both species. Still, as the human genome is employed during the update phase only, a resulting database does not include $k$-mers from the human genome (other than those from contaminating sequences). This ensures a small-sized database.

    Besides $k$-mer matching and read classification, Genestrip supports filtering of fastq files via a Bloom filter in similarity to \citep{biobloom}. When generated via the goal \verb|index|, the Bloom filter gets stored in a separate file. The goal \verb|filter| loads the filter and processes fastq files accordingly. The standard configuration of the filtering process rewrites a read to the output if the read contains at least one $k$-mer that is recognized by the filter since because of this, the read may belong to a selected organism of interest.

    Of note, Genestrip allows for the exclusion of $k$-mers with low complexity known as genomic dust \citep{doi:10.1089/cmb.2006.13.1028}: As part of the entry phase, each $k$-mers's repetitiveness in terms of base pairs is measured via a fibonacci-based function. If the corresponding value exceeds a configurable threshold then the $k$-mer is dropped.

    \subsection{Read classification}

    The principal read classification algorithm is similar to that from  Kraken 1 \citep{kraken} and equivalently KrakenUniq. When iterating over a read's $k$-mers, each $k$-mer is encoded as a long -- once by considering its regular order and once via its reverse complement order. For efficiency, only the larger of the two encoded values is looked up in the database. This is sufficient since, if at all, then only just the larger of two values got stored in the first place during database generation (see Section \ref{database}).

    Genestrip implements a multithreading enabled classification procedure following a producer consumer pattern, where a producer thread reads a fastq file and puts reads into an in-memory queue while consumer threads pick reads from the queue in order to classify them.

    Two types of error thresholds can be configured for read classification:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The ratio or number of $k$-mers per read that must at least be found in the database and
        \item the ratio or number of $k$-mers per read that must be consistent with the read's designated class taxon. A $k$-mer is consistent with a read's class taxon if the $k$-mer's taxon as stored in the database is the same or if it is a taxonomic ancestor of the class taxon.
    \end{enumerate}
    The thresholds allow for playing off recall against precision in light of potentially high error rates of a sequencing device \citep{Delahaye:2021aa,Liu-Wei:2024aa}. For all the experiments throughout this paper, both thresholds stick to the default of one $k$-mer which is the most sensitive level.

    \subsection{Analysis reports} \label{measures}

    Genestrip produces analysis reports in CSV file format with a comprehensive set of statistical values. We use the following notation to describe them: Let $k_{t,f,d}$ be the number of $k$-mers for \taxon\ $t$ found in fastq file $f$ with respect to database $d$ and let $u_{t,f,d}$ be the corresponding number of unique $k$-mers. Similarly, let $u_{t,d}$ be the number of (unique) $k$-mers for $t$ stored in $d$.

    Table \ref{csvcolumns} describes the most important reported columns. Additional columns exist to  accumulate $k$-mer counts and read counts etc. upward to ancestors in the taxonomy. We also track the standard deviations of \verb|avg. contig length|, \verb|avg. read length|, \verb|mean class error| and other columns wherever suitable. Moreover, normalized values derived from the original values by dividing by $u_{t,d}$ are included where suitable. Normalized values aim to reduce counting bias due to a high or low representation of a \taxon\ $t$ in terms of its stored $k$-mers $u_{t,d}$.

    Genestrip introduces a novel, derived statistical measure --- the \emph{$k$-mer consistency ratio} --- as explained in the following: Given $k_{t,f,d}$, the apriori probability $p(t\ |\ k_{t,f,d})$ of a unique $k$-mer from $d$ belonging to $t$ to occur \emph{at least once} in $f$\ is approximately:\footnote{The approximation is based on the assumption that all $k$-mers in $d$ belonging to $t$ are equally likely to be found in $f$ (such as reported for the results in \cite{nanoporeticks}). However, this assumption does not hold when special DNA library preparation protocols such as 16S or 18S amplification are used \citep{Wensel:2022aa,16srrnause}. In such cases the $k$-mer consistency ratio must therefore be disregarded.}
    \begin{equation}
        \label{ptk}
        p(t\ |\ k_{t,f,d}) = 1 - (1 - 1/u_{t,d})^{k_{t,f,d}}
    \end{equation}
    The corresponding \emph{expected number of unique $k$-mers} is as follows:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{E}
        E(k_{t,f,d},u_{t,d}) = u_{t,d} \cdot p(t\ |\ k_{t,f,d})
    \end{equation}

    It is useful to compare $E(k_{t,f,d},u_{t,d})$ with the actual count $u_{t,f,d}$: If these quantities divert too much from each other, then the consistency of the $k$-mer counting results for $t$ in $f$ should be questioned. If, on the other hand, these quantities are close, then this supports the consistency of the counting results. This justifies the \emph{$k$-mer consistency ratio}, which ideally is close to $1$:\footnote{A mathematically sounder approach is to consider the probability distribution
        $q(u_{t,f,d}\ |\ k_{t,f,d})$, i.e., the probability of obtaining exactly $u_{t,f,d}$ unique $k$-mers for a given (nonunique) $k$-mer count of $k_{t,f,d}$ by sampling with replacement as the underlying random process.
        Interestingly, this distribution is non-trivial and has been investigated in \cite{Mendelson2016WhatIT}. The authors have shown that the distribution can be approximated by a normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(E(k_{t,f,d},u_{t,d}),\sigma^2_q)$ under most practically relevant conditions. Moreover, they confirm the expectation $E(k_{t,f,d},u_{t,d})$ from above and give a formula for the variance $\sigma^2_q$ which in our case depends on $k_{t,f,d}$ and $u_{t,d}$.
        Although mathematically sound, the approach did not prove useful with respect to $k$-mer counts because $\mathcal{N}$ tends to be extremely peaked and so, even small deviations from $E(k_{t,f,d},u_{t,d})$ result in a $q(u_{t,f,d}\ |\ k_{t,f,d}) \approx 0$.}
%Expectation accounting for duplicate kmers in DB:
%Let $k_{t,d}(i)$ be the number of times a unique kmer $i$ occurs in the genome of $t$ with $1 \leq i \leq u_{t,d}$ and $\vec{k}_{t,d}$ the corresponding vector:
%\[ E_q(k_{t,f,d},\vec{k}_{t,d}) = \sum_{i = 1}^{u_{t,d}} k_{t,d}(i) \cdot (1 - (1 - 1/k_{t,d}(i))^{k_{t,f,d}}) \]
    \begin{equation}
        \label{r}
        r_{t,f,d} := u_{t,f,d} / E(k_{t,f,d},u_{t,d})
    \end{equation}

    \section{Results}\label{results}

    \subsection{Correctness}
    \label{correctness}

    To assess Genestrip's correctness with regard to $k$-mer counting and read classification, we compared example databases and respective analysis results directly and in detail with those from KrakenUniq. We consulted KrakenUniq because Genestrip aims to provide a similar $k$-mer based functionality while also enabling small databases without loss of precision and recall. Moreover, KrakenUniq and related tools are trusted by a large user base in bioinformatics \citep{Lu:2022aa}.

    As part of our test procedure, we created database contents under both systems that were as identical as possible in terms of their genomic basis and their stored $k$-mers.
    We then applied the created databases to suitable fastq files in order to compare the results between both systems. Highly similar results from this check mutually solidify the correctness of both systems since they had been developed completely independently. (In addition, Genestrip's correctness is also supported via extensive automated functional tests with high test coverage as part of its code base.)

    To create the databases, we chose all RefSeq genomes from the category ``Viral". Figure \ref{viraldbscatter} compares the structure of the two databases produced with KrakenUniq (with 373,250,262 $k$-mers in total) and Genestrip (with 374,412,041 $k$-mers in total). The databases are nearly identical with regard to the number of $k$-mers stored per \taxon\ with 15 genomes and respective taxa occurring in KrakenUniq but not in Genestrip and 33 genomes vice versa.\footnote{The differences between the sets of selected genomes are caused by different approaches on how the genomes are preselected and downloaded by KrakenUniq and Genestrip, respectively. They do not constitute erroneous behavior of either system.} The slightly different sets of genomes resulted in 158 differences in $k$-mers stored per \taxon . A large part of the differences is a consequence of the necessary lowest common ancestor update on stored $k$-mers (see Section \ref{background}). In the following, we name the two databases \texttt{cv\_ku} and \texttt{cv\_g} with regard to KrakenUniq and Genestrip, respectively.

    \subsection{Accuracy in the context of small databases}
    \label{accuracy}

    The following results highlight the importance of the update phase according to Section \ref{background} in the context of small databases.

    As a first example, we created two small viral databases based on the RefSeq covering \emph{human viruses only}\footnote{The list of human viruses originates from \cite{viralzone}. Some human viruses known to be missing were manually added. All \taxon s were chosen on the species rank.}, again under both KrakenUniq and Genestrip.  In the following, we name the two databases \texttt{hv\_ku} and \texttt{hv\_g} with regard to KrakenUniq and Genestrip, respectively. We again assured that the databases were as identical as possible in terms of their genomic basis and their stored $k$-mers.
    Figure \ref{viraldbscomp} compares the viral database contents for the databases from Section \ref{correctness} with the respective ``human virus only" database for (a) Genestrip and (b) KrakenUniq. The plots depict data points of the 407 taxa referenced in both databases.

    Regarding Genestrip's databases from Figure \ref{viraldbscomp} (a), all $k$-mer counts on the species rank and below are identical per \taxon\ as an effect of the update phase (as discussed in Section \ref{background}). This is an important precondition met by Genestrip's \texttt{hv\_g} database to avoid false positives at read classification time. Nevertheless, data points from higher taxonomic ranks scatter because the complete viral database  \texttt{cv\_g} contains $k$-mers from many more nonhuman viruses, and these $k$-mers were pushed to higher ranks due to the lowest common ancestor update. Obviously, these additional $k$-mers are not required to classify reads accurately with respect to human viruses. The database \texttt{hv\_g} contains only 3,865,987 $k$-mers with a disk size of approximately 53 MB, whereas \texttt{cv\_g} has as disk size of 5.4 GB with almost 97 times as many $k$-mers.

Although visually not well obvious in (b), this accounts for 158 scattering data points on the species rank and below.     More precisely, we used the following error measure:
    Let $S_d$ be the list of all taxa \emph{for the rank species or below} in a database $d$.
    The mean absolute percentage error $err_{f,d}(e)$ for a database $e$ with respect to fastq file $f$ and the ``ground truth" database $d$ is as follows:\footnote{The term ``$k_{t,f,d} + 1$" in the formula includes a Laplace correction to avoid a potential division by zero.}
    \begin{equation}
        err_{f,d}(e) := 100 / | S_{d} \cap S_{e} | \cdot \sum_{t \in S_{d} \cap S_{e}} |k_{t,f,d} - k_{t,f,e}| / (k_{t,f,d} + 1)
    \end{equation}
    Note that we limit the taxa to the rank species or below because, as discussed in the context of Figure \ref{viraldbscomp} and \ref{salivascatter}, we cannot expect $k$-mer counts and read counts to be close between $d$ and a corresponding smaller database $e$ for higher taxonomic ranks.
    Analogously, we also measured the error with regard to the counts of classified reads.

    As shown in Table \ref{errorscvhv}, the error rates for $k$-mer counts are zero under Genestrip, and for read counts, they are considerably lower than those under KrakenUniq across all the examples. These findings solidify the usefulness of Genestrip's analysis results in the context of smaller databases.

    \subsection{Example: Tick-borne bacterial pathogens}
    \label{tickborne}

    This section presents the detection of tick-related bacterial pathogens as an example application of Genestrip. We compare the very large standard database \texttt{MicrobialDB} available for KrakenUniq\footnote{See ``MicrobialDB" under \cite{microbialdb}.} against a relatively small Genestrip database \texttt{tb} with high genomic coverage regarding the organisms of interest. The results aim to show that a small but genomically deeper database such as \texttt{tb} can lead to higher recall than can KrakenUniq's standard database while sustaining high precision.

    An envisioned application is tick surveillance via metagenomics according to \cite{nanoporeticks}, where the authors applied Nanopore technology to check eight field-collected ticks but back then relied on Kraken 2 to identify the corresponding organisms. Due to \cite{HUGGINS2024100839}, \cite{Miao:2024aa} and \cite{Huggins:2024aa} we also see a use case when screening for tick-borne pathogens in vertebrates potentially including humans.

    \subsubsection{The databases \texttt{MicrobialDB} and \texttt{tb}}
    \label{mdbtb}
    \texttt{MicrobialDB} for KrakenUniq aims at the diagnosis of infectious diseases using metagenomics and contains 97.68 times as many $k$-mers as \texttt{tb}. It has a disk size of 574.31 GB excluding the taxonomy tree and additional mapping files. It covers a broad set of microbials including archaea, bacteria, viral genomes, the human genome and pathogenic eukaryotes with contaminants removed \citep{Lu:2018aa}.

    The database \texttt{tb} for Genestrip is based on the RefSeq Release 230 and covers all RefSeq  genomes under the twelve bacterial genera Bartonella, Rickettsia, Anaplasma, Ehrlichia, Borrelia, Borreliella, Chlamydia, Mycoplasma, Malacoplasma, Mycoplasmoides, Metamycoplasma and Francisella as many included species are pathogenic to humans and known or assumed to occur in ticks \citep{Eisen:2018aa, JOHNSON20181499, Janecek:2012aa, Thomas:1990aa, 10.3389/fvets.2025.1522904}. To avoid contaminants from human DNA and black-legged tick DNA, we added corresponding genomes to Genestrip's update phase as explained at the end of Section \ref{database}. We also activated the genomic dust filter at high sensitivity: e.g., the $k$-mer \texttt{ATACGGCGCGCGCGCGCGCGCATATATATTC} would be dropped because its repetitiveness is just above the set threshold.

    \texttt{tb} contains 487,834,649 $k$-mers and has a disk size of just 5.87 GB including the accompanied Bloom filter and the matching extract of the NCBI taxonomy tree. \texttt{tb} has 1.96 times as many $k$-mers stored under the selected genera and below as \texttt{MicrobialDB}. The macro average increase across the chosen genera is a factor of 1.83 with a maximum factor of 3.25 for Bartonella and a minimum factor of 1.29 for Clamydia. The main reason behind this increase is the total growth of the RefSeq's number of prokaryotic genomes by a factor of over 1.39 between the generation of \texttt{MicrobialDB} in mid 2023 and the generation of \texttt{tb} \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038}.

    The generation of \texttt{tb} revealed that its included genomes from the RefSeq have a high overlap: On average each stored $k$-mer occurred 11.2 times in these genomes, as determined by the \texttt{tempindex} goal (see Section \ref{database}). Only 0.037 \% of all $k$-mers considered for storing were dropped as ``dust". The update phase pushed 26.3 \% of the stored $k$-mers to ranks above genus. 0.027 \% of the stored $k$-mers had been pushed to the taxon of ``cellular organisms" because tick DNA and human DNA were included on purpose during the update phase for the sake for contaminant detection.
%Finally, 2.891 $k$-mers landed on the taxonomic root because of plasmids included in the bacterial part of RefSeq that overlap with the genomes of the selected genera.

    \label{tickanalysis}

    \subsection{Performance}
    \label{performance}

    As mentioned in Section \ref{background}, Genestrip's focus is on generating and using potentially small but also deep $k$-mer databases for accurate metagenomic analysis of selected organisms of interest. This implies that users must be empowered to flexibly create new databases -- ideally on their own devices. We therefore put focus on the performance of database generation as well as on the performance of read classification.

    The following experiments were all performed on a modern PC with 32 GB of RAM, 8 CPU cores and a 2 TB solid state disk.\footnote{We ensured that as few competing processes as possible were running during the experiments and OS disk swapping was disabled. Platform details: AMD Ryzen 7 2700X, Linux 6.12.37, OpenJDK 21.0.7 64-Bit Server VM.}

    \subsection{Read classification}
    \label{runtimeperf}

    To assess Genestrip's performance regarding $k$-mer counting and read classification, we compared its analysis runs with corresponding runs under KrakenUniq.

    For the first comparison we applied the almost equally structured databases \texttt{cv\_ku} for KrakenUniq and \texttt{cv\_g} for Genestrip from Section \ref{correctness} to the human saliva fastq files from Table \ref{errorsviral}. The first section of Table \ref{analysisperf} summarizes the analysis performance across all five fastq files.

    The second comparison, shown in Table \ref{analysisperf}, uses the database \texttt{MicrobialDB} vs. \texttt{tb} from Section \ref{mdbtb} to analyze the eight tick-related fastq files from Section \ref{tickanalysis}. Since \texttt{MicrobialDB} is so large, we had to make use of KrakenUniq's database processing in chunks which broke down each run into 23 rounds. The chunk processing caused considerably longer run times. On the one hand, comparing the tools this way may be considered ``unfair" because of unequally sized database sizes. On the other hand, there is no alternative to employing a very large KakenUniq database for the purpose (of detecting tick-related bacterial pathogens) because, as examined in Section \ref{accuracy}, a small KrakenUniq database bares the high risk of many false positives.

    Regarding main memory, KrakenUniq's and Genestrip's analysis performance are dominated by the size of the database. In both cases, the required memory did not vary much across runs when the same database was used. Since \texttt{MicrobialDB} did not fit into memory, we set KrakenUniq's preload size such that in total, it allocated most of the available memory. In case of the viral database, KrakenUniq still needs more RAM than Genestrip. We suspect this is because KrakenUniq loads the entire NCBI taxonomy tree whereas Genestrip represents only those parts of the tree in main memory that are needed for the analysis.

    Overall, the results from both comparisons suggest that depending on the use case, Genestrip  and its small databases offer competitive to highly favorable analysis performance.

    \section{Conclusions}\label{discussion}

    We have presented Genestrip - a bioinformatics tool for (unique) $k$-mer counting, read classification and fastq file filtering. Its major improvement over existing systems is its very low memory consumption at database generation time and at read analysis time while also maintaining very high accuracy. To achieve this, Genestrip facilitates and suggests the generation of small databases that cover the genomes of a collection of organisms of interest. Genestrip's update phase ensures that during database generation, the most suitable lowest common ancestor taxon is computed for each stored $k$-mer by \emph{also considering the genomes of organisms whose $k$-mers are not included in the database}. Normally, \emph{Genestrip considers all genomes from a matching category of the RefSeq for this purpose}.

    The results from Section \ref{correctness} indicate that Genestrip attains the same type of correctness as KrakenUniq in terms of database structure, $k$-mer counting and read classification given that the databases of both systems are filled via all genomes belonging to an entire RefSeq category. But more importantly, Section \ref{accuracy} highlights that Genestrip sustains a high level accuracy when changing from a large, comprehensive database to a small database, although just a subset of the genomes out of a RefSeq category is included in that small database. In contrast, a small KrakenUniq database fails to achieve this level of accuracy because KrakenUniq's lowest common ancestor computation does not ever consider genomes outside of the database to be generated.

    Section \ref{tickborne} has exemplified the usefulness of Genestrip's ``small databases" in the context of tick surveillance where ticks were screened for a collection of well-known bacterial pathogens via metagenomics \citep{nanoporeticks}. The resulting database \texttt{tb} has a disk size of less than 6 GB and can be used for analysis on any modern PC. Nevertheless, Genestrip offers the exactness of KrakenUniq's very large database \texttt{MicrobialDB} in terms of $k$-mer counting, unique $k$-mer analysis and read classification for the chosen genera.

    Unlike Kraken 2, for example, Genestrip also eliminates the potential for false positive $k$-mer counts because it does not compress $k$-mers under loss of information. This renders our tool suitable in fields such as medical diagnostics where precision of $k$-mer counting and read classification is paramount --- which was also a motive for evolving Kraken 1 into KrakenUniq \citep{lowmemkraken} after the same research group had already authored Kraken 2 \citep{kraken2}.

    The deliberate focus on a particular set of genera or species allows for more genomes to be included for the organisms of interest while the resulting Genestrip database may still remain small. For example, the tick-related database \texttt{tb} from Section \ref{mdbtb} contains 1.83 times more $k$-mers from the chosen genera than the latest publicly available large database \texttt{MicrobialDB} for KrakenUniq. As \texttt{tb} is deeper than \texttt{MicrobialDB} in this respect, it also tends to achieve better recall when analyzing tick related fastq files without loss of precision. We have presented and discussed the corresponding results as part of Section \ref{tickanalysis}.

    Regarding performance, we have examined \emph{database generation as well as fastq file analysis}: Section \ref{genperformance} has shown that Genestrip empowers users to flexibly create new databases on regular machines in just a few hours. As summarized in Tables \ref{dbdesc} and \ref{dbperf}, we created databases with different applications in mind where the generation of \texttt{tb} took the longest with still less than 14 hours while main memory consumption remained below 30 GB in all the cases.

    Let us contrast Genestrip's database generation performance with the corresponding resources required under KrakenUniq: As mentioned in Section \ref{problem}, \cite{Nasko:2018aa} stated that generating a full bacterial database for Kraken 1 and so equivalently for KrakenUniq from the RefSeq ``required ca. 2.5 TB of RAM and ca. 11 days" on a suitable computer. However, since then, the RefSeq's number of prokaryotic genomes alone has increased up by a factor of 4.5 to over 38 Mio. \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038}. The additional growth of the RefSeq is also a likely reason why \texttt{MicrobialDB} has not been updated anymore since 2023 because ultralarge main memory and extremely high computational power would be required for this. Overall, this development suggests that $k$-mer databases with very broad and comprehensive coverage (such as by including \emph{all} genomes from the RefSeq category ``Bacteria") have reached a ``dead-end" -- at least when a lossless storing of $k$-mers is desired.

    In Section \ref{runtimeperf} we examined Genestrip's analysis performance: When the database structure under KrakenUniq and Genestrip was similar then the performance was similar too with KrakenUniq offering about 33\% better runtimes. However, when the use case for Genestrip's small databases was met, then the database size played off with regard to the analysis time: E.g., when analyzing tick-borne bacterial pathogens via the database \texttt{tb}, Genestrip was more than ten times faster than KrakenUniq because on a regular PC, KrakenUniq's large database \texttt{MicrobialDB} has to be processed in chunks over several iterations. Regarding main memory, Genestrip analysis performance is dominated by the size of the database itself. E.g, it remained below 9 GB of RAM under the database \texttt{tb}.

    In use cases where metagenomics is meant to be performed with very few restrictions on what organisms to look out for, then Genestrip is not the right choice. Although it is certainly possible to create large databases under Genestrip, one would end up with similar problems as with KrakenUniq (see above). However, if exactness of results is required in addition to broad coverage, then a two-stage analysis procedure might prove useful: In stage one, a tool such as Kraken 2 with a compressed database for broad coverage may be applied to identify candidate organisms of interest. In stage two, Genestrip might be used to confirm and ``drill down" the initial findings by potentially first generating and then using a dedicated database.
