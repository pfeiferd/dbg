    \abstract{\textbf{Background:} The consumption of main memory resources is a significant burden in $k$-mer-based metagenomic analysis when creating related databases but also when performing (unique) $k$-mer-counting and read classification. Genestrip addresses this issue by focusing on small but freely configurable groups of organisms. Regarding the selected organisms, Genestrip produces $k$-mer databases and results comparable to those of KrakenUniq but at a fraction of its required memory resources. Our tool ensures that during database generation, the most suitable lowest common ancestor taxon is assigned for each stored $k$-mer by \emph{also considering genomes of organisms whose $k$-mers are not included in the database}. This enables read analysis with high  precision and recall for the organisms of interest.

\todo{Rework to address false positives and $k$-mer compression, less repeat in the results section...}
    \textbf{Results:}  We assessed the correctness, usefulness and performance of Genestrip in different contexts and showed that it indeed ascertains high quality read classifications for organisms whose genomes were included in a corresponding database. Our example databases comprise millions to a few billions of $k$-mers covering a dozen to a few thousands of species and lend themselves to usage in tick surveillance, medical diagnostics or agriculture. All databases were generated on a regular PC within hours, and related analysis performance was competitive to highly favorable.
    The deliberate focus on a particular set of genera or species allowed for more genomes to be included from related organisms while the resulting databases remained small. We exemplify, that such small but deep databases tend to improve recall during read classification while sustaining high precision.

    \textbf{Conclusions:}  Due to Genestrip's particular way of updating the $k$-mers' lowest common ancestor taxa, both database creation and fastq file analysis can be realized with little memory and with favorable runtimes as well as high classification quality. So both, database creation \emph{and} read classification may be performed even on regular PCs. Genestrip's qualities empower users to flexibly design, build and use small $k$-mer databases for their own needs with potentially deep genomic coverage.

    \section{Background}\label{background}

    \subsection{Introduction}
\label{problem}

    With the availability of low-cost and small-scale sequencing devices, metagenomic analysis will likely replace many established technologies for the detection and surveillance of specific groups of microorganisms. Related applications may affect various areas such as farming, ecology, food processing and medicine. While sequencing devices have become ready for ubiquitous use, software tools for metagenomics still tend to be resource intensive and too hard to use for end users.

    To analyze large sets of reads, classifications tools based on $k$-mer matching and counting such as Kraken 1 \citep{kraken}, Kraken 2 \citep{kraken2}, KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq}, Ganon \citep{Piro:2020aa, 10.1093/nargab/lqaf094} and others are at hand. With regard to pathogen detection, KrakenUniq is particularly suited because of its very low false positive rate but also because of its efficiency and high sensitivity \citep{krakenUniq}.

In order to avoid false positive classifications of reads, KrakenUniq resorts to very large databases, containing billions of encoded but uncompressed $k$-mers along with their respective taxa. A database is usually loaded entirely into main memory and often consumes tens of gigabytes of space. This requirement mandates specialized and expensive compute servers with very large main memory.

    In recognition of this problem, KrakenUniq has been extended for database processing in chunks at read analysis time \citep{lowmemkraken}. However, database creation still mandates ultralarge main memory: For example, in 2018 the production of a database for Kraken 1 and, equivalently, KrakenUniq that just covers the bacterial genomes from the RefSeq \citep{refseq} ``required ca. 2.5 TB of RAM and ca. 11 days" on a high-performance computer \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. But since then, the RefSeq's number of prokaryotic genomes alone has increased by a factor of 5.8 to over 48 Mio. \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038}.
    Therefore, related organisms are often not covered below the species rank and also genomes below the quality level ``complete genome" are usually not included in a corresponding database.

    Despite the fact that these databases may not cover organisms of interest at the necessary genomic depth, users tend to apply them because otherwise, creating even larger databases would require even more main memory \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. So, although a much richer set of genomes for organisms of interest may be available in principle -- for example via recent versions of the RefSeq or in addition via Genbank \citep{genbank} -- these genomes cannot easily be leveraged for metagenomic analysis \citep{Cuesta-Zuluaga:2020aa,Youngblut:2021aa}.
    
    Even though Kraken 2 offers more memory efficient $k$-mer databases, \emph{it is not a suitable alternative in fields such as medical diagnostics where precision of read classification is paramount} \citep{lowmemkraken}. This is because in comparison with Kraken 1 and KrakenUniq, Kraken 2 produces smaller databases, but it compresses stored $k$-mers under loss of information, therefore facilitating false positive $k$-mer counts \citep{kraken2, lowmemkraken}. Many recent $k$-mer analysis tools such as Ganon follow this idea and trade $k$-mer compression against potentially false positive $k$-mer matches.

    We focus on a comparison with KrakenUniq because Genestrip aims to provide a similar $k$-mer based functionality by avoiding $k$-mer compression altogether while still enabling small databases without substantial loss of precision and recall. In addition, we include Kraken 2 and Ganon (Version 2.1) in our evaluations as representatives of $k$-mer compressing systems.
  
    \subsection{Approach}
    \label{approach}

    If only a smaller group of a few dozen genera or a few 100 species is under consideration, then it seems promising to simply \emph{generate a smaller database that contains just the $k$-mers from respective genomes}. \emph{However, without additional measures, this very likely leads to many false positives}: The reason for this is that owing to the evolutionary relationship of organisms, \emph{a large fraction of $k$-mers that belong to the genome of a species are unspecific} in the sense that they can as well be found in the genomes other species or genera not considered for analysis. Thus, when using too small a database, a read may be falsely assigned to a species because it could consist of $k$-mers that \emph{also} belong to another species' genome not included in the database.

    In order to enable a smaller database with no false positives emerging from this problem, we introduce Genestrip, as new metagenomic software tool supporting (unique) $k$-mer counting, read classification and read filtering.

    In principle, Genestrip employs a \emph{two-phase} database generation process: During the \emph{entry phase}, all $k$-mers of genomes from selected organisms are entered into the database on the basis of the RefSeq \citep{refseq} and optionally GenBank \citep{genbank} as well as additional sources. During the \emph{update phase}, all stored $k$-mers from the database are checked against the genomes of \emph{all} other organisms from the RefSeq belonging to the same \emph{category}\footnote{By standard, the RefSeq is structurally divided into 14 categories including ``Bacteria", ``Fungi", ``Invertebrate", ``Protozoa", ``Viral" and others.}. So, if a stored $k$-mer also occurs in another organism which is not part of the database, the $k$-mer's \taxon\ still gets updated in the database and the lowest common ancestor's \taxon\ (known as LCA) is entered instead \citep{Nasko:2018aa}. This prevents a $k$-mer from being falsely associated with one of the selected organisms given that it is too unspecific.

    Based on this approach, Genestrip achieves a low memory footprint during database creation as well as at read analysis time. Moreover, it offers competitive to highly favorable runtimes for $k$-mer matching and read classification. Together, these qualities empower users to create and use respective databases on machines with regular main memory size. Genestrip's $k$-mer counting results are similar to those of KrakenUniq \citep{krakenUniq} with respect to the selected organisms. Genestrip also supports unique $k$-mer counting with exact counts and with low memory overhead.

    In the results section of this paper, we demonstrate the usefulness of our approach by introducing a small Genestrip database for tick-borne bacterial pathogens. An envisioned application is tick surveillance via metagenomics as reported in \citep{nanoporeticks}. We performed analyses of tick-related fastq files on a regular PC in less than a minute. Thanks to the small database's high genomic coverage for the organisms of interest, we achieved considerably better recall than with a large standard database under \todo{Comment on other K2 and Ganon here too} KrakenUniq. This means we found more $k$-mers and matching reads for such organisms while also sustaining high precision. As part of our performance experiments, we briefly present further small databases that may prove useful in agriculture and infectious disease diagnostics.

    Genestrip is written in Java, has a small code base and supports command-line-based as well as API-based usage. Based on the Java Virtual Machine, it can be executed on  modern operating systems including Linux, Windows and macOS.

    \section{Implementation}\label{methods}

    \subsection{System structure}
\label{system}

    Genestrip's database generation mandates the setting of groups of organisms whose genomes will be exclusively represented in a resulting database. Users choose the desired genera, species or strains via a configuration file by listing the corresponding taxa. If higher ranked taxa are entered, then by default, \emph{all} of the descending taxa will be included and $k$-mers of their genomes will be added to the database accordingly.

    Genestrip generates a database for a selected group of organisms primarily via
    genomes from the RefSeq \citep{refseq} but genomes from Genbank and additional genomic files may be included on top or instead. By default, related genomic files are only downloaded once to a common folder and shared when further Genestrip databases are to be generated.
    
    To better handle the complexity of the generation process, it employs a goal-oriented approach similar to Make \citep{feldman1979make}: Upon access, each goal returns a file or an object in main memory. A goal is executed only if its result is not yet available. I.e., in case of a file goal, the system first checks whether the corresponding file is present and only if it is missing, the corresponding goal is executed to produce the file. 
    
    Goal execution may involve the access of other goals which in turn, may trigger their execution. Respective goal dependencies are explicitly specified in terms of a directed acyclic graph. Details on the system's goals and the goal graph can be found in Section \ref{ggg} of the Appendix.

    \subsection{Database generation}
    \label{database}

    Genestrip stores a database primarily as a large array $L$ of sorted long values. Each long value encodes a $k$-mer from the genome of a selected organism. The default for $k$ is $31$ but smaller values and $k=32$ are possible.
    In order to handle reverse complements of reads later at read analysis time, stored $k$-mers are standardized in the following way: Each $k$-mer is encoded twice as a long value --- once in its canonical version and once via its reverse complement --- but only the larger of the two encoded values is stored in the database. A second array $T$ with the same entry size as $L$ encodes taxa for each $k$-mer in $L$ at corresponding positions. At read analysis time, lookups in $L$ are performed via a binary search.

    All parts of the NCBI taxonomy tree \citep{taxonomy} required at analysis time are also included in  the database. The taxonomy extract comprises entries for the selected organisms and all of their ancestors. At analysis time, a bit vector with the same entry size as $L$ indicates which $k$-mers from $L$ were found at least once. This is sufficient for exact unique $k$-mer counting and is space-efficient, as it requires only about 1.6\% of additional main memory with respect to $L$.

     Genestrip employs one Bloom filter \citep{bloomfilter} at \emph{database generation time} with a very low FPP. While the database gets filled, it tracks the already stored $k$-mers to avoid duplicates. Since $L$ is not sorted at that point in time, the filter offers an efficient containment check.\footnote{More recent probabilistic filters such as Xor filters \citep{10.1145/3376122} or a Ribbon filters \citep{9920402} do not apply there since their construction demands all $k$-mers to be known upfront, whereas at database generation time, the $k$-mers stream in one by one from the genomic files being read.}

    A so-called Blocked Bloom filter with a rather high FPP of almost 1\% gets constructed after $L$ got sorted. It is exclusively used for performance optimization \emph{at read classification time}:\footnote{Here, we chose a Blocked Bloom filter as characterized in \cite{10.1145/3376122} since its query performance is superior to all other filters evaluated there. The filter's memory overhead is tolerable considering $L$ and $T$ together are eight times larger in bits.} The filter is consulted prior to a $k$-mer lookup in $L$ to check whether the $k$-mer might be in $L$. \emph{A negative result avoids a considerably more expensive binary search in $L$ and therefore saves overall execution cost.} We compared the average access time to look up a random $k$-mer in Genestrip's $k$-mer store with and without the Blocked Bloom filter being activated: Depending on the size of the database the filter brought an improvement of about factor 9 for very small databases to roughly factor 11 for larger databases. So, at read analysis time, this filter merely improves efficiency but does not cause false positives, since a hit is always backed by $L$. Employing the filter pays off because in practice, many $k$-mers from reads are likely not stored in a typical database.\footnote{For example, in the case of detecting infections, related samples typically contain a lot of host-DNA leading to many $k$-mers from the host in reads, whereas a related database typically would contain the pathogens $k$-mers only.}
    
    Regarding database generation, additional genomes can be manually added either to create deeper databases or, else, to filter out contaminating sequences that are mistakenly included in the genome of an organism of interest. For example, \cite{Breitwieser:2019aa} reported that human DNA fragments often  contaminate bacterial genomes for various reasons whereas \cite{Lu:2018aa} discussed a pipeline to remove contaminants from genomes of eukaryotic pathogens.
With Genestrip, $k$-mers from contaminating sequences can be identified during the update phase as introduced in Section \ref{approach}: For example, the human genome can be included via configuration in the update phase only; thus, a $k$-mer from the human genome that is also found in the contaminated genome will be pushed to the lowest common ancestor taxon of both species. Still, as the human genome is employed during the update phase only, a resulting database does not include $k$-mers from the human genome (other than those from contaminating sequences). This ensures a small-sized database.

    Of note, Genestrip allows for the exclusion of $k$-mers with low complexity known as genomic dust \citep{doi:10.1089/cmb.2006.13.1028}: As part of the entry phase, each $k$-mers's repetitiveness in terms of base pairs is measured via a fibonacci-based function. If the corresponding value exceeds a configurable threshold then the $k$-mer is dropped. Section \ref{dustfunction} in the Appendix presents our dust function in detail.

    \subsection{Read classification}
\label{readclassification}

    The goal \verb|match| loads a database and performs the read classification on fastq files.
    The principal classification algorithm is similar to that from  Kraken 1 \citep{kraken} and equivalently KrakenUniq. When iterating over a read's $k$-mers, each $k$-mer is encoded as a long -- once by considering its canonical version and once via its reverse complement.\footnote{Considering both versions is necessary because sequenced reads may occur in either direction but also because genomes may be represented in reverse complement direction, such as in the case of circular DNA.} For efficiency, only the larger of the two encoded values is looked up in the database. This is sufficient since, if at all, then only just the larger of two values got stored in the first place during database generation (see Section \ref{database}).

    Genestrip implements a multithreading enabled classification procedure following a producer consumer pattern, where a producer thread reads a fastq file and puts reads into an in-memory queue while consumer threads pick reads from the queue in order to classify them.

    Three types of error thresholds can be configured for read classification:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The ratio or number of $k$-mers per read that must at least be found in the database and
        \item the ratio or number of $k$-mers per read that must be consistent with the read's designated class taxon. A $k$-mer is consistent with a read's class taxon if the $k$-mer's taxon as stored in the database is the same or if it is a taxonomic ancestor of the class taxon.
        \item  Moreover, the classification threshold \verb|minKMersForClass| supports the reduction of potential false positive classifications. It can be set to adjust the minimal sum of $k$-mers under taxon $t$ required for a read to be classified to $t$. I.e., given a read $r$ and taxon $t_1$ on the genus rank with two $k$-mers in $r$ and taxon $t_2$ subordinate to $t_1$ on the species rank with one $k$-mer in $r$. Furthermore, $r$ shall contain no other $k$-mers matching any taxons. Then, if \verb|minKMersForClass=2|, $r$ would \emph{not} be classified to $t_1$ but to $t_2$ instead since the single $k$-mer under $t_1$ is below the threshold but the sum of three $k$-mers under $t_2$ is sufficient. 
    \end{enumerate}
    
    The thresholds allow for playing off recall against precision in light of potentially high error rates of some sequencing devices \citep{Delahaye:2021aa,Liu-Wei:2024aa}. For all the experiments throughout this paper, the first two thresholds were left at the default of one $k$-mer which is the most sensitive level.

We use the following notation to describe them: Let $k_{t,f,d}$ be the number of $k$-mers for \taxon\ $t$ found in fastq file $f$ with respect to database $d$ and let $u_{t,f,d}$ be the corresponding number of unique $k$-mers. Similarly, let $u_{t,d}$ be the number of (unique) $k$-mers for $t$ stored in $d$.

Moreover, normalized values derived from the original values by dividing by $u_{t,d}$ are included where suitable. Normalized values aim to reduce counting bias due to a high or low representation of a \taxon\ $t$ in terms of its stored $k$-mers $u_{t,d}$.

    \subsection{Example Usage}
    
    We give a brief impression on how to use Genestrip from the Unix command line.\footnote{For detailed information see: \url{https://github.com/pfeiferd/genestrip/blob/master/README.md}} The installation includes a sample project \verb|human_virus|. After building Genestrip, you may call
\begin{lstlisting}[language=sh, basicstyle=\ttfamily]
sh ./bin/genestrip.sh human_virus dbinfo
\end{lstlisting}
from the installation directory to generate the \verb|human_virus| database and also, to create a CSV file with basic information about the database content. 

As stated in Section \ref{system}, Genestrip follows a goal-oriented approach in order to create any result files. So, when generating the \verb|human_virus| database, Genestrip will automatically
\begin{enumerate}
	\item 
    download the NCBI taxonomy and unzip it to \verb|./data/common|, if not yet present,
    \item
    download the RefSeq release catalog to \verb|./data/common/refseq|, if not yet present,
    \item
    download all virus related RefSeq fna files to \verb|./data/commmon/refseq| (which is currently just a single g-zipped file), if not yet present,
    \item
    perform several follow-up goals, until the database file \verb|human_virus_db.zip| is finally made under \verb|./data/projects/human_virus/db| and
    \item
    create a CSV file \verb|human_virus_dbinfo.csv| under \verb|./data/projects/human_virus/csv|, which contains basic information about the database, i.e. the number of $k$-mers stored per taxon.
\end{enumerate}
The generated database comprises $k$-mers for all viruses whose taxons are listed in the configuration file \verb|./data/project/human_virus/taxids.txt|.

To try out classification, the installation comes with the small fastq file \verb|sample.fastq.gz| in \verb|./data/projects/human_virus/fastq|. One may trigger its analysis via
\begin{lstlisting}[language=sh, basicstyle=\ttfamily, breaklines=true, postbreak=\mbox{\textcolor{red}{$\hookrightarrow$}\space}]
sh ./bin/genestrip.sh human_virus match -f ./data/projects/human_virus/fastq/sample.fastq.gz
\end{lstlisting}
The resulting CSV file named \verb|human_virus_match_sample.csv| can then be found under \verb|./data/projects/human_virus/csv|.
    
    \section{Results}\label{results}

    \subsection{Correctness}
    \label{correctness}

    To assess Genestrip's correctness with regard to $k$-mer counting and read classification, we compared example databases and respective analysis results directly and in detail with those from KrakenUniq. 
    As part of our test procedure, we created database contents under both systems that were identical in terms of their genomic basis and their stored $k$-mers.
    We then applied the databases to suitable fastq files in order to compare the results between both systems.\footnote{For compatibility with KrakenUniq, we kept \texttt{minKMersForClass} from Section \ref{readclassification} at the default of \texttt{1}.} Highly similar results from this check mutually solidify the correctness of both systems since they had been developed completely independently. (In addition, Genestrip's correctness is also supported via extensive automated functional tests with high test coverage as part of its code base.)

    We chose all RefSeq genomes from the category ``Viral" for the two databases. The latter turned out to be completely identical in terms of the number of $k$-mers stored per taxon. In the following, we name the two databases \texttt{cv\_ku} and \texttt{cv\_g} with regard to KrakenUniq and Genestrip, respectively.

    We applied \texttt{cv\_ku} and \texttt{cv\_g} to a simulated fastq file extracted from \cite{Wu:2024aa} where the file's reads were used to benchmark metagenomic pipelines aimed at the discovery of new viruses.
The file contains 61,550 long reads extracted from 6,155 viral standard genomes from the RefSeq \citep{Wu:2024aa}. 
Furthermore, we generated simulated fastq files based on all RefSeq genomes from the category ``Viral" via the tool InSilicoSeq \citep{10.1093/bioinformatics/bty630}: We applied the respective Illumina generation models ``MiSeq" and ``HiSeq" to produce two pairs of fastq files with one million reads per pair. Then, we analyzed these files via \texttt{cv\_ku} and \texttt{cv\_g} as well.
In all of the cases the $k$-mer counts per taxon were identical between KrakenUniq and Genestrip and the classified read counts were also identical.
     
As real-world examples, we applied the two databases to short-read fastq files from DNA-sequencing human saliva of five different donors. Table \ref{errorsviral} shows basic statistics of these files as downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive \cite{10.1093/nar/gkab1053}.     \subsection{Classification via broad coverage databases}
    \label{broadcov}

As a baseline, we tested read classification quality using all of the simulated data introduced in Section \ref{correctness}. With the ground truth known, this allowed for comparing precision and recall across different $k$-mer-based analysis tools.

Regarding the databases, we reused \texttt{cv\_ku} and \texttt{cv\_g} from Section \ref{correctness} and generated analogous databases for Kraken 2 and Ganon, respectively. 
Ganon depends on an important hyper-parameter \verb|max-fp| that controls the false positive probability (FPP) for matching $k$-mers and must be set at database generation time. (A lowered FPP incurs a larger database.) We used the default value of 0.001 (combined with \verb|--filter-type hibf|) which is documented as highly sensitive in Ganon's online manual.\footnote{See \url{https://pirovc.github.io/ganon/default_databases/#filter-type-ibf-and-hibf}}

All analysis tools but KrakenUniq offer classification time options to trade sensitivity against confidence.     \subsection{Example: Tick-borne bacterial pathogens}
    \label{tickborne}

    This section presents the detection of tick-related bacterial pathogens as an example application of Genestrip. Once again, we compare our tool against KrakenUniq, Kraken 2 and Ganon and reuse or generate standard databases available for the latter systems. Regarding Genestrip, we employ a relatively small database \texttt{tb} with high genomic coverage for the organisms of interest.

    An envisioned application is tick surveillance via metagenomics according to \cite{nanoporeticks}, where the authors applied Nanopore technology to check eight field-collected ticks but back then relied on Kraken 2 to identify corresponding organisms. Due to \cite{HUGGINS2024100839}, \cite{Miao:2024aa} and \cite{Huggins:2024aa} we also see a use case when screening for tick-borne pathogens in vertebrates potentially including humans.

    \subsubsection{Analysis databases}
    \label{mdbtb}
    
    We discuss the databases used in conjunction with each system but with an emphasis on Genestrip's database \texttt{tb}.
    
    \texttt{MicrobialDB} \cite{microbialdb} for KrakenUniq aims at the diagnosis of infectious diseases using metagenomics and contains about 98 times as many $k$-mers as \texttt{tb}. It has a disk size of 574 GB and  covers a broad set of microbials including bacteria and the human genome with contaminants removed \citep{Lu:2018aa}. Regarding Kraken 2, we used the latest \texttt{Standard} database from \cite{microbialdb} covering the entire RefSeq category ``Bacteria", the human genome and more with a disk size of 73 GB and with about 18 billion compressed $k$-mers. For Ganon, we generated a database covering all the genomes of category ``Bacteria" from the RefSeq Release 233 using Ganon's default FPP (see also Section \ref{broadcov}) which resulted in a database size of 58 GB.
    %\footnote{The generation of an additional ``Ganon low FPP" database as in Section \ref{broadcov} would have exceeded the set main memory limit of 128 GB (see Section \ref{performance}). We therefore have no classification results available for this variant.} 

    The database \texttt{tb} for Genestrip is based on the RefSeq Release 233 and covers all RefSeq genomes under the twelve bacterial genera Bartonella, Rickettsia, Anaplasma, Ehrlichia, Borrelia, Borreliella, Chlamydia, Mycoplasma, Malacoplasma, Mycoplasmoides, Metamycoplasma and Francisella as many included species are pathogenic to humans and known or assumed to occur in ticks \citep{Eisen:2018aa, JOHNSON20181499, Janecek:2012aa, Thomas:1990aa, 10.3389/fvets.2025.1522904}. To avoid contaminants from human DNA and black-legged tick DNA, we added corresponding genomes to Genestrip's update phase as explained at the end of Section \ref{database}. We also activated the genomic dust filter at moderate sensitivity -- e.g., the $k$-mer \texttt{TTTTTTTTTTTTTCGGTGTTTTTTTGTTACT} would have been dropped because its repetitiveness is just above the set threshold.

    \texttt{tb} contains about 525 million uncompressed $k$-mers and has a disk size of just 3.91 GB including the accompanying Bloom filter and a suitable extract of the NCBI taxonomy tree. \texttt{tb} has 1.96 times as many $k$-mers stored under the selected genera and below as \texttt{MicrobialDB}. The main reason behind this increase is the general growth of the RefSeq's number of prokaryotic genomes between the generation of \texttt{MicrobialDB} in mid 2023 and the generation of \texttt{tb} \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038}.

    The generation of \texttt{tb} revealed that its included genomes from the RefSeq have a high overlap: On average each stored $k$-mer occurred 11 times in these genomes. Only 0.0079 \% of all $k$-mers considered for storing were dropped as ``dust". The update phase pushed 26.3 \% of the stored $k$-mers to ranks above genus. 0.027 \% of the stored $k$-mers had been pushed to the taxon of ``cellular organisms" because tick DNA and human DNA were included on purpose during the update phase for the sake for contaminant detection.
%Finally, 2.891 $k$-mers landed on the taxonomic root because of plasmids included in the bacterial part of RefSeq that overlap with the genomes of the selected genera.

\subsubsection{Analysis of simulated data}
\label{ticksim}

To assess classification quality for this case, we first generated ground truth data via the eight real-world fastq files from \cite{nanoporeticks} mentioned above. For this purpose, we employed the tool NanoSim from \cite{10.1093/gigascience/gix010, 10.1093/gigascience/giad013} which is capable of generating realistic long reads based on a set genomes and a sample fastq file used for training. The tool tries to reflect the genomic abundance of organisms from the training fastq file. Moreover, it imitates long read sequencing errors resulting from Nanopore technology via an error model which is also estimated on the basis of the training fastq file.

We supplied NanoSim with all the RefSeq genomes from the twelve bacterial genera mentioned in Section \ref{mdbtb} and used each of the eight tick-related real-world fastq files to generate  corresponding \emph{simulated} fastq files.
%\todo{what about the black-legged tick's genome?}
 The number of reads per simulated file ranges between 13,000 and 160,000 with 319,531 reads in total and an average read length of 4,480 BPs.

    \subsection{Performance}
    \label{performance}

    As mentioned in Section \ref{background}, Genestrip's focus is on generating and using potentially small but also deep $k$-mer databases for metagenomic analysis of selected organisms of interest. This implies that users must be empowered to flexibly create new databases -- ideally on their own devices. We therefore verify that Genestrip's database generation performance adheres to this requirement. Moreover, we report on read classification performance in comparison with KrakenUniq, Kraken 2 and Ganon.

  The following experiments were all performed on a modern PC with 128 GB of RAM, 10 CPU cores and a 2 TB solid state disk. Although for Genestrip about 32 GB of RAM would have sufficed, we needed the additional RAM for the competing analysis tools.\footnote{We ensured that as few competing processes as possible were running during the experiments and OS disk swapping was disabled. Platform details: Intel Xeon W-2255 CPU @3.70 GHz, Ubuntu 24.04.4 LTS, Java Oracle 23.0.1 64-Bit Server VM.}

    \label{runtimeperf}

    To assess Genestrip's performance regarding $k$-mer counting and read classification, we compared its analysis runs with corresponding runs under KrakenUniq, Kraken 2 und Ganon.

    For the first comparison we applied the viral databases from  Section \ref{broadcov} to the human saliva fastq files from Table \ref{errorsviral}. Scenario (a) of Table \ref{analysisperf} summarizes the analysis performance across all five fastq files.
    
     In scenario (b) of Table \ref{analysisperf} we used the  bacterial databases like \texttt{MicrobialDB} and \texttt{Standard} from Section \ref{mdbtb} vs. \texttt{tb} to analyze the eight tick-related fastq files from Section \ref{tickanalysis}. On the one hand, comparing the tools this way may be considered ``unfair" because of unequally sized database. But for KrakenUniq, Kraken 2 and Ganon, there is no good alternative to employing broad-coverage bacterial databases for the purpose (of detecting tick-related bacterial pathogens) because, as examined in Section \ref{accuracy}, a corresponding small database bares the high risk of many false positives.
     Since \texttt{MicrobialDB} is so large, we had to make use of KrakenUniq's database processing in chunks which broke down each run into 9 rounds. This caused considerably longer run times under KrakenUniq.
     
     In scenario (c) we had the fastq files from (b) jointly analyzed. In this case, Genestrip performed even better because the database had to be loaded only once, whereas in scenario (b), it got loaded separately for each fastq file.

    Regarding main memory, all the tools' analysis performance is dominated by the size of the database. The required memory did not vary much across runs when the same database was used.
    
    Overall, the results from both comparisons suggest that depending on the use case, Genestrip  and its small databases offer competitive to highly favorable analysis performance.

    \section{Conclusions}\label{discussion}

    We have presented Genestrip -- a bioinformatics tool for (unique) $k$-mer counting and read classification. Its major improvement over existing systems is its very low memory consumption at database generation time and at read analysis time while also maintaining very high precision and recall. To achieve this, Genestrip facilitates and suggests the generation of small databases that cover the genomes of a collection of organisms of interest. Genestrip's update phase ensures that during database generation, the most suitable lowest common ancestor taxon is computed for each stored $k$-mer by \emph{also considering the genomes of organisms whose $k$-mers are not included in the database}. Normally, \emph{Genestrip considers all genomes from a matching category of the RefSeq for this purpose}.

    The results from Section \ref{correctness} indicate that Genestrip attains the same type of correctness as KrakenUniq in terms of database structure, $k$-mer counting and read classification given that the databases of both systems are filled via all genomes from an entire RefSeq category. But more importantly, Section \ref{accuracy} highlights that Genestrip sustains high classification quality when changing from a large, comprehensive database to a small database, although just a subset of the genomes out of a RefSeq category is included in that small database. In contrast, we showed in Section \ref{smalldbreal} that corresponding small databases under KrakenUniq, Kraken 2 or Ganon fail to achieve a suitable level of read classification quality. This is because the latter tools do not consider genomes outside of the database to be generated like Genestrip does during its update phase at database generation time.

    Section \ref{tickborne} has exemplified the usefulness of Genestrip's ``small databases" in the context of tick surveillance where ticks were screened for a collection of well-known bacterial pathogens via metagenomics \citep{nanoporeticks}. The resulting database \texttt{tb} has a disk size of less than 4 GB and can be used for analysis on any modern PC. Nevertheless, Genestrip offers the exactness of KrakenUniq's very large database \texttt{MicrobialDB} in terms of $k$-mer counting, unique $k$-mer analysis and read classification for the chosen genera. \todo{Talk about results on simulated data here.}

    Unlike Kraken 2 and Ganon, Genestrip also eliminates the potential for false positive $k$-mer counts because it does not compress $k$-mers under loss of information. This renders our tool suitable in fields such as medical diagnostics where precision of $k$-mer counting and read classification is paramount --- which was also a motive for evolving Kraken 1 into KrakenUniq \citep{lowmemkraken} after the same research group had already authored Kraken 2 \citep{kraken2}. \todo{Relate to results on simulated tick data here.}

    The deliberate focus on a particular set of genera or species allows for more genomes to be included for the organisms of interest while the resulting Genestrip database may still remain small. For example, the tick-related database \texttt{tb} from Section \ref{mdbtb} contains almost twice as many $k$-mers from the chosen genera than the latest publicly available large database \texttt{MicrobialDB} for KrakenUniq. As \texttt{tb} is deeper than \texttt{MicrobialDB} in this respect, it also tends to achieve better recall when analyzing tick related fastq files without loss of precision. We have presented and discussed the corresponding results as part of Section \ref{tickanalysis}.

    Regarding performance, we have examined \emph{database generation as well as fastq file analysis}: Section \ref{genperformance} has shown that Genestrip empowers users to flexibly create new databases on regular PCs in minutes to just a few hours. As summarized in Tables \ref{dbdesc} and \ref{dbperf}, we created databases with different applications in mind where the generation of \texttt{tb} took the longest with still less than 9 hours while main memory consumption remained below 32 GB in all the cases.

    Let us contrast Genestrip's database generation performance with the corresponding resources required under KrakenUniq: As mentioned in Section \ref{problem}, \cite{Nasko:2018aa} stated that generating a full bacterial database for Kraken 1 and so equivalently for KrakenUniq from the RefSeq ``required ca. 2.5 TB of RAM and ca. 11 days" on a suitable computer. However, since then, the RefSeq's number of prokaryotic genomes alone has increased up by a factor of 5.8 to over 48 Mio. \citep{10.1093/nar/gkae1038}. The additional growth of the RefSeq is also a likely reason why \texttt{MicrobialDB} has not been updated anymore since 2023 because ultralarge main memory and extremely high computational power would be required for this. Overall, this development suggests that $k$-mer databases with very broad and comprehensive coverage (such as by including \emph{all} genomes from the RefSeq category ``Bacteria") have reached a ``dead-end" --- at least when a lossless storing of $k$-mers is desired.

    In Section \ref{runtimeperf} we examined Genestrip's analysis performance: When the database structure under KrakenUniq and Genestrip was similar then the performance was similar too with KrakenUniq offering about 33\% better runtimes.
     However, when the use case for Genestrip's small databases was met, then the database size played off with regard to the analysis time: E.g., when analyzing tick-borne bacterial pathogens via the database \texttt{tb}, Genestrip was more than ten times faster than KrakenUniq because on a regular PC, KrakenUniq's large database \texttt{MicrobialDB} has to be processed in chunks over several iterations. Regarding main memory, Genestrip's analysis performance is dominated by the size of the database itself. E.g, it remained below 9 GB of RAM under the database \texttt{tb} which has a disk size of about 6 GB.

    In use cases where metagenomics is meant to be performed with very few restrictions on what organisms to look out for, then Genestrip may not the right choice. Although it is certainly possible to create large databases under Genestrip, one would end up with similar challenges as with KrakenUniq (see above). If exactness of results is required in addition to broad coverage, then a two-stage analysis procedure might prove useful: In stage one, a tool such as Kraken 2 with a compressed database for broad coverage may be applied to identify candidate organisms of interest. In stage two, Genestrip might be used to confirm and ``drill down" the initial findings by potentially first generating and then using a dedicated database.
