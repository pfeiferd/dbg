    \section{Introduction}\label{introduction}
    A metagenomic classifier does not answer a question so much as produce a table. Kraken~2 reports,
    per taxon, the reads assigned to it and the reads assigned at or below it \citep{kraken2};
    KrakenUniq adds the number of \emph{distinct} $k$-mers seen for a taxon, which is what separates a
    genuine hit from a few reads landing by chance \citep{krakenuniq}; Genestrip reports $k$-mer
    counts, unique $k$-mer counts, read counts and a consistency score beside one another
    \citep{genestrip-pub}. Each column earns its place and each answers a real question, and the
    $k$-mer columns earn theirs by measurement: thresholded on the same ten million reads, the
    distinct-$k$-mer count recovers $0.85$ of the species present where the read count recovers
    $0.76$, because reads piled onto one short region raise a read count without covering much of a
    genome \citep{krakenuniq}. Together they
    are hard to read, and the difficulty is not that there are several of them.

    Table \ref{motivating} shows the shape of it. The rows carrying the most evidence are typically
    the ones that name no organism: the reads at a genus came from some mixture of the species
    beneath it, and the table says neither which nor how many are in play. The columns need not agree
    with each other either. A taxon whose reads are mostly shared sequence yields few $k$-mers of its
    own for each read assigned to it, where a well-distinguished one yields many, so which taxon
    looks dominant can depend on which column is read; and the share of $k$-mers that are distinct
    moves with repeat content and with depth. The figures also do not compare across samples, since all
    of them scale with how much was sequenced. Reading such a table is a skill, exercised through
    conventions --- a minimum read count here, a minimum unique-$k$-mer count there --- whose
    thresholds are agreements among practitioners rather than statements about the sample.

    The deeper trouble is that a count at a node is not a count of an organism. The lowest common
    ancestor rule that makes a classifier precise also sends the $k$-mers an organism shares with its
    relatives up the tree, so a count high in the taxonomy is fed by several organisms at once and
    names none of them. By the time one reads a genus the frequencies of its species have mixed, and
    the mixture is exactly what the table does not resolve. Even where a count does name one organism
    it is not an abundance: it is a share of $k$-mers, and therefore of sequence, subject to how long
    the genome is and to how much of it the database happens to hold.

    One thing is missing altogether. No column says how much of the sample the database could not
    name --- an organism absent from the reference contributes its $k$-mers to whichever relatives it
    resembles, silently, and nothing in the output marks the difference between a well-covered sample
    and a poorly covered one.

    A reader without the expertise to reconcile those columns wants the quantity the table is
    evidence for and does not state: the distribution over the organisms that produced the counts.
    That is what this paper estimates, together with the share it cannot attribute to anything the
    database names.

    Throughout, $d$ is a $k$-mer database over an underlying taxonomy whose nodes are written $n$,
    holding the $k$-mers of a set of genomes $g$. Each $k$-mer is tied to a node by the lowest common
    ancestor rule: it is held at the lowest taxon shared by every genome that carries it. The rule
    was introduced for reads by \citet{megan} and carried to $k$-mers by \citet{kraken}, as an index
    built once and consulted per read; this paper calls the operation the $\mathrm{LCA}$ update.

    Figure \ref{pngfig} puts the whole of it in one picture. Panel (1) is what $d$ contributes. Its
    genomes $g$ sit at the leaves of the taxonomy, and each of them holds a distribution
    $p(n \mid g)$ over the nodes $n$, drawn here as a coloured band whose width at a node is the
    share of that genome's $k$-mers $d$ keeps there. The distribution is supported on the path from
    the genome's own node up to the root, by the $\mathrm{LCA}$ update, and it sums to one over that
    path. Where paths meet, the bands are drawn alongside
    one another rather than merged, so the total width at a node is what a count observed there has
    to be explained by. It is these distributions that carry the information and not the shape of the
    tree: $g_3$ is the only genome under $n_2$, so almost nothing of it is kept there and its band
    passes through in a waist.

    Each $p(n \mid g)$ is measured once, by consulting the genome behind $g$ in the $k$-mer
    database, and then stays fixed. The same distributions serve every later sample whose counts
    that database produced, so measuring them belongs to building a database and not to analysing a
    sample.

    Panel (2) is what a sample contributes: the $k$-mer counts $c_n$, as dots at the nodes where
    they were made. They carry no colour, and that is the whole of the difficulty --- a count says how many
    $k$-mers were seen at a node and never which genome they came from. They have to come from the
    very database the distributions were measured on, for the reason Section \ref{model} gives.
    Panel (3) is what is wanted, the same dots coloured by the genome they are owed to.

    The arrow between the two is the estimate, which Section \ref{model} states precisely. The
    sample drawn here holds $g_1$ and $g_3$ and no $g_2$, and the empty node under $g_2$ settles
    $n_1$ as well: those counts could have come from either genome below it, and with $g_2$ absent
    they are $g_1$'s. Only $n_{\text{root}}$ stays divided, which is the general case --- the higher
    the node, the more genomes feed it.

    \section{Related work}\label{relatedwork}
    Topic modeling was devised for text. A corpus is a set of documents and each document a bag of
    words, their order discarded. The model supposes a small number of \emph{topics}, each a
    distribution over the vocabulary, and describes every document as a mixture of them: a paper on
    protein folding draws heavily on one topic, lightly on a second, and the words it contains are
    the evidence for both. Fitting the model recovers the two together --- what the topics are, and
    how much of each a document holds. Its appeal is that a word belonging to several topics is not
    forced into one of them. It is explained as a blend, in the proportions that best account for the
    document as a whole.

    That is the shape of the problem here. A sample is a document, and the taxonomy nodes at which a
    classifier reports counts are its words; the organisms that shed DNA into it are the topics, and
    we want to know how much of the sample came from each. A $k$-mer sitting at a genus is a word
    that several topics use, and the mixture resolves it --- not by choosing an organism for it,
    but by asking which proportions account for the whole table at once.

    Probabilistic topic modeling begins with probabilistic latent semantic analysis
    \citep{hofmann1999plsa}, which fits a document as a mixture over topics by maximum likelihood,
    and with latent Dirichlet allocation \citep{blei2003lda}, which places the mixtures under a
    Dirichlet prior and makes the model generative. Maximum-a-posteriori estimation of that model was
    derived by \citet{defreitas2001bayesian} and \citet{chien2008adaptive}; it is Hofmann's fit with
    the prior entering as a pseudo-count. Of the inference methods that followed, collapsed Gibbs sampling
    \citep{griffiths2004scientific} is the one this paper reaches for; \citet{asuncion2009smoothing}
    compare it against the others systematically and give the hyperparameter re-estimation step built
    on \citet{minka2000dirichlet} that is known as Minka's update.
    \citet{pfeifer2019topicgrouper} set out the same correspondence between documents, words and
    topics that this paper carries over to samples, taxonomy nodes and genomes.

    The analogy stops at the point that makes this problem the easier one. Fitting a topic model to a
    corpus means learning the topics and the per-document mixtures together, and it is the first of
    those that makes the objective non-convex and the answer dependent on where the fit was started.
    Here the topics are not learned at all. Where a taxon's $k$-mers are stored follows from the
    database, which is fixed before any sample is seen, so what remains is only the per-document
    mixture --- the analogue of inference for an unseen document against an already-fitted model,
    not of fitting one. Section \ref{model} draws the consequence: the objective is concave, and an
    iteration reaches its maximum wherever it starts.

    Topic modeling has reached metagenomics before, but at a different level. The larger body of work
    treats a sample as a document and a \emph{taxon} as a word, and learns topics that turn out to be
    assemblages of co-occurring organisms: \citet{hosoda2020assemblage} recover microbial assemblages
    of the human gut this way, and \citet{kim2023environmental} sub-communities of environmental
    microbiomes. A smaller body takes the $k$-mers themselves as words ---
    \citet{zhang2015binning} to bin metagenomic reads, \citet{borrayo2020kmertopic} to associate
    whole genomes with bacterial families. In both, the topics are learned from the corpus and mean
    whatever the data makes them mean. Here they are
    neither: the words are the \emph{nodes} at which a database stores $k$-mers, so the vocabulary
    carries the ambiguity of the lowest-common-ancestor update rather than discarding it, and the
    topic--word distribution is computed from the database before any sample is seen rather than
    fitted to one.

    The nearer relative is not a topic model at all. Bracken \citep{lu2017bracken} estimates species
    abundance from the output of a read classifier by the same insight this paper starts from: that
    where a classifier puts the material of a known genome is a property of the reference and can be
    determined in advance. It simulates reads of length $R$ across each genome, records how the
    classifier assigns them, and obtains $P(G_j \mid S_i)$, the probability that a read of species
    $S_i$ is classified at ancestor $G_j$. Reads stranded at a higher node are then redistributed to
    the species beneath it by Bayes' rule, in one pass, with the species prior taken from the reads
    the classifier assigned uniquely.

    Three differences matter, and they are what this paper is for. The distribution is of a different
    thing: $P(G_j \mid S_i)$ describes where a \emph{read} would be \emph{classified}, so it depends
    on the read length and on the classifier's rule and has to be obtained by simulation, whereas
    $p(n \mid g)$ describes where the database \emph{stores} a genome's $k$-mers and is counted
    directly. The estimate is arrived at differently: a single application of Bayes' rule with a prior
    from uniquely-assigned reads, against the maximisation of one likelihood over the whole
    simplex --- so the ambiguous evidence sets the answer here rather than only being apportioned by
    it. And Bracken is explicit that it cannot report what its reference lacks: \enquote{if Kraken
    fails to identify a species (e.g., if the species was missing from the Kraken database), Bracken
    too will not identify that species.} The topics Section \ref{pgn} admits above the data taxa
    exist for exactly that case.

    The correction that turns a count into an abundance is older than the mixture models that use it.
    GAAS \citep{gaas} states the reason plainly: a metagenome is a set of fragments drawn from the
    genomes present, so at equal abundance a longer genome yields more of them, and an estimate that
    does not divide by genome length reports length as though it were abundance. Its normalisation is
    the same division Section \ref{abundances} performs, arrived at from BLAST similarities weighted
    by E-value rather than from stored $k$-mers, and its second output --- the average genome length
    of a community --- is the quantity a mixture needs and cannot always obtain.

    The line this paper stands in begins with GRAMMy \citep{grammy}, which put genome relative
    abundance as a finite mixture and fitted it by expectation--maximisation. It reaches that mixture
    from statistics and not from text: the word \emph{topic} does not appear in it, and nothing is
    borrowed from the corpus literature Section \ref{relatedwork} opens with. Its components are the
    reference genomes themselves, one per genome and none for a taxon --- where here a taxon can be a
    topic in its own right, which is what lets a clade account for what its genomes cannot. The set
    is fixed before the fit rather than searched over, as it is here, and unlike metaMix below. The
    taxonomy enters only afterwards, genome-level estimates being combined upward to give species and
    genus figures, which is the step Equation \eqref{smoothyagg} repeats here for the same reason.

    The first difference is where the mixture weights come from, and it decides what each method
    needs to be given. GRAMMy does the read assignment itself: it takes the read set and the
    reference genomes, aligns the reads or compares $k$-mer \emph{composition} profiles, and builds a
    matrix of probabilities that read $i$ came from genome $j$, which is what the fit then consumes.
    The assignment is soft rather than hard --- that is the point of it, and what GRAMMy shows to be
    worth doing --- but it is read classification all the same, carried out inside the estimator and
    repeated for every sample. Simulation appears in that paper only to benchmark the result, not to
    obtain the probabilities, which is where Bracken differs from it in turn.

    This method does none of that. It begins after a classifier has run and reads only what the
    classifier reported: $k$-mer counts at taxonomy nodes, which are decisions already taken and
    written down. It never sees a read, never aligns anything and needs no reference sequence at
    analysis time --- $p(n \mid g)$ comes from the database and is counted once for all samples. The
    input is a table rather than a read set, so the method composes with any classifier that produces
    one, and what it adds to an existing pipeline is a fit over that table.

    The cost of standing downstream is that the classifier's decisions are inherited, errors
    included, and no fitting recovers a $k$-mer the classifier placed wrongly. What is gained is that
    the ambiguity the classifier could not resolve --- the counts left high in the tree --- is
    precisely what the fit is given to work on, rather than being resolved twice by two different
    rules. GRAMMy's $k$-mer option should not be read as this method tried and found wanting, either:
    there a genome is a frequency distribution over short words and a read is scored by a multinomial
    pseudo-likelihood under it \citep{sandberg2001composition}, where a $31$-mer here is an address,
    and what separates two genomes is which $k$-mers they hold and where the update placed them.

    The second difference is the length correction, and GRAMMy has it in the right place. The
    abundance vector is a transformation of the mixing parameters that divides out genome length, in
    the manner GAAS set out, so the correction sits inside the model rather than being applied to its
    output --- as it does here. What differs is where the length comes from. GRAMMy takes it as an
    input, supposing the genome sizes known, which is why its $k$-mer variant loses the correction
    altogether: \enquote{there is no genome size bias correction if `k-mer' method is used without
    prior knowledge of genome lengths.} Here the divisor is $R_g$, which the goal of
    Section \ref{pgn} produces on the way to the fractions, so a $k$-mer method keeps the correction
    instead of forfeiting it.

    That is also what separates the two on the unknown component, and the reason is worth quoting.
    GRAMMy admits a $(K+1)$-th component for \enquote{the collective of unknown genomes}, and then:
    \enquote{Since the effective genome length for the unknown genomes is not available, we cannot
    estimate the relative abundance of the unknown component.} The unnamed material enters the model
    and is left unquantified. Here it needs no component of its own: the $k$-mers of a genome the
    database never entered are held at the lowest taxon it shares with something stored, so they are
    that taxon's own counts and a topic there accounts for them. What that topic then needs is a
    length, and $R_t$ is one --- not looked up but counted off the database, for a node as readily as
    for a genome --- so the quantity GRAMMy could not reach is the one this paper reports as $u$. What metaMix puts in its place is taken up below.

    metaMix \citep{metamix} is nearer still in form, and its likelihood is similar in shape to
    Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood}: $P(X \mid w) = \prod_i \sum_j p_{ij}\, w_j$ over reads $x_i$ and
    species $j$, a product over observations of a sum over sources. The resemblance stops at the
    index, since that product runs over reads and carries no exponent --- the difference
    Table \ref{modelstructure} turns on. Like GRAMMy it comes at the
    mixture from statistics and not from text, and like GRAMMy it does its own read assignment ---
    more of it, in fact. Its pipeline assembles the unmapped reads, strips ribosomal sequence against
    Silva and runs \textsc{blastx} of every read and contig against a protein database, from which
    $p_{ij} = \mathrm{Pois}(m; \lambda)/l_g$ follows out of the mismatch count. All of that is per
    sample, and none of it is what this method asks for.

    What it does with the ambiguity is the instructive part, because the aim is the same and the
    direction opposite. metaMix objects to the $\mathrm{LCA}$ convention as a way of placing
    \emph{reads}: assigning an ambiguous read to the lowest common ancestor of its candidates, as
    MEGAN \citep{megan} does, is \enquote{accurate on a higher taxonomic level} and no lower, so metaMix goes back
    to the reads and resolves them probabilistically to species. This method has no read assignment
    to object to. The same rule reaches it one level down, as the rule by which the database stores a
    $k$-mer at the lowest taxon shared by the genomes carrying it, and that is what $p(n \mid g)$
    measures. Both answer the push-up the $\mathrm{LCA}$ rule performs: one by re-deciding it read by
    read, the other by modelling where it puts a genome's $k$-mers and reading the counts that
    result as evidence.

    Two further differences follow. The first is what is inferred. metaMix estimates the species
    \emph{set} as well as the weights, exploring model space by parallel tempering and paying a prior
    penalty per species admitted; within a candidate set it fits $w$ by EM or by a Gibbs sampler, and
    reports the two as giving similar answers with EM the faster. That inner fit is what this paper
    does --- once, over a set the admission rule of Section \ref{pgn} has already fixed --- and the
    outer search is what it declines. The exchange is real and worth naming: metaMix returns a
    posterior probability that a species is present, where Section \ref{pgn} offers a threshold and
    nothing more.

    The second is the unknown, and here the three methods form a sequence. GRAMMy admits the
    component and cannot quantify it for want of a length. metaMix quantifies it by supplying one:
    the $p_{ij}$ of its unknown category are approximated by the reciprocal of \enquote{median genome
    length in the reference database}, a single figure standing for whatever is missing. This paper
    divides by $R_t$ over the genomes it pools --- measured, not assumed, and measured over the
    members of \emph{that clade} rather than over the whole collection. And where the \emph{unknown}
    is a single category, here it is one figure per node: unnamed material under
    \emph{Streptococcus} raises the counts on the \emph{Streptococcus} lineage and unnamed material
    under \emph{Nocardia} on the \emph{Nocardia} lineage, so metaMix can say how much of a sample it
    cannot name while this method can also say where.

    PathoScope \citep{pathoscope} belongs to the same family and shows how far it can be taken.
    It is a Bayesian missing-data mixture over reads and genomes, fitted by EM, with an indicator
    saying which single genome a read came from; it weighs each read by its mapping quality, and its
    reassignment deliberately penalises non-unique reads where unique ones are present, which is a
    rule about evidence rather than a term in a likelihood. It is built for the clinical question
    this paper's companion is built for --- identify the pathogen, attribute the strain --- and it
    counts an absent reference among the cases it must handle. The distances are the ones already
    named: it aligns the sample's reads itself and therefore needs them, its components are genomes
    with no place for a clade, and what it reports about material outside the database is which reads
    it could not place rather than how much of the sample they are.

    HTTMM \citep{httmm} is the closest of these in shape, and the closest to this paper in what it
    objects to. Its mixture has the leaves \emph{and} the internal nodes of the taxonomy tree as
    components together, added rank by rank from species upwards, on the ground taken here: a read
    that several relatives share is evidence about the clade, and the $\mathrm{LCA}$ rule disposes of
    it \enquote{heuristically \ldots without statistical support}. An internal node carries its own
    weight in the fit, so the answer can put a share of the sample at a genus without naming a
    species under it.

    The distance is the term that does the work. HTTMM needs a probability that a node $z$ produced a
    given read, and it builds one from the shape of the tree alone: $f_j(z) = 1/r$ for each of the
    $r$ genomes below $z$, and $0$ elsewhere. Each read arrives as its own profile $d_i$ of
    \textsc{blast} scores over the genomes it hits, normalised to sum to one, and the two profiles
    are compared,
    $\Pr(r_i \mid z) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_j \lvert d_{ij} - f_j(z) \rvert$, so a read spread
    evenly over exactly the members of $z$ scores well there. That uniform $f$ is the counterpart of
    $p(n \mid g)$, and it is uniform because nothing in the method measures it. The authors say so,
    calling it a probability that \enquote{may be inaccurately reflecting the reality} and naming its
    replacement as future work. Section \ref{pgn} is a replacement, reached from the other side: the
    database has already recorded, for every genome, how its $k$-mers fall across the nodes, so the
    profile HTTMM has to assume is available as a measurement.

    Two things follow. HTTMM forms a per-read quantity and runs \textsc{blast} over the sample, so it
    needs the reads. And the internal nodes both methods fit mean different things, which is the
    comparison to make carefully now that neither treats them as a special case. HTTMM's hold
    homology: weight at a genus says the method could not resolve a read among that genus's
    \emph{reference} genomes. Here the ambiguity is already spoken for --- a named genome's row has
    mass at every node above it, so the shared $k$-mers of the genomes actually present are explained
    by their own topics --- and what is left at the genus is what none of them accounts for, which is
    material the database cannot name. The same node, fitted the same way, reports irresolution there
    and novelty here. Reads matching nothing never enter HTTMM's mapping matrix, and its model has no
    component for them. HTTMM also reports its mixture
    weights $\Pr(z)$ as relative abundances directly, with no length division anywhere in the model,
    which is the conflation Section \ref{abundances} separates.

    One column of the table wants a word in advance, since seven of its eight rows say
    \enquote{reads}. Working from $k$-mer counts is not a compromise that the input forces.
    \citet{krakenuniq} set the two statistics against each other on the same data, thresholding each
    to decide which species a sample contains: with the false-discovery rate held at $5\%$, the
    distinct-$k$-mer count recovers $0.85$ of the species where the read count recovers $0.76$, and
    across their biological data sets recall rises by $3$--$12\%$ at species level and $4$--$9\%$ at
    genus level. The reason is the one this method also leans on --- reads concentrated on a short
    region inflate a read count without covering much of a genome, and the $k$-mers show it.

    Two qualifications belong with that figure. It is a statement about \emph{detection} and not
    about proportions, and it concerns \emph{distinct} $k$-mers where
    Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} consumes counts with multiplicity, so it bears on the admission
    rule of Section \ref{pgn} more directly than on the likelihood. What it does settle is the
    question the column raises. The other rows work from reads because their estimators need a
    probability tying an individual read to a genome, which is the third column and not the second;
    the preference is a consequence of the method and not a finding that reads carry more.

    The exponent is the difference that matters, and it is worth saying why this method needs one
    where the others do not. This method never sees an individual $k$-mer. What a classifier hands
    it is $c_n$, the number of occurrences at each node, and the occurrences behind that number are
    gone by the time it arrives. A likelihood over tokens is therefore not available to write, and
    one over types with the counts as exponents is the only form the input admits. Nothing is given
    up in that, the two being one product written over tokens and over types,
    %
    \begin{equation*}
        \prod_{i=1}^{M} \sum_{g \in G} \varphi_{n_i g}\, \theta_g
        \;=\;
        \prod_{n \in N_s} \Bigl( \sum_{g \in G} \varphi_{ng}\, \theta_g \Bigr)^{c_n} ,
    \end{equation*}
    %
    with $n_i$ the node of the $i$-th of the $M$ observations and
    $\varphi_{ng} = p(n \mid g)$. The grouping on the right is available because every $k$-mer at
    $n$ is interchangeable under the model: $p(n \mid g)$ is a property of the database, and says
    nothing about which occurrence is which.

    The others need no exponent, because nothing in their input repeats. Each read carries an
    emission row of its own --- an alignment score computed for that read against each genome --- so
    grouping reads by type would put every read in a class by itself and leave every count at $1$.
    The exponent would be the same product in longer notation. Neither could they adopt one: it
    presupposes an emission indexed by a vocabulary, and theirs is indexed by the observation. This
    is the trade of the third column of Table \ref{priorwork} seen from the other side. A per-read
    probability is what lets those methods assign reads, which is what they are built to do; it is
    also what obliges them to keep the reads.

    Against a topic model proper the difference lies elsewhere, and Section \ref{relatedwork} has
    already put it: LDA has the exponent, the bag-of-words likelihood being exactly a product over
    the vocabulary with the word counts as powers, but it learns $\varphi$ together with $\theta$
    over a corpus. Here $\varphi$ is measured on the database before any sample is seen, and one
    sample is one document scored against it.

    \subsection{The generative model}\label{model}
    Shotgun sequencing draws reads from the whole of a genome rather than from one locus, so to a
    first approximation every $k$-mer of an organism's genome is equally likely to turn up in the
    reads. It is the sampling model \citet{landerwaterman} built the coverage of a shotgun project
    on, and how nearly it holds is a property of the protocol rather than of sequencing as such.
    \citet{ross2013bias} measure coverage across four platforms and find library construction the
    significant influence on it, evenness varying from one platform to the next. Where a protocol
    reads native molecules the ground for a compositional bias largely goes: nanopore sequencing is
    not expected to show the GC bias in coverage at all, GC telling there on the error rate instead
    \citep{ontbias} --- which is Section \ref{error}'s concern and not this one. In this paper's own
    setting \citet{nanoporeticks} report the whole-genome half of the assumption, the reads they
    recover from field-collected ticks spanning each assembled pathogen genome, at varying depths of
    coverage. Both halves are the point --- across the whole genome, and only approximately evenly.
    That is the assumption the method rests on, and it is why the method does not apply to
    metabarcoding data as it stands --- Section \ref{amplicon} sets out what has to change for it to.

    Under it, the per-taxon counts a classifier reports are a mixture. Let $G$ be the topics --- the
    nodes of the taxonomy the sample gives evidence at, a topic standing for the genomes beneath its
    node and for one genome where that node is a leaf, all fixed in Section \ref{pgn} --- $N$ the
    nodes that survive the modelling-phase drop of Section \ref{pgn}, which is a property of the
    database alone, $N_s \subseteq N$ those of them the sample $s$ gives counts at, and $c_n$ the
    $k$-mer count that the analysis of a sample $s$ reports at node $n$. Let $p(n \mid g)$ be the
    probability that a $k$-mer drawn from the genomes behind topic $g$ is stored at node $n$ of that
    database, and $p(g \mid s)$ the share of $g$ in the sample --- the abundance we are after. A
    $k$-mer observed at $n$ may have come from any topic, so
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothylikelihood}
        \widehat{p}(g \mid s) \;=\; \argmax_{p(g \mid s)} \;\prod_{n \in N_s}
            \Bigl( \sum_{g \in G} p(n \mid g)\, p(g \mid s) \Bigr)^{c_n}.
    \end{equation}
    %
    The mixing is worst high in the tree, where a single node is fed by many genomes at once. That is
    exactly where a classifier's own answer says least, which is what the model has to offer: it
    reads the counts at the ambiguous nodes as evidence rather than discarding them. Figure \ref{pngfig} is
    Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} drawn over a tree of three genomes, its three panels the
    $p(n \mid g)$, the $c_n$ and the $p(g \mid s)$ of the equation in that order.

    The two inputs have to come from one and the same database, and the condition is worth stating
    rather than assuming. $p(n \mid g)$ describes where one particular build keeps a genome's
    $k$-mers: which genomes were entered, which taxa the tree carries, and where the update placed
    each $k$-mer. The $c_n$ are what classifying a sample against a build reports. Pair them across
    two builds and the rows describe a storage pattern the counts do not follow --- a genome entered
    in the one and not the other draws counts to nodes where its row has no mass, and the fit has to
    account for them out of whatever else is at hand. What is required is not merely the same
    reference genomes but the same build of them: the same $k$, the same taxonomy, and the same
    update scope. Section \ref{pgn} obtains $p(n \mid g)$ by walking the database itself, which is
    what makes the condition easy to meet rather than easy to overlook.
    A match result reports two counts per taxon --- the $k$-mers attributed to it and the reads
    classified to it --- and $c_n$ is the former. Both the reason for that and the cost of it are
    worth stating.

    The cost is that the $k$-mers of one read overlap and are therefore far from independent: a read
    of length $L$ contributes $L - k + 1$ of them and the likelihood counts each as an observation of
    its own. They do not all land at one node --- a read spans conserved sequence and specific
    sequence, so its $k$-mers are stored at several --- but they land on a single lineage, the update
    holding a $k$-mer of an organism at an ancestor of its taxon or at that taxon itself. Which nodes
    along the lineage are hit is informative and the $k$-mers differ there. But $\theta$ is about
    which lineage it is, and on that every $k$-mer of the read says the same thing.

    So the overcount depends on the question asked of the counts. For the shape along a lineage it is
    about $k$: consecutive $k$-mers share $k-1$ bases, so each step brings one new base and a read
    carries some $(L-k+1)/k$ items. For the choice between topics it is $L - k + 1$, a read being one
    draw from one organism however many nodes its $k$-mers touch. At $k = 31$ and $L = 150$ that is
    $31$ against $120$, and the larger figure is the one that governs $\theta$. This does not move the maximiser, since a factor common to every $c_n$
    cancels out of Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood}, but it overstates how much evidence there is
    wherever the magnitude of the counts is read rather than their ratios --- against a prior, in any
    interval derived from the posterior, and most of all in a sample with few reads to begin with,
    where a single read landing on an unusual locus can carry a node by itself.
    \citet{krakenuniq} reach the same factor from the other side. They report up to about $L - k$
    \emph{distinct} $k$-mers per read for a species that is genuinely present, each read landing at a
    fresh locus, and they draw from it the working rule that a threshold on $k$-mers has to sit
    several times above one on reads --- which is the admission rule of Section \ref{pgn}. Their
    count stops growing once a genome is covered, where the counts used here keep multiplicity and
    do not.

    Against that, $k$-mer counts use the whole of a read rather than a summary of it. A read informs
    several nodes at once --- its species-specific $k$-mers one, those it shares with a neighbour
    another, its core $k$-mers a third --- and the ambiguous part, which is the part this method
    exists to use, is kept rather than resolved away.

    Read counts are not the alternative they appear to be, and the objection is not that they are
    coarser. A read is placed at one node by the classifier's own rule, and that rule is built to name
    the most specific taxon the read supports, so it assigns systematically deeper than the nodes at
    which the read's $k$-mers actually sit --- for good reasons, since naming a genus where a species
    is warranted is the failure a classifier is designed to avoid. But $p(n \mid g)$ says where the
    database \emph{stores} the $k$-mers of $g$. It is not a statement about where a classifier would
    place a read of $g$. Pairing read counts with it would put an observation model and an emission
    distribution together that describe different processes, and the discrepancy is a systematic
    skew towards the deep nodes rather than noise, so it would not average away with more data.

    Using read counts would therefore require a different $p(n \mid g)$ altogether: the probability
    that a \emph{read} of $g$ is classified at $n$, which depends on the classifier's rule, on read
    length and on error rate. That is a property of the analysis and the sequencing run rather than
    of the database, so it could no longer be computed once and reused --- and it would have to be
    obtained by simulation, since nothing in the database implies it.

    The two inputs come from different places, and the difference matters. The $c_n$ describe one
    sample and are what a tool such as Genestrip already reports. The $p(n \mid g)$ describe the
    \emph{database} and not the sample: they are fixed once the database is built, so they are
    computed once and reused for every sample analysed against it.

    In the terms of Table \ref{correspondence}, $s$ is the document, $n$ the word, $g$ the topic,
    $c_n$ the count of a word in a document and $p(n \mid g)$ the topic--word distribution. Only
    $p(g \mid s)$ is inferred; $p(n \mid g)$ is read off the database, as Section \ref{pgn} sets out.

    This makes Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} well behaved. With the $p(n \mid g)$ fixed,
    its logarithm is a positive combination of logarithms of functions linear in $p(g \mid s)$, hence
    concave over the simplex. There are therefore no local optima to be trapped in, and an iteration
    reaches the maximum wherever it starts. Whether that maximum is a single point is a separate
    question, and it turns on the taxa being distinguishable from one another; Section \ref{pgn}
    takes it up, since it is a property of the $p(n \mid g)$ rather than of the objective.

    \subsection{Estimating \texorpdfstring{$p(n \mid g)$}{p(n|g)}}\label{pgn}
    The topic--word distribution is a property of the database, so it is measured on the database and
    not fitted to a sample. One number in it is chosen rather than counted --- the $\lambda$ of
    Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth}, which regularises the measurement before the fit reads it --- and
    that changes nothing about when the work is done: the rows are still fixed before any sample is
    seen and reused for every one of them. A new Genestrip goal walks the genomes the update phase
    considers ---
    which is every genome of the release in scope, and not only those the entry phase stored --- and
    for each of them looks up every $k$-mer in the database $d$.

    The goal produces a counter for each genome $d$ stores, and one at each taxon for the genomes
    beneath it that nothing names --- an unentered genome raises the counter of the lowest taxon it
    shares with something stored. A row is then written at every taxon from those counters, because
    it costs nothing and because the admission rule below may put a topic at any of them: at a data
    taxon the row is one genome's, and higher up it is the clade's, the unnamed material among it.
    One quantity has to be named at genome granularity, which $c^{\circ}$ does not provide. Write
    $\kappa(h,n)$ for the number of $k$-mers of a single genome $h$ that $d$ holds at node $n$. The
    own counts are its sums, $c^{\circ}(s,n) = \sum_{h \in H(s)} \kappa(h,n)$ over the genomes $H(s)$
    attaching at taxon $s$ --- one genome at a data taxon, and at an internal taxon the unnamed ones
    the update left there. Write $\mathrm{path}(t)$ for the nodes from $t$ up to the root, $t$ among
    them, which is where the update can have left a $k$-mer of anything sitting at or below $t$.
    What a genome contributes to the row of $t$ is then
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyrho}
        \rho_h(t) \;=\; \sum_{m \,\in\, \mathrm{path}(t)} \kappa(h,m) ,
        \qquad
        R_t \;=\; \sum_{h \,\preceq\, t} \rho_h(t) ,
    \end{equation}
    %
    with $h \preceq t$ meaning that $h$'s taxon lies at or below $t$: every genome of the release in
    scope there, entered or not. The second equality groups those $k$-mers by genome where
    Equation \eqref{smoothyrows} below groups the same ones by taxon, so the row total is what the
    genomes beneath $t$ contribute between them either way. The argument $t$ is carried because
    $\rho$ depends on the range the row is read on, and it is dropped below wherever one topic is in
    view.

    Beside every row the goal stores what Section \ref{abundances} needs to divide by: that sum,
    which is the row total, and the sum $\sum_h \rho_h^{2}$ of the squares, from which the effective
    number of genomes follows. Both accumulate while the row is being pooled, at a cost of two
    numbers per row. The squares have to be taken \emph{per genome} and not per taxon, which is the
    one place the two granularities part: $c^{\circ}$ gathers the unentered genomes of a taxon into
    one counter, and squaring that would call a hundred unnamed organisms a single large one. The
    goal sees them singly on its walk, so it adds each $\rho_h$ into the taxon's counter and each
    $\rho_h^{2}$ into the sum of squares as it goes. The plain count is worth keeping alongside them,
    not because the abundance uses it but because the two together say how uneven a clade's pool is
    --- a count of forty against an effective three is a fact about the reference collection worth
    reporting. Storing all of it everywhere, including at the data taxa where the effective count is
    one, is what keeps the abundance a single expression.

    \paragraph{The topics.} A \emph{data taxon} is a taxon with a complete genome directly
    associated, whose $k$-mers the database stores \citep{genestrip-ft}; in RefSeq such genomes
    usually attach at the rank of species or below. Every taxon carries a row, the ones above the
    data taxa included, but an inference does not use them all. $G$ in
    Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} is a set of nodes drawn from them, which the admission rule of
    Equation \eqref{smoothyadmit} settles. Two node sets are in play and they are dropped to at
    different times: $N$ is what the modelling-phase drop leaves and belongs to the database, while
    $N_s$ is what the sample leaves of $N$ and belongs to the analysis. At $T = 1$ the admitted
    topics are exactly $N_s$, so every node still standing is a topic. A coarser answer is not a coarser
    fit but a sum over the fitted one, as Section \ref{abundances} sets out.

    The material the database cannot name needs no topic of its own. A genome that was never entered
    still took part in the update, so its $k$-mers are held wherever its stored relatives put them:
    at the lowest taxon it shares with them, which for an organism sitting directly under $n$ with
    nothing named below is $n$ itself. Those are $n$'s own counts, they enter $n$'s row through
    Equation \eqref{smoothyrows}, and a topic at $n$ carries the unnamed material of $n$ with
    everything else that sits there. One row per node, and no second kind of topic.

    Two things stand in the way of that topic set, and one device settles both. A data taxon may have
    descendants, a species with strains beneath it being both. A data taxon may carry several
    genomes, which would enter one topic however unlike each other they are. The first makes a topic
    stand for two things at once: the $k$-mers of the taxon's own stored genomes sit at that taxon,
    and so does whatever the sample gives there that nothing beneath explains, and one weight cannot
    report both. The second is a matter of pooling, one row for genomes that may be very unlike.

    The companion paper answers the first with a tree transformation, which inserts a node beneath a
    data taxon and moves that taxon's sequence data to it, leaving the taxon internal and the new
    node a leaf \citep{genestrip-ft}. We restate it because the method depends on it, and apply it
    per genome: every genome the entry phase stored is given a leaf of its own beneath its taxon and
    carries its $k$-mers there, as Figure \ref{fttrans} draws. That settles the second problem with
    the first. A topic is then one genome, so nothing is pooled and no average stands in for the
    organism present; nothing is weighted by sequencing effort, a clade with nine thousand assemblies
    contributing nine thousand topics rather than one topic nine thousand times over; and
    $\lvert K(g) \rvert$ in Section \ref{abundances} is a genome's length rather than a clade's mean.
    The transformation adds nodes but moves no genome between clades, so the phylogenetic structure
    of the taxonomy is left as it was.

    It costs topics: as many as the reference collection has assemblies, and assemblies of
    one species share nearly all their $k$-mers. The admission rule that follows makes that
    workable --- a genome with no $k$-mers of its own is never a topic --- and the aggregation of
    Section \ref{abundances} makes the remainder harmless, since which of a set of
    near-identical assemblies is credited does not change their sum.

    After both, every data taxon is a leaf of the transformed tree. Each stored genome is a topic of
    its own, and every internal node is free of stored genomes, so a topic there reports only what
    the topics beneath it cannot account for.

    \paragraph{Admitting a topic.} A topic with no evidence behind it does not sit at zero: the
    iteration of Section \ref{inference} drives it towards zero and arrives only in the limit. Left
    alone, every genome of the database appears in the report with a small positive abundance, and a
    clade with many genomes beneath it collects more of that residue than a clade with few --- so the
    aggregate would lean towards whichever clades the reference collection covers best.

    The remedy is to fit only what the sample supports. A data taxon $g$ is admitted when
    $c_g \geq T$ --- the count the \emph{sample} reports at $g$'s own node, not what the database
    stores there --- and dropped otherwise. The test is on the same $c_n$ the likelihood consumes,
    in $k$-mers and not in reads. Counting reads would mean asking which taxon each
    read was assigned to, which is the classifier's decision and subject to its algorithm --- the
    reason Section \ref{model} takes $c_n$ from the $k$-mers in the first place, and not one to give
    up at the door. A $k$-mer threshold is the finer of the two as well: it can be set as low as one
    and admit a genome on partial evidence, where a read threshold cannot see below a read.

    In practice the test is decisive rather than delicate, and at $T = 1$ it states itself: for a
    sample $s$, drop every leaf that got no counts and leave the rest. A database names far more
    genomes than any one sample contains, and for most of them the sample reports nothing at their
    own node at all, so that one pass already cuts $G$ to the size of what was actually seen. Raising
    $T$ is the remedy if a sample proves noisier than that, and it is the same rule read at a higher
    bar. Either way what is dropped is what could not have been estimated, for the reason the next
    paragraphs give.

    \paragraph{Where the counts stop short of the data taxa.} The rule as stated reads the count at a
    data taxon's own node, and there are samples in which no data taxon passes it. A genome's own
    node holds only the $k$-mers that distinguish it from every relative in the database; where a
    clade is densely covered there are few of those, and a shallow sample may see none of them. The
    counts then stop at some node above the leaves, and a test read at the leaves alone admits
    nothing and leaves $G$ empty.

    The remedy keeps the threshold and reads it at every node rather than at the data taxa alone.
    Admit each node whose count reaches $T$,
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyadmit}
        A \;=\; \bigl\{\, n \in N_s \;:\; c_n \geq T \,\bigr\} ,
    \end{equation}
    %
    and let the members of $A$ be the topics. At a data taxon this is the rule above and nothing
    changes. At an inner node the topic stands for the clade beneath it rather than for a genome, and
    it is admitted on the same evidence as any other.

    An inner node and a data taxon beneath it may both be topics, and that is no longer the objection
    it would have been. A row is read on its own node's lineage, so the row of an inner node $t$ has
    no mass below $t$ while the row of a leaf beneath it does: the count at the leaf is carried by the
    leaf's row alone, and the two are told apart there. What $t$ takes is what the leaves under it
    cannot explain, which is what an inner topic is for.

    What this changes is the counting, on both sides of
    Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} and in opposite directions.

    On the database side the topic's row aggregates. Equation \eqref{smoothyrows} builds the row at
    $t$ by pooling the own-counts $c^{\circ}(s,n)$ of every taxon $s \preceq t$, so an admitted node
    stands for all the genomes beneath it at once and the topic is the clade's average member rather
    than any one of them. At a data taxon that sum has a single term --- the transformation left it a
    leaf with nothing beneath it --- which is why the ordinary case needed no separate statement. It
    is the same equation doing the same thing at a different node, so no new one is wanted, and the
    aggregation is what lets a node stand in for genomes the sample could not tell apart.

    On the sample side the counts are cut back. Those strictly beneath an admitted node do not enter
    it: they are by construction below $T$, which is the evidence the threshold judged too thin to
    fit on. They are dropped rather than folded upwards into the admitted node, which would let
    material the classifier has already resolved weigh on a fit that could not resolve it.

    Dropping a topic is not dropping its genomes. A leaf $h$ beneath $n$ that fails the test stops
    being a topic of its own and is thereafter represented by $n$, but only as far as $n$ can see it.
    What passes to $n$ is what $h$ contributes \emph{at $n$ and above} --- the $k$-mers of $h$ the
    database holds there, which are the ones shared with its relatives. What $h$ holds privately at
    its own node does not pass: that node is not on $\mathrm{path}(n)$, so Equation \eqref{smoothyrows}
    leaves it out of $c(n,\cdot)$ exactly as the sample counts there are left out of the likelihood.
    It is a drop and not a fold, and the two sides drop the same thing.

    The divisor is the exception, and deliberately so: $h$'s genomes join the pool at $n$, because
    they are organisms the estimate at $n$ now stands for. They join it weighted by what they
    contribute there, which is the whole point of the effective count of
    Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance}: a dropped leaf that holds a great deal at $n$ and above moves
    the divisor, and one holding almost nothing barely moves it, as a member of a pool should. That
    is what makes an estimate at $n$ a share of one organism of the clade rather than of the clade
    entire.

    What $\rho_h$ measures, read against the drops, is the part of $h$ that $n$ has to account for,
    and the range of the row is what makes it so. A genome struck out by the modelling drop retains
    nothing at its own node, so every $k$-mer of it sits at an ancestor and its whole size falls
    inside $\mathrm{path}(n)$: it enters the pool at $n$ as a full member. That is right, because it
    is a real organism that has just lost the topic which would have spoken for it. A genome
    distinguished from all its relatives is the mirror of it --- everything it holds is at its own
    node, which is not on $\mathrm{path}(n)$, so $\rho_h = 0$ and it leaves the divisor of $n$
    without a rule being written for it. That is right too, since it keeps a topic of its own. The
    genomes the drop strips of a topic are the ones that weigh most at $n$, and the ones that keep a
    topic weigh least.

    A leaf that keeps its topic and still shares $k$-mers higher up falls between the two, and there
    the pooling is an approximation rather than an identity: its shared part counts towards $n$'s
    divisor although its abundance is being estimated separately. Removing it would mean knowing
    which topics the admission rule kept, which is a property of the sample, and the divisor would
    stop being something the database can be asked for once. The error runs one way and is bounded by
    how much of a resolved genome is shared, which is the same quantity Section \ref{pgn} reports
    when it lists what each genome retains at its own node.

    Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} is untouched. Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance} is not:
    $R_t$ is the total of the pooled row and so sums the counts of every genome beneath $t$, which is
    the database's hold on the clade and not on one member of it. The divisor is therefore
    $R_t / \lvert G_t \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}}$, for the reason Section \ref{abundances} gives.

    One thing follows for the report. An estimate at such a topic is a share of the clade and not of
    a genome, and it has to be named as the clade: a figure reported against a species the sample
    never distinguished would claim more than the counts support.

    The saving is not incidental, and it is had without discarding anything. A tree carries a great
    many nodes, and a topic at each of them would enter every fit whatever the sample held; the rule
    admits only those the sample gives counts at, which are the lineages it actually touches, so
    $\lvert G \rvert$ is the size of what was seen rather than the size of the reference collection.
    Nothing is given up for that. A leaf dropped for want of counts does not take its genomes out of
    the fit --- they are pooled into the node above, which is a topic wherever the sample gives
    evidence there, and $\lvert G \rvert$ counts them. What the rule removes is a name the data
    cannot support, not the evidence itself.

    Passing the test is a necessary condition and not a claim of presence: $c_t > 0$ may be the
    clade's own genomes pushing $k$-mers up, just as $c_g > 0$ may be a near-identical relative of
    $g$. The fit is there to decide between them. A topic admitted but truly absent is a smaller matter
    and not nothing --- the iteration takes it towards zero and arrives only in the limit, so
    Appendix \ref{sparsity} sets out what to do if exact zeros are wanted. And the counts of a
    rejected topic do not vanish; they are explained by whatever remains, usually the admitted node
    above it, so the filter moves weak named evidence into that node's share and $u$ should be read
    knowing it.

    \paragraph{What the database must be.} The method requires that $d$ was built with its LCA update
    ranging over the whole RefSeq category and not only over the entered genomes --- in Genestrip's
    terms, \texttt{refseq.updateScope=all} rather than a scope narrowed to the database's own taxa
    \citep{genestrip-pub}. That is Genestrip's default, so the requirement costs nothing in practice,
    but it is a requirement and not a convenience. The narrowings each cost it in their own way:
    \texttt{ownTaxaOnly} leaves a $k$-mer claimed for a requested taxon although a genome of another
    carries it, and \texttt{allButExcluded} does the same in the small, for the taxa struck out of
    \texttt{taxids.txt} --- which is the case Section \ref{pgn} returns to below, since an excluded
    branch is precisely a set of genomes the database does not hold and cannot account for. It is
    what puts a genome's $k$-mers at or above its own taxon, and every locality claim below rests on
    that. Under a narrowed scope a genome absent from $d$ leaves them wherever its stored relatives
    happen to carry them, so an unnamed genome's counts spread across the tree instead of gathering
    on the lineage of the clade it belongs to. A database built the other way can still be read by
    the model, but $u$ and the abundances at the inner nodes should not be believed from it.

    Three cases arise. A genome the entry phase stored has a leaf of its own, and each of
    its $k$-mers raises $c^{\circ}(g,n)$ at the node $n$ where $d$ holds it. The
    entry phase stores every $k$-mer of such a genome, so all of them are somewhere in $d$ and none is
    lost --- a fact Section \ref{abundances} rests on.

    A genome whose taxon has no node in $d$'s tree --- a species the database has never heard of,
    which is what material genuinely outside it looks like --- has no leaf and no taxon it could
    honestly be attributed to. It raises $c^{\circ}(t,n)$ at the nearest ancestor $t$ the tree does
    have, and only where $d$ holds the $k$-mer at all, which for such a genome is only where it
    overlaps something stored. Its counts are part of $t$'s own counts, and a topic at $t$ is what
    accounts for them.

    A genome between the two raises nothing: one whose taxon $d$ names but which the entry phase did
    not store, of which there are many, Genestrip entering at most a fixed number of genomes per
    taxon. It is not unnamed and must not be read as such --- doing so would put the bulk of RefSeq
    into the unnamed share and leave $u$ reporting the entry cap rather than the state of the
    reference collection. It is not a topic either, having no row of its own. It is represented
    by the conspecifics that were stored, and it has already done its work: it took part in the LCA
    update, so the node positions every row is read from account for it.

    That the unnamed material of different clades stays apart follows from this and needs no
    machinery of its own. A missing \emph{Streptococcus} species raises counts on the
    \emph{Streptococcus} lineage and a missing \emph{Nocardia} species on the \emph{Nocardia}
    lineage. What matters is
    that there is not a single global one: a missing \emph{Streptococcus} species and a missing \emph{Nocardia} species
    raise different counters, so the model can say which clade the unnamed material sits in rather
    than only that it exists. Counting is by frequency rather than by distinct $k$-mer, since a $k$-mer shared
    between genomes is the evidence the mixture is about and discarding its multiplicity would
    discard the ambiguity the model exists to resolve.

    Two quantities have to be kept apart. A counter $c^{\circ}(s,\cdot)$ is what a taxon holds of its
    own --- at a data taxon the $k$-mers of the one genome the transformation left there, at an
    internal taxon those of the unnamed genomes attaching to it --- while a row is what a whole clade
    beneath a taxon does. At a data taxon the two coincide, the transformation having left it a leaf
    with nothing beneath it, and that row is read as it stands. Writing $s \preceq t$ for a taxon at or below $t$, the row of $t$ is
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyrows}
        c(t,n) \;=\; \sum_{s \,\preceq\, t} c^{\circ}(s,n) ,
        \qquad n \in \mathrm{path}(t) .
    \end{equation}
    %
    One rule, and it needs no second form for the unnamed material, which sits in the own counts of
    the node it belongs under and is pooled with everything else there. Summing the own counts
    rather than the rows is what keeps this from being circular, $t$ being its own ancestor; in an
    implementation the same thing is had from the children, $c(t,n) = c^{\circ}(t,n) + \sum_{h} c(h,n)$
    over the children $h$ of $t$, which a single post-order traversal computes for every taxon at
    once. At a data taxon with nothing below it the sum has one term and $c$ and $c^{\circ}$
    coincide.

    The range keeps the rows from overlapping. A row of $t$ is read on $t$'s own lineage:
    what the database stores \emph{beneath} $t$ belongs to the topics sitting there and not to $t$,
    and without the restriction the row of $t$ would carry the mass of every topic below it while
    those are topics in their own right. At a data taxon, being a leaf, the restriction takes nothing
    away.

    That the own counts respect it already --- that a genome sitting at $t$ never has its $k$-mers
    stored below $t$ --- is a fact about the database rather than an assumption.
    Genestrip's LCA update is not confined to the genomes the entry phase stored:
    all taxa of a RefSeq category under which the database holds anything take part in it, so a taxon
    absent from $d$ still pushes a $k$-mer up \citep{genestrip-pub}. A $k$-mer of a genome at $t$ is
    therefore held at the lowest common ancestor of a carrier set that contains $t$, entered or not,
    and that node is an ancestor of $t$ or $t$ itself. The own count of a taxon never leaves that
    taxon's lineage. So the restriction discards a genome's $k$-mers only where the genome sits below $t$, and never
    where it sits at $t$.

    It holds exactly as far as the update scope reaches and no further. An organism outside it ---
    a taxon struck out of the build, or one absent from the reference collection altogether --- took
    no part in the update, so the $k$-mers a sample yields from it sit wherever its stored relatives
    alone put them, which may be beneath the node it belongs under. No row expects them there, so
    they are read as the named relative's and $u$ is silent about them. This is a limit of the construction rather than a defect in it, and it cannot be repaired from the database side: it
    would take knowing the genome that was missing.

    A row above the data taxa is not an organism's. The method reads such rows wherever
    Equation \eqref{smoothyadmit} puts a topic at an internal taxon, and there the row is the pooled
    contribution of every genome beneath the
    taxon --- that clade's pan-genome as the
    reference collection happens to represent it --- and, counting being by frequency, a pan-genome
    weighted towards whatever its members were sequenced most often. A $k$-mer carried by many
    genomes of the clade weighs more than one carried by a single draft.

    The weighting is defensible, since a $k$-mer common to a clade is likelier to be observed from
    it, but it makes the topic a property of the reference collection as much as of the organisms.
    If a clade is dominated by one heavily sequenced member --- \emph{S.~pneumoniae} accounts for
    more than nine thousand of the twenty-two thousand \emph{Streptococcus} assemblies in RefSeq ---
    a genus row is largely that member's genome wearing the genus's name. The data taxa are free of
    it, each being one genome and pooling nothing; it is the topics above them that carry the
    weighting.

    The estimate is then
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothypng}
        p(n \mid g) \;=\; \frac{c(g,n)}{R_g} ,
        \qquad R_g \;=\; \sum_{m \,\in\, \mathrm{path}(g)} c(g,m) ,
    \end{equation}
    %
    the row total over the nodes the row occupies, so $p(\cdot \mid g)$ sums to one over
    $\mathrm{path}(g)$ by construction, and over any node set containing it.
    It is the distribution of where a $k$-mer of $g$ is stored, \emph{given that the database holds
    it at all} --- a condition that binds only above the data taxa. A data taxon is a genome the
    entry phase stored, so $d$ holds every $k$-mer of it and its row accounts for the whole genome. A
    row higher up pools genomes the entry phase never stored along with those it did, and the former
    are held only where they overlap something that was, so for them the row describes the shared
    part and not the material itself.

    The condition is not a concession where it binds. The counts $c_n$ are subject to the same one --- a $k$-mer absent from $d$ is never
    reported at a node either --- so both sides of
    Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} speak about the
    same observable population, and the likelihood is a proper multinomial over it. What the database
    does not hold is not modelled here at all; Section \ref{abundances} is where it re-enters, since
    it is a question about abundance rather than about attribution.

    $R_g$ counts occurrences and not distinct $k$-mers, since $c(g,n)$ does. It is worth keeping apart
    from the $u_{t,d}$ of Section \ref{error}, which counts the \emph{distinct} $k$-mers stored for a
    taxon and belongs to a different measure; the two are easily conflated and are not
    interchangeable, with the gap between them widening exactly for the repeat-rich genomes where it
    matters most.

    A topic above the data taxa plays the part of an organism the database does not contain. Without
    one, every count would have to be explained by some named topic, and a sample holding an organism
    absent from $d$ would have its abundance pushed onto whichever genomes happen to resemble it.
    Equation \eqref{smoothypng} applies to such a node unchanged --- it is a topic and is read like
    one, over its own row and its own total --- which is the whole of what has to be said about it.
    That row averages locally: a genome-weighted average over the taxa \emph{of that clade}, the
    omitted ones among them, and not over everything the database omits.

    The row is skewed, though, in a way a data taxon's is not. A genome the entry phase stored has
    all of its $k$-mers in $d$, so its row accounts for the whole of it. The omitted genomes a higher
    row pools stand in $d$ only where they overlap something that was stored: whatever is particular
    to them raises no counter anywhere. So $c(t,\cdot)$ records the shared part of the unnamed
    material rather than the material itself, weighted towards whatever is conserved. Read as a description of the organisms
    the database cannot name, or of the reference collection at large, it is simply wrong.

    It is still the right row for the model, because the sample is censored in the same way. A
    read from an organism $d$ does not contain yields $k$-mers, and only those $d$ holds are reported
    at a node; the particular ones are as absent from $c_n$ as they are from the row. Both sides of
    Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} therefore speak about the shared part and about nothing else,
    which is the conditioning that makes $p(\cdot \mid g)$ a distribution at all, applied to a topic
    with no genome behind it.

    Accounting for the observable is all such a topic is asked to do. It is not
    there to portray an absent organism but to take up the counts the named topics cannot carry: a
    slack term, fitted like any other topic, whose value says how much of what was observed nothing
    in $d$ can name. Skewed with respect to the organism, faithful with respect to the observable ---
    and the likelihood is written over the observable.

    One degeneracy remains after all this, and it is not hypothetical. Two topics are
    distinguishable only where their columns differ, and a genome that retains none of its own
    $k$-mers has a column that is zero at its own node and carries weight only on the ancestors it
    shares with its siblings. Where two such genomes sit under the same parent, their columns are
    equal --- and the smoothing of Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} does not separate them, adding the
    same background to both and any split of abundance
    between them fits the data equally well. Near-identical assemblies of one species are the common
    case of it, and the reason the transformation of Section \ref{pgn} needs the admission rule
    beside it. This is the mitis complex of the companion paper seen
    from the estimation side: in a \emph{Streptococcus} database built on the reference taxonomy
    \emph{S.~mitis} retains nothing at all and cannot be told from the pneumococcus by any amount of
    sequencing.

    One drop needs no sample at all and can be made once, when the database is read. A leaf $n$ with
    $c(n,n) = 0$ holds no $k$-mer of its own, and a count is only ever reported at a node because the
    database holds the $k$-mer there, so nothing a sample yields can be reported at $n$ --- not for
    this sample but for any. A count appearing there would mean the analysis had placed a $k$-mer at
    a node $d$ does not hold it at, which is not a case to provide for but an inconsistent database.
    Such a leaf can therefore be struck from $G$ in the modelling phase rather than failing the
    admission rule again for every sample.

    It also settles where a row can be zero. A leaf that survives the drop holds $k$-mers of its own,
    so its row is positive at its own node; a row's zeros therefore lie at \emph{inner} nodes of its
    path, where the database records nothing of that genome exclusively. Those are the zeros
    Section \ref{pgn} smooths, and there are no others to smooth: a leaf is either dropped, and then
    reports no counts either, or kept, and then carries a row positive where its own counts are.

    A different question is whether a node can be left that \emph{no} topic reaches, which would take
    Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} to zero outright. At $T = 1$ none can: a node with any count
    is admitted and lies on its own path. Above $T = 1$ one can, leaf or inner alike --- it takes a
    node whose counts fall below the threshold with every node beneath it below too, which is a
    lineage the sample touched in traces and nowhere strongly enough to admit anything. Its counts
    then leave the product with the topics they would have supported. That is what raising $T$ means:
    evidence below the threshold is set aside, and setting the topics aside while keeping their
    counts would ask the remaining topics to explain what was already judged too thin to fit on. It
    is the same drop applied on both sides, as with a dropped leaf, and it is arithmetic rather than
    modelling --- those counts carry nothing the fit was going to use.

    The admission rule above meets it head on, which is the second reason for that rule. A taxon
    retaining none of its own $k$-mers has an empty node, so it never passes the threshold and never
    enters $G$: the filter and the degeneracy have the same test, and what it removes was not
    estimable to begin with. What is lost with it is a name and not a quantity --- the counts stay,
    explained by the taxon's neighbours and by the topic above it, and the aggregate over
    the clade is unchanged. The condition is a property of the database, so the affected taxa can be
    listed from the stored fractions before a sample is seen, which is the right place to report them
    rather than leaving a reader to wonder why a species is missing.

    A named topic and the topic above it are separated by the same fact. The named one is a leaf and
    holds the $k$-mers private to it; the unnamed genomes pooled higher up hold none, their own
    $k$-mers being absent from $d$ altogether. So the two rows differ where it matters and $u$ is
    determined by what the named topics cannot account for, which is what it is meant to be.

    \todo{Check the degeneracy argument above before relying on it: that two topics

    What follows is not a repair of the arithmetic. After the two drops every node still contributing
    a factor holds counts, so at $T = 1$ it is admitted and is its own topic, and its own row is
    positive there --- a count is reported at a node only because the database holds a $k$-mer there,
    so $c(n,n) > 0$ wherever $c_n > 0$. Every such node is therefore covered by the topic sitting on
    it, and Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} is positive with no smoothing whatever. What follows
    is a modelling choice, and the case for it has to be made on those terms.

    A row is zero at more nodes than its own path, and it can be zero on that path too. The database
    holds nothing of $g$ at a node where $g$ shares no $k$-mer exclusively with the clade below it,
    and $p(n \mid g) = 0$ there. A count at such a node then tells against $g$ with the whole weight
    the likelihood can bring: it is explained by whatever topics do reach $n$ and not at all by $g$,
    so $\theta_g$ is pushed down however much of $g$ the sample really holds. One node where the
    database happens to record nothing can put a genome out of the report.

    Smoothing the rows settles it. After the topics are fixed, interpolate each row with a uniform
    background over the nodes the row lives on,
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothysmooth}
        \tilde{p}(n \mid g) \;=\; (1 - \lambda)\, p(n \mid g) \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{\lvert
        \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert} ,
        \qquad n \in \mathrm{path}(g),
        \qquad 0 < \lambda \ll 1 ,
    \end{equation}
    %
    and zero off the path as before. It still sums to one, and it is positive at every node of
    $\mathrm{path}(g)$, so no node of a topic's own lineage can drive that topic out. This is
    Jelinek--Mercer smoothing \citep{jelinekmercer}, linear interpolation of an estimated
    distribution with a background one, the background being uniform over the row's support. Taking
    that background over the node set instead is the obvious alternative and the wrong one;
    Appendix \ref{nodesmooth} sets out why, since the reason is not the one it appears to be.

    The denominator is $\lvert \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert$ and the numerator a constant, which is what
    keeps the tree's shape out of it. $\lambda$ is a share of the \emph{whole} row, handed to the
    background and spread evenly over the nodes that row occupies, so every topic gives up the same
    $\lambda$ whatever its depth. An amount added at each node instead would hand a row as much mass
    as it has nodes to receive it, and since a row is supported on the path from its node to the
    root, a topic deep in the taxonomy would be smoothed harder than a shallow one for no reason but
    its depth. A long lineage is a fact about where a genome sits, not evidence about a sample, and
    the smoothing must not turn one into the other.

    The same objection rules out additive smoothing, which the form otherwise resembles: adding
    $\alpha$ to every count is the same interpolation with
    $\lambda = \alpha \lvert \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert / (R_g + \alpha \lvert \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert)$,
    so the background share falls as the row's total rises and a thinly held topic is smoothed
    hardest. Here $\lambda$ is the same for every row by choice, since what it repairs --- a node of
    a lineage the database records nothing at --- has nothing to do with how much of any one genome
    the database holds, nor with how deep in the tree it sits. \citet{zhailafferty} compare the two
    on retrieval models, where the distinction is the same one.

    What that dependence would cost is worth being concrete about, because it falls on the topics
    least able to bear it. Under $\tilde{p}(n \mid g) = (c(g,n) + \alpha) / (R_g + \alpha \lvert
    \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert)$ a node the row does not reach receives $\alpha / (R_g + \alpha \lvert
    \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert)$, which grows as $R_g$ falls: the smaller the genome, the more
    probability it is handed at nodes holding none of its $k$-mers. A small genome would become a
    better explanation for counts along its lineage than a large one, for no reason but its size. And
    the error would not stay where it was made --- Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance} divides
    $\theta_g$ by $R_g$, so a small genome that absorbed counts it should not have is amplified by
    the same smallness a second time. A constant $\lambda$ has no such gradient: every row gives up
    the same share and receives the same floor, whatever its total.

    Nor does the share depend on how many topics there are. It is fixed once and applies to each row
    on its own, so admitting one more topic changes no other row's floor. That is worth setting
    against the rule of thumb the topic-modeling literature offers for the prior of
    Appendix \ref{prior}, $\alpha = 50 / \lvert G \rvert$, which scales with the size of the topic
    set by design: a prior divides a fixed amount of belief among however many topics are on offer,
    where $\lambda$ makes a statement about one row at a time. A quantity that moved with $\lvert G
    \rvert$ would make a topic's floor depend on which \emph{other} topics the sample happened to
    admit, and nothing about the database's silence at a node has anything to do with that.

    That settles what $\lambda$ is for, and it is more than a guard against a zero. It is the floor
    on how hard a count at one node may press against a topic whose lineage runs through it: with
    $\lambda = 0$ a large enough $c_n$ drives a topic recording nothing at $n$ to zero, and with
    $\lambda > 0$ it cannot fall below what the background allows. That is the model's main evidence
    about which lineage a sample holds, so how far one node's silence may go towards excluding a
    lineage is a modelling decision --- a question about how completely the database is trusted to
    record a genome --- and not a numerical tolerance.

    \todo{Fix $\lambda$ against data, as with $\beta$ in Section \ref{error}. The two ends are clear
    and the middle is not: too small and one node the database is silent at can exclude a lineage the
    sample really holds, too large and the rows stop discriminating between lineages at all. A known
    composition with a genome deliberately withheld from the build is what settles it, since that is
    the case the floor exists for.}

    It is worth saying plainly how this stands to Bayesian practice, since a floor that keeps a topic
    off zero looks like a prior and is not one. The prior version of smoothing a row is additive: put
    a symmetric Dirichlet $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha \mathbf{1})$ on $p(\cdot \mid g)$, observe the counts
    $c(g,\cdot)$, and the posterior mean is Lidstone exactly, rejected above. A constant $\lambda$ is
    the Dirichlet whose concentration $\alpha = \lambda R_g / \bigl( (1 - \lambda) \lvert
    \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert \bigr)$ grows with the row it is applied to, and a prior that reads the
    data before deciding its own strength is not a prior.

    The difference has a consequence rather than being a matter of naming. A prior is swamped as the
    counts grow; $\lambda$ is not, because it changes the emission and therefore the likelihood
    itself. The floor it puts under a topic at a silent node of its own lineage survives any amount
    of sequencing, which is what makes it a statement about the database --- that the database's
    silence at a node is not evidence of absence --- rather than a belief to be overruled by
    evidence. That also spares it the objection Appendix \ref{prior} raises against the Dirichlet on
    $\theta$: there the inflated $k$-mer counts leave a prior nothing to weigh against, and here
    there is nothing being weighed. The two act on different objects in any case, $\alpha$ on the
    mixture and $\lambda$ on the rows, and neither substitutes for the other.

    Nothing is paid for it in the fit. The smoothing enters as a rank-one term on each row's own
    support, so the rows need never be stored smoothed: the iteration of
    Equation \eqref{smoothyem} walks the non-zeros of the rows as Equation \eqref{smoothyrows} leaves
    them and adds one constant per node of the path. And $R_g$ is untouched: the smoothing is applied
    to the emission the fit reads and not to the counts the database yields, so
    Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance} divides by what it always did.

    \paragraph{Cost and storage.} The goal runs once for a database and writes its result to a
    serialized file, which every later analysis against that database reads. Nothing here is paid per
    sample.

    The result is small, and for a reason that belongs to the method rather than to the encoding.
    Equation \eqref{smoothyrows} leaves a row with exactly $\lvert \mathrm{path}(t) \rvert$ entries,
    one double per ancestor and one for the taxon, so what is stored per taxon is an array of the
    tree's depth and never of its size: a dozen or so where a bacterial data taxon sits, and no more
    at any rank.

    A data taxon is a leaf of the transformed tree, so its row is its own count and can be written as
    it stands; only the rows above them pool.

    The row total $R_g$ goes beside each array and cannot be recovered from it: it is divided out by
    Equation \eqref{smoothypng}, so a normalised row sums to one whatever $R_g$ was, and
    Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance} wants it back. It is known while the row is being built and so
    written with it rather than recomputed, at a cost of one long against an array of $n$ doubles.
    A data taxon needs nothing further; a row above them carries the sum of squares of its members'
    contributions as well, since Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance} divides by the effective number of
    genomes rather than by a count of them.

    That turns a matrix over taxa and nodes into one over taxa and tree depth, so a database entering
    a few thousand genomes stores a few hundred kilobytes rather than the tens of megabytes a dense
    matrix over all its nodes would need.

    That count is the number of genomes beneath $t$ whose taxon the tree does not have. Neither kind
    of row is written where nothing was counted.

    \subsection{Correcting the counts for sequencing error}\label{error}
    A $k$-mer is observed only if all $k$ of its bases were read correctly. With a per-base error rate
    $e_b$ and errors taken as independent, an instance survives with probability $(1 - e_b)^k$, so the
    reported counts are a systematic undercount and the corrected count is
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyerror}
        \hat{c}_n \;=\; \frac{c_n}{(1 - e_b)^k}.
    \end{equation}
    %
    The factor is not a small adjustment. At $k = 31$ it is $0.73$ for $e_b = 1\,\%$, $0.53$ at
    $2\,\%$ and $0.20$ at the $5\,\%$ of a nanopore run: four $k$-mers in five are lost before
    anything is counted.

    Two things follow, and the first is the one to say plainly. Applied as written,
    Equation \eqref{smoothyerror} does not change the estimate at all. It scales every $c_n$ by one
    factor common to every $n$, so the objective $\sum_n c_n \log (\varphi\theta)_n$ scales with it and
    the maximiser over the simplex is unchanged; the cancellation runs through
    Equation \eqref{smoothyem} in the same way, since $\hat{c}_n$ and $C = \sum_n \hat{c}_n$ carry the
    factor alike. Under maximum likelihood the correction is inert, and $e_b$ need not be estimated at
    all to obtain $\hat{\theta}$, the abundances or $u$.

    It earns its place wherever the magnitude of the counts is read and not only their ratios, and
    two such places are in this paper. In the MAP iteration of Equation \eqref{smoothymapem} the
    likelihood term scales and the $\alpha - 1$ beside it does not, so uncorrected counts silently
    multiply the weight of the prior by $(1 - e_b)^k$ --- a factor of five at nanopore rates, which is
    not a subtlety but the difference between a prior that regularises and one that dominates. And in
    the prior estimation of Appendix \ref{prior} each sample enters weighted by its own total, so a
    nanopore sample and an Illumina one are not on one scale until both are corrected. The same holds
    of any absolute count reported beside the distribution.

    The second is that $e_b$ need not be a constant. The quality strings carry a per-base estimate,
    so the surviving fraction can be averaged over the reads actually seen instead of assumed. That
    matters most where it is largest: nanopore error varies widely between reads, and a mean rate
    understates the loss because $(1 - e_b)^k$ is convex in $e_b$.

    \paragraph{Counts that the assumption does not fit.} The correction above rescales every node
    alike and therefore says nothing about whether the assumption behind
    Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} holds at any particular one. There is a measure that does, and
    it comes from the classifier itself. The $k$-mer consistency ratio of \citet{genestrip-pub}
    compares the distinct $k$-mers actually seen for a taxon against the number expected if its
    $k$-mers were drawn uniformly: with $u_{t,d}$ the $k$-mers the database stores for $t$,
    $k_{t,f,d}$ the count observed and $u_{t,f,d}$ the distinct ones among them,
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyconsistency}
        E \;=\; u_{t,d} \Bigl( 1 - (1 - 1/u_{t,d})^{k_{t,f,d}} \Bigr),
        \qquad r_{t,f,d} \;=\; u_{t,f,d} \,/\, E .
    \end{equation}

    Its assumption is worth reading twice, because it is ours. It holds where every $k$-mer of a taxon
    is equally likely to be seen and fails on repeats, on genomic dust and under amplification
    protocols --- which is word for word the assumption Section \ref{model} rests on and the reason
    this method does not apply to metabarcoding unless the reference is narrowed to the amplified
    region, as Section \ref{amplicon} sets out. So $r$ is not an external criterion bolted on. It is
    the method's own premise, tested against the data, per node and per sample.

    That makes it a weight rather than a filter. A threshold on $r$ would discard a node's counts
    whole and turn a continuous doubt into a binary one; the same information is better spent
    attenuating a node's contribution in proportion to how far its counts depart from what the
    assumption predicts. It enters where the error correction does,
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyweight}
        \hat{c}_n \;=\; \frac{w(r_n)\, c_n}{(1 - e_b)^k} ,
    \end{equation}
    %
    and unlike that correction it is not a common factor: $w$ varies from node to node, so this one
    does move the estimate rather than only its scale. That is the point of it.

    A form that meets the requirements below, and the one we propose, is
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyw}
        w(r) \;=\; \max\Bigl( 0, \; 1 + \beta \ln \bigl( \min(r, 1) \bigr) \Bigr),
        \qquad \beta > 0 .
    \end{equation}
    %
    The inner $\min$ discards the uninformative direction, the logarithm makes the penalty gentle near
    one and steep as $r$ falls, and the outer $\max$ puts a floor at zero: a node below
    $r = e^{-1/\beta}$ contributes nothing. Filtering is therefore not rejected but recovered, as
    the limiting case of a weight rather than as a separate rule with a threshold of its own.

    One parameter remains, and $\beta$ says how severely inconsistency is punished. It is worth
    noting that a calibration point already exists: the worked example of \citet{genestrip-pub} reports
    $r = 0.8631$ for Epstein--Barr virus in a saliva sample and reads it as \emph{supporting} the
    consistency of the counts. A node so described should keep most of its weight, which
    $\beta = 1$ does not do --- it leaves $0.85$ --- while $\beta = 1/2$ leaves $0.93$. That
    suggests $\beta$ well below one, but it is one example and the value wants measuring.

    What $w$ must satisfy, however $\beta$ is fixed:
    %
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $w(1) = 1$. A node whose counts match the expectation is not penalised.
        \item $w$ falls as $r$ falls below one, and smoothly. A step function is a filter wearing a
              weight's clothes, with a tuning parameter and a cliff.
        \item $w \approx 1$ for $r$ above one. Seeing appreciably more distinct $k$-mers than expected
              is not a thing that happens, so that direction carries no information and should not be
              read as one.
        \item $w$ depends on the count as well as the ratio. At small $k_{t,f,d}$ the ratio is noise,
              and a node holding three $k$-mers must not be discounted because its $r$ came out at
              $0.6$. Equation \eqref{smoothyw} does not yet do this, and the natural repair is to
              shrink $r$ towards one as the count falls --- replacing $r$ by
              $1 + (r - 1)\,k_{t,f,d}/(k_{t,f,d} + k_0)$ for some scale $k_0$ --- rather than to
              complicate $w$ itself.
        \item $w$ is a heuristic in $r$ and not a tail probability. The exact distribution of
              $u_{t,f,d}$ is known and approximately normal about $E$, but it is so sharply peaked
              that small deviations drive it to zero, which is why \citet{genestrip-pub} report the
              ratio rather than a test.
    \end{itemize}

    \todo{Fix $\beta$ and $k_0$ against data rather than by argument, and check
    Equation \eqref{smoothyw} against alternatives while doing so. The measurements that decide it:
    how $r$ is distributed across nodes in real samples, and whether the nodes with low $r$ are the
    ones an independent standard says are wrong.}

    \todo{The effect that would change the estimate is the one not modelled here, and the section

    still be in the database, at some other node. How often depends on how densely the database

    \subsection{Inference}\label{inference}
    Write $\theta_g = p(g \mid s)$, $\varphi_{ng} = \tilde{p}(n \mid g)$ and $C = \sum_n \hat{c}_n$,
    following the usual symbols of the topic-modeling literature for the mixture and for the
    topic--word distribution. It is the \emph{smoothed} rows of Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} that
    $\varphi$ stands for, those being what the fit reads; how they are stored is a separate matter,
    set out with that equation. The indices differ from that literature deliberately: $k$ is the $k$-mer length
    throughout this paper and cannot also be a topic index, and $w$ for a node would say nothing. The estimate
    maximises $\ell(\theta) = \sum_n \hat{c}_n \log (\varphi\theta)_n$ over the simplex. Two ways to
    compute it are worth setting out, because the second follows from the first and needs less
    apparatus.

    \paragraph{Constrained ascent.} With the constraint in affine form the Lagrangian is
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothylagrange}
        \Lambda(\theta, \nu) \;=\; \sum_{n \in N_s} \hat{c}_n \log (\varphi\theta)_n
            \;-\; \nu \Bigl( \sum_{g \in G} \theta_g - 1 \Bigr),
    \end{equation}
    %
    and stationarity gives $\sum_n \hat{c}_n \varphi_{ng} / (\varphi\theta)_n = \nu$ at every topic carrying
    mass. The multiplier is not left to be solved for: multiplying that condition by $\theta_g$,
    summing over $g$ and using $\sum_g \varphi_{ng} \theta_g = (\varphi\theta)_n$ collapses the left side to
    $\sum_n \hat{c}_n$, so
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothylambda}
        \nu \;=\; C .
    \end{equation}
    %
    Equation \eqref{smoothylagrange} handles $\sum_g \theta_g = 1$ and not $\theta_g \geq 0$, which
    is the constraint that can still bind. The admission rule of Section \ref{pgn} keeps topics with
    no evidence out of $G$ altogether, which is most of them, but passing that rule is necessary and
    not sufficient: a topic whose counts its neighbours explain better is driven to zero all the same.
    So the maximiser may lie on the boundary of the simplex, where the stationarity condition holds as
    the inequality
    $\sum_n \hat{c}_n \varphi_{ng} / (\varphi\theta)_n \leq C$. Ascent therefore needs either the full
    Karush--Kuhn--Tucker conditions, or a projection of each iterate back onto the simplex, or a
    mirror step in the Kullback--Leibler geometry that keeps positivity by construction. All three
    work, and all three need a step size.

    \paragraph{Expectation--maximisation.} Substituting $\nu = C$ into the stationarity condition
    and reading it as a fixed point gives the iteration directly, with no step size and no projection.
    It is expectation--maximisation \citep{dempster1977em} in the form
    \citet{hofmann1999plsa} gives it for mixture proportions, with the topic--word distributions
    held fixed:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyem}
        \theta_g \;\longleftarrow\; \frac{\theta_g}{C} \sum_{n \in N_s}
            \frac{\hat{c}_n \, \varphi_{ng}}{(\varphi\theta)_n}.
    \end{equation}
    %
    So the two are not rivals but two readings of one condition. The quantity
    $\varphi_{ng}\theta_g / (\varphi\theta)_n$ is the share of the count at $n$ that topic $g$ is responsible for;
    the step reassigns each $\hat{c}_n$ among the topics in that proportion and renormalises. Four
    properties make it the one to implement.

    It needs no constraint machinery. The update sums to one over $g$ by construction, because the
    responsibilities do, so the simplex is preserved exactly and no multiplier or projection appears.
    Non-negativity is preserved for the same reason. It has no step size, so nothing has to be tuned
    or line-searched. It increases the likelihood at every step, by the usual
    minorise--maximise argument, and that argument needs only the responsibilities to be a
    distribution over $g$, which they are by construction. And with the objective
    concave, the point it converges to is the global maximum rather than a stationary point that
    happens to be near where it started.

    Each iteration costs one pass over the non-zeros of $\varphi$. Those are few: a column is
    supported on the path from its taxon to the root, so $\varphi$ has of the order of one entry per
    taxon per level rather than one per taxon per node. The smoothing of
    Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} does not change that, being spread over a row's own path rather
    than over the tree: it fills in the zeros a column had \emph{within} its support and adds none
    outside it.

    \todo{Two known weaknesses to state rather than discover. EM converges linearly, and slowly when
    two topics are near-collinear --- which is exactly the degenerate case of Section \ref{pgn}, so
    slow convergence is a symptom worth reporting rather than only a cost. And the update is
    zero-preserving: a topic that starts at zero stays there, so the iteration is started uniform.
    If the sizes turn out to warrant it, SQUAREM-style acceleration or a few Newton steps on the
    support at the end are the usual remedies; measure before adding either.}

    \subsection{From topics to abundances}\label{abundances}
    What Equation \eqref{smoothylikelihood} estimates is a share of $k$-mers, and therefore a share of
    \emph{sequence}. It is not a share of organisms. A genome twice as long contributes twice the
    $k$-mers at equal cell count, so reporting $\theta_g$ as an abundance overstates the large genomes
    and understates the small ones by exactly their length ratio. The two quantities are both worth
    reporting and must not be confused, which in practice they often are.

    Relative abundance follows by reweighting each topic. The unnormalised quantity is
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyabundance}
        \tilde{a}_g \;=\; \frac{\theta_g}{R_g / \lvert G_g \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}}} ,
        \qquad
        \lvert G_g \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}} \;=\;
            \frac{R_g^{2}}{\sum_{h \,\preceq\, g} \rho_h(g)^{2}} ,
    \end{equation}
    %
    with $R_g$ the row total of Equation \eqref{smoothypng} and $\rho_h(g)$ the contribution of a
    single genome to it, both as Equation \eqref{smoothyrho} defines them. The sum runs over the
    genomes that equation sums, those whose taxon lies at or below $g$'s node, and the numerator is
    their total squared: $R_g^{2} = \bigl( \sum_{h \preceq g} \rho_h(g) \bigr)^{2}$ by the same
    equation, which is why only the squares have to be stored beside the row. At a data taxon there is one of them,
    $\lvert G_g \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1$ and the divisor is $R_g$ itself; above the data taxa it is
    more, for the reason given below. The abundance is that quantity
    normalised over all topics, the ones above the data taxa among them:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothynorm}
        a_g \;=\; \frac{\tilde{a}_g}{\sum_{g' \in G} \tilde{a}_{g'}} .
    \end{equation}
    %
    Including them makes $a$ an account of the sample rather than of the part of it the database
    happens to cover. The narrower reading is not lost by it: dividing among the data taxa alone is
    $a_g / (1 - \sum a_{t})$, summing over the admitted topics that are not data taxa, one division
    away for a reader who wants it, whereas the whole-sample account cannot be recovered from the
    narrower one at all.

    For a data taxon the divisor is the genome's own $k$-mer count and nothing more. That a length
    has to be divided out at all is not a nicety: \citet{beszteri2010} show genome size to be a
    source of bias in comparative metagenomics in its own right, one that moves conclusions drawn
    between samples rather than only the figures within one. $\theta_g$ is a
    share of $k$-mers, so dividing by the number a single organism of $g$ contributes ---
    $\lvert K(g) \rvert = \sum_c (\lvert c \rvert - k + 1)$ over its contigs --- turns a share of
    sequence into a count of organisms. That Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance} divides by $R_g$
    instead is a convenience and not a second correction: the entry phase stores every $k$-mer of a
    genome it enters, so all of them are somewhere in $d$ and $R_g = \lvert K(g) \rvert$ exactly. The
    goal of Section \ref{pgn} produces $R_g$ on the way to the fractions, so no length has to be
    computed or stored.

    The two part company above the data taxa, where the genomes pooled include ones never entered,
    held only where they overlap something that was. There $R_g$ falls short of $\lvert K(g) \rvert$ by the
    share $d$ does not hold, and $R_g$ is the one wanted: $\theta_g$ counts observed $k$-mers, and an
    organism held thinly yields fewer of them per unit of sequence present, so the shortfall belongs
    in the divisor rather than being corrected away.

    A topic that is not a genome reads differently. A topic admitted by
    Equation \eqref{smoothyadmit} above the data taxa pools every genome beneath its node, the ones
    the tree cannot name among them. Its row counts by
    frequency, so its total is the sum over the genomes pooled --- the database's hold on all of them
    together --- and dividing by that would count copies of the whole set rather than organisms of
    it. The hold on one member is what is needed. That is the work
    $\lvert G_g \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}}$ does in Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance}, and why the goal of
    Section \ref{pgn} stores what it needs beside every row.

    Why the count is effective and not plain is worth a paragraph, because the plain one fails badly
    and fails often. A reference collection is full of fragments: a clade may hold one finished
    genome and a draft contributing a single $k$-mer, and a count of members calls that a pool of
    two. The total $R_t$ hardly moves --- one $k$-mer --- while the divisor halves, so the abundance
    doubles. Three such fragments quadruple it. What is wanted is not how many genomes were pooled
    but how many the pool amounts to, and
    $\lvert G_t \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}} = \bigl( \sum_{h \preceq t} \rho_h(t) \bigr)^2 /
    \sum_{h \preceq t} \rho_h(t)^{2}$ is that: one finished
    genome beside a one-$k$-mer draft gives $1.000$, two comparable genomes $1.994$, ten comparable
    ones exactly $10$. Where the members are alike it is the count, and it departs from it only
    where the count was misreporting. Appendix \ref{effexample} sets those four pools out side by
    side with the divisor each produces.

    Where the square comes from is worth setting out, together with what it assumes, because the
    exponent does real work and is not derived from nothing. Written out,
    $R_t / \lvert G_t \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}} = \sum_{h \preceq t} \rho_h(t)^{2} / \sum_{h \preceq t}
    \rho_h(t)$, which is the hold of the genome that a $k$-mer drawn at random from the pool belongs
    to. The two factors of $\rho$ are not the same kind of quantity, and it is worth keeping them
    apart since $\rho$ is a count of $k$-mers and one of them is being used as a probability. Draw a
    $k$-mer uniformly from the $R_t$ the pool holds. It is $h$'s with probability
    $\rho_h(t) / R_t$ --- a count over a count, and dimensionless --- and the genome it then turns
    out to belong to holds $\rho_h(t)$ of them. So the expected hold is
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothysizebias}
        \sum_{h \,\preceq\, t} \frac{\rho_h(t)}{R_t} \, \rho_h(t)
        \;=\; \frac{\sum_{h \preceq t} \rho_h(t)^{2}}{R_t} ,
    \end{equation}
    %
    which is the divisor above, $R_t$ being $\sum_h \rho_h(t)$ by Equation \eqref{smoothyrho}. One
    factor of $\rho$ is normalised and one is not; the normalisation is the $R_t$ in the denominator,
    and what comes out is a count of $k$-mers, as a divisor for $\theta_t$ has to be.

    That is a proxy, and what it stands in for is not available. Write $a_h$ for the abundance of
    each genome of the clade. What the sample yields from the clade goes as $\sum_h a_h \rho_h$ and
    what is wanted is $\sum_h a_h$, so the divisor that would be exactly right is the mean of $\rho$
    weighted by $a$ --- and $a$ is what the estimate is for. Every choice of divisor is therefore a
    guess at $a$. The arithmetic mean guesses that the members are equally likely to be present,
    which is what admits the fragment; the effective count guesses $a_h \propto \rho_h$, which is
    what excludes it.

    Neither guess is true, and the second is not defended by supposing a large genome likelier to be
    present, which it is not. It is defended on narrower ground: a fragment's $\rho$ being tiny goes
    with its not being a distinct organism at all, but a poorly assembled record of a relative
    already in the pool. Weighting it down is right for that reason rather than the one the algebra
    suggests. The exponent is a convention on top of it --- $(\sum \rho)^2 / \sum \rho^2$ is the
    inverse Simpson index, the effective count of order two, and other orders reduce to the plain
    count for equal members just as well. Two is the one with a closed form and long use behind it.

    What the divisor is a hold \emph{of} is settled by the range of the row, and settled in the way
    the estimate needs. Read on $\mathrm{path}(t)$, the row of $t$ carries what each genome beneath
    contributes at $t$ and above and nothing it holds privately lower down, so $R_t$ is the shared
    hold of the clade and $R_t / \lvert G_t \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}}$ the shared hold of one member of
    it. That is the
    right scale for what a topic at $t$ actually estimates. Where the counts at $t$ are owed to an
    organism the database cannot name, that organism stands in $d$ only where it overlaps something
    stored --- by its shared part and nothing else --- so it is measured against a divisor built the
    same way. The pooling running over every genome of the clade rather than over the omitted ones
    alone costs nothing here: what the named genomes contribute to $R_t$ is their shared part too,
    since their private $k$-mers sit at their own nodes and those are not on $\mathrm{path}(t)$.

    \paragraph{Coarser answers.} The fit is at the finest resolution the sample supports, a topic
    sitting at each node it gives evidence at. An answer at any coarser taxon is a sum over it,
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyagg}
        a_t \;=\; \sum_{g \,\preceq\, t} a_g ,
    \end{equation}
    %
    over the admitted topics at or below $t$. The relation $\preceq$ is what makes that
    \emph{at or below}: where $t$ is itself a topic --- which it is whenever the sample gave evidence
    at $t$ that the taxa beneath it could not explain --- its own $a_t$ is one of the terms, and the
    aggregate is the named organisms of the clade together with what the database could not name in
    it. Leaving it out would report the clade as though its unnamed part were absent. The sum is over
    $a$ and not over $\theta$: an abundance is a share of organisms and organisms add, whereas
    $\theta$ is a share of $k$-mers weighted by how much of each genome the database holds, and those
    do not.

    Aggregating rather than refitting makes the degeneracy of Section \ref{pgn} harmless.
    Where two topics cannot be told apart their split is arbitrary, but their sum is determined, and
    at any taxon above them the sum is what Equation \eqref{smoothyagg} reports. The same holds of the
    admission rule: a topic it rejects contributes its counts to its neighbours, and the aggregate
    over a clade containing both is unmoved. Near-identical assemblies of one species are the case
    that matters, and the case the per-genome transformation creates.

    Amplicon data changes this equation in one place and the rest of the method in one; both are set
    out in Section \ref{amplicon}.

    \todo{Two limits of Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance} to state rather than let a reader find. The
    lengths implicit in $R_g$ are those of the reference genomes and not of the sample's organisms, so
    a strain carrying a large accessory complement is measured against the wrong ruler. And above the
    data taxa the pooling runs over an unevenly sampled set --- the sequencing-effort bias that shapes
    the row itself, entering the abundance a second time. The effective count meets the second
    halfway and no further: it stops a fragment counting for a genome in the divisor, but a clade
    sequenced a thousand times over still enters its own row a thousand times, and that is a property
    of the collection rather than of the organisms. Say which half is fixed.}

    \paragraph{What the database cannot account for.} One figure per topic is reported on its own and
    is not an abundance at all,
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyunaccounted}
        u_t \;=\; \theta_t \quad\text{for an admitted topic } t \text{ above the data taxa,}
    \end{equation}
    %
    the share of the sample's \emph{sequence} that the database cannot name \emph{and that belongs
    under $t$}. Nothing has to be set aside to obtain it: the data taxa beneath $t$ are topics of
    their own, and their rows carry the mass they contribute at $t$, so what the fit leaves at $t$ is
    what none of them explains. It is a statement about the database's coverage of this sample rather
    than about an organism in it, which is why it is left out of the normalisation and stated as it
    is. Their sum over those topics is the unaccounted share of the whole sample,
    but the terms are worth reporting apart:
    unnamed material under \emph{Streptococcus} and unnamed material under \emph{Nocardia} are
    different findings, and a single residue would say neither.

    Its magnitude can be read directly, which was not true of an earlier form of this method. Because
    Section \ref{pgn} normalises every row over its own total, no topic is handicapped by how little
    of it the database happens to hold: a topic with thin coverage does not have to carry a larger
    $\theta$ to explain the same counts.

    The entries $a_t$ of Equation \eqref{smoothynorm} at such a topic read as the share of organisms
    the database could not name in that clade. They are the entries of the profile to be read with more
    caution than the rest, and the caution belongs on them rather than on the choice of denominator.
    Leaving them out of the sum would not make the others safer: $\theta$ is fitted with those
    topics competing under $\sum_g \theta_g = 1$, so every $\theta_g$ already carries
    their influence, and a different denominator would change what the numbers mean rather than how
    far they can be trusted.

    And they are crude, for a reason worth stating rather than hedging about --- one that is not
    peculiar to them but only at its worst there. Every pooled topic divides by a hold averaged over
    its members and thereby assumes the organism present is typical of the sequence pooled into it.
    Weighting by contribution makes that assumption less wrong than a plain mean would --- a
    fragment no longer counts for as much as a finished genome --- but it does not make it right. A data taxon pools nothing,
    being one genome; a topic above them pools the members of its clade, the ones the database cannot
    name among them, which is where the assumption is at its weakest and the figure at its crudest.
    It is, however, far better than it would be with a single global residue: the assumption is then
    only that an unnamed \emph{Streptococcus} resembles the
    average omitted \emph{Streptococcus}, rather than the average omitted organism of a whole
    bacterial division. The figure says whether the unnamed material of a clade is of the order of its
    named organisms or dwarfs them, and it should not be read for more than that.

    \todo{Say what $u$ is not. It is a share of the $k$-mers the fit sees, so it misses two things at
    once: an organism with no $k$-mer in $d$ at all, whose reads are never reported at any node and
    never enter $c_n$; and unnamed material sitting under a taxon the admission rule rejected, which
    is explained away by that taxon's neighbours. A reader will take $u$ for `the fraction of this
    sample I cannot account for', and the honest version of that sentence has to name both.}

    \subsection{Amplicon protocols}\label{amplicon}
    Section \ref{model} rules out metabarcoding and Section \ref{error} says why: the reads come from
    one amplified region rather than from the genome, so the $k$-mers of a taxon are not equally
    likely to be seen. The obstacle is a mismatch between what was sequenced and what the reference
    is taken to be, and it is removed by changing the second rather than by giving up the model. Let
    $A(g)$ be the region the primers delimit in $g$ --- the 16S gene, or the variable window within
    it.

    \paragraph{The rows.} Nothing in Section \ref{pgn} is rebuilt. The goal already walks each genome
    position by position and looks up the $k$-mer it finds; all that is added is a test applied
    before the counter is raised, admitting the $k$-mer when its position lies inside $A(g)$ and
    discarding it otherwise. The database $d$ is untouched and the LCA positions are the ones it
    already holds, so only which $k$-mers are counted changes. The own counts, the pooling of
    Equation \eqref{smoothyrows} carries over verbatim, and
    Equation \eqref{smoothypng} reads $p(n \mid g) = c^{A}(g,n) / R^{A}_g$ with both terms computed
    over $A(g)$. The smoothing of Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} carries over as it stands, over the
    same $\mathrm{path}(g)$, and it earns its place here more than in the shotgun case: a marker is
    shared more widely than a genome, so an amplicon row is concentrated higher in the tree and has
    more zeros along its own path for one silent node to exploit.

    The test is on the position and not on the $k$-mer: the same sequence may occur inside the region
    in one genome and outside it in another, and it is where it sits that decides whether a read
    could have produced it. Every copy of the marker passes, and none is deduplicated --- which
    matters for the next paragraph. What the region is for a given genome comes from the annotation,
    from an rRNA prediction, or from an in-silico amplification with the primer pair in hand; the
    method needs the coordinates and does not care which produced them.

    The rows come out concentrated higher in the tree than their shotgun counterparts, a conserved
    marker being shared more widely than a genome, which is a fact about the data and not a defect in
    the estimate.

    \paragraph{The abundances.} The divisor changes in Section \ref{abundances} and nothing else
    does. The contributions $\rho_h$ are the restricted ones, counted over $A(h)$ like everything
    else here, so the row total is $R^{A}_g$ and the effective count is taken over the same
    restricted contributions. Both parts of the divisor therefore carry the length of the amplified
    region where they carried the length of the genome --- which is what the counts are now a sample
    of. The effective count does not simply survive the restriction, since the marker is not a fixed
    share of every genome: a member carrying more copies of it keeps proportionally more, and the
    pool is measured as the survey sees it rather than as the assemblies stand.
    Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance} is otherwise unaltered:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyamplicon}
        \tilde{a}_g \;=\; \frac{\theta_g}{R^{A}_g / \lvert G_g \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}}} .
    \end{equation}
    %
    Both corrections a marker survey needs are inside that divisor, and neither is to be applied
    again. Region length is the first: $R^{A}_g$ counts the amplicon $k$-mer positions of $g$ that
    $d$ holds, so a taxon whose region is longer contributes proportionally more of them and is
    divided by proportionally more, exactly as genome length was under Section \ref{abundances}. It
    varies between taxa --- the variable windows differ in length --- and the variation is carried
    per genome rather than assumed away.

    Copy number is the second, and here the cancellation is worth spelling out because the
    correction is the familiar one in the literature. A genome carrying $m$ ribosomal operons has $m$
    copies of the region, every one of them passing the filter and counted by frequency, so
    $R^{A}_g$ is already $m$ times what a single-copy genome would give. The sample is inflated by the
    same $m$, and the two cancel in $\theta_g / R^{A}_g$. The bias corrects itself for the reason it
    exists at all --- the reference is counted the way the sample is --- and it does so only as long
    as the filter admits every copy.

    Both hold on one condition: that the region the filter uses is the region the primers gave. Where
    it is not --- coordinates for the whole gene against a run that amplified one window --- the
    length in $R^{A}_g$ is the wrong length, and since windows differ between taxa the error does not
    cancel but tilts the profile towards whichever taxa have the longer windows.

    \paragraph{What the change costs.} Resolution. A conserved marker separates taxa far less than a
    genome does, so the proportional columns of Section \ref{pgn} become the rule at species rank
    rather than the exception, and an amplicon run should be read at genus or above. The consistency
    ratio of Section \ref{error} has to be recomputed over $A(g)$ as well --- $u_{t,d}$ being the
    distinct $k$-mers of the amplified region that $d$ stores for $t$ --- after which $r$ measures
    what it was built to measure and $\beta$ wants fixing again against amplicon data.

    \section{A Dirichlet prior and a sampler}\label{prior}
    Everything above estimates $\theta$ from the counts alone. A symmetric Dirichlet prior
    $\theta \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha \mathbf{1})$, the scalar concentration of topic modeling, is the
    natural addition. It is set down here rather than in the method
    because it does not, on the evidence so far, earn its cost: the useful range of $\alpha$ is
    exactly the range in which maximising fails, so taking the prior means giving up the fixed point
    of Section \ref{inference} for a sampler, giving up concavity, and committing to analysing
    samples in sets rather than singly. That is a different method, not an option within this one.
    It is kept because the reasoning behind it is worth not repeating from scratch, and because the
    objection to it is contingent rather than absolute. That objection is the one of
    Section \ref{model}: $k$-mer counts overstate the evidence by roughly the read length, so a prior
    has nothing left to weigh against. \citet{krakenuniq} put a measured figure on that inflation, up
    to about $L - k$ per read, so it is the ordinary case and not a worst one. Read counts would not
    rescue it --- Section \ref{model} gives
    the reason they are unusable at all --- but anything that restored an honest sample size would,
    whether that is thinning the $k$-mers of a read, an explicit overdispersion term, or a likelihood
    stated per read against a $p(n \mid g)$ built for one.

    \subsection{Where EM still works}\label{mapworks}
    Maximum-a-posteriori estimation of a topic model
    \citep{defreitas2001bayesian,chien2008adaptive} adds the prior to the maximum-likelihood fit of
    \citet{hofmann1999plsa} as a pseudo-count. For $\alpha \geq 1$ the objective is
    $\ell(\theta) + (\alpha - 1) \sum_g \log \theta_g$ and the iteration keeps its shape, gaining
    only a pseudo-count:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothymapem}
        \theta_g \;\longleftarrow\; \frac{\bigl( \sum_n \hat{c}_n \, \varphi_{ng} \theta_g /
            (\varphi\theta)_n \bigr) + \alpha - 1}{C + \lvert G \rvert (\alpha - 1)} .
    \end{equation}
    %
    The prior then shrinks the estimate towards the uniform distribution. That is smoothing, and it is
    the opposite of what a Dirichlet prior is usually wanted for here.

    \paragraph{Mode and mean differ by one.} The pseudo-count carries an offset that is easy to lose
    sight of when the two estimators of this appendix are compared. Equation \eqref{smoothymapem}
    converges to the posterior \emph{mode},
    $(m_g + \alpha - 1) / (M + \lvert G \rvert (\alpha - 1))$, while the sampler below reports the
    posterior \emph{mean}, $(m_g + \alpha) / (M + \lvert G \rvert \alpha)$. The two differ by exactly
    one in the concentration: the mode at $\alpha$ is the mean at $\alpha - 1$. Setting the same
    $\alpha$ for both and reading the difference in their output as a difference between maximising
    and sampling would therefore be a mistake --- part of it is only the offset, and at the small
    $\alpha$ of interest here that part is not small. It is also why the two have different admissible
    ranges: the mean is defined for every $\alpha > 0$ and the mode only from $\alpha = 1$ upwards.

    This agrees with the MAP update of \citet{asuncion2009smoothing}, whose estimate
    $\hat{\theta}_{kj} = (N_{kj} + \alpha - 1)/(N_j + K\alpha - K)$ is
    Equation \eqref{smoothymapem} in their notation, $K\alpha - K$ being $\lvert G \rvert (\alpha -
    1)$ here. They reach the same conclusion about the offset from the other direction, observing that
    MAP offsets by $-1$ against collapsed Gibbs sampling and that the two are brought into agreement
    by incrementing $\alpha$ by one --- which is why their own hyperparameter search shifts its grid
    by one for MAP, hyperparameters below one giving it negative probabilities. Only the mixture side
    of their derivation applies here: they estimate the topic--word distributions as well, and ours
    are given by the database.

    \subsection{Where it does not}\label{gibbs}
    Sparsity needs $\alpha < 1$, and there the MAP is not merely harder
    but ill-posed: the Dirichlet density is unbounded at the corners of the simplex, so the objective
    has no finite maximum and Equation \eqref{smoothymapem} produces negative numerators that have to
    be clamped, which is a repair rather than a solution. The remedy is to stop maximising over
    $\theta$ and integrate it out. Collapsed Gibbs sampling \citep{griffiths2004scientific} does that, and it is markedly cheaper here
    than in the corpus setting it comes from: the topics are fixed, so only the assignment of each
    counted $k$-mer occurrence is sampled and never a topic--word distribution. For an occurrence
    observed at node $n$,
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothygibbs}
        p(z = g \mid \cdot) \;\propto\; \varphi_{ng} \, \bigl( m_g^{\neg} + \alpha \bigr),
    \end{equation}
    %
    with $m_g^{\neg}$ the number of occurrences currently assigned to $g$, that one excluded. A sweep
    visits every occurrence; the estimate is the posterior mean
    $\mathbb{E}[\theta_g] = (m_g + \alpha) / (M + \lvert G \rvert \alpha)$ over the retained sweeps,
    with $M$ the total number of occurrences.

    The path structure of Section \ref{pgn} bounds the work, and the smoothing leaves it alone. An
    occurrence observed at node $n$ can only belong to a topic whose column has support at $n$, and
    $\varphi_{ng} > 0$ only where $n$ lies on the path from $g$ to the root --- that is, only for the
    topics lying at or beneath $n$. The candidate set is therefore small for a specific node and
    large only for the nodes near the root, which is the right shape: the sampler spends its time on
    exactly the occurrences that are ambiguous.
    Occurrences at one node are moreover exchangeable, so the state is the assignment counts per node
    rather than a label per $k$-mer, and a sweep re-draws a node's occurrences from the urn in turn
    instead of touching millions of tokens individually.

    \subsection{Estimating \texorpdfstring{$\alpha$}{alpha} needs more than one sample}
    Everything so
    far has concerned a single statistic $s$. A concentration parameter cannot be estimated from it:
    $\alpha$ describes how $\theta$ varies from one sample to the next, and one sample exhibits no
    variation. Fitting it anyway returns whatever value best explains the single $\theta$ at hand,
    which is not an estimate of anything.

    So the estimation is stated over a set. Let $s_1, \dots, s_m$ be statistics produced against the
    same database, $c^{(i)}_n$ the count that $s_i$ reports at node $n$, and
    $\theta^{(i)} = p(g \mid s_i)$ its mixture. The samples share the prior and nothing else:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyjoint}
        p\bigl(c^{(1)}, \dots, c^{(m)} \bigm| \alpha\bigr) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{m} \int
            \mathrm{Dir}\bigl(\theta^{(i)} \bigm| \alpha \mathbf{1}\bigr)
            \prod_{n \in N_{s_i}} \bigl(\varphi\theta^{(i)}\bigr)_n^{\,c^{(i)}_n} \, d\theta^{(i)} .
    \end{equation}
    %
    Each $\theta^{(i)}$ stays a quantity of its own sample --- the aim is still one abundance
    distribution per sample, not a shared one --- while $\alpha$ is common to all $m$ and is what the
    set is pooled to determine. That is empirical Bayes, and it is why the method analyses samples in
    sets. A clinical cohort measured on one database is exactly such a set.

    Minka's update is the fixed point for $\alpha$: \citet{asuncion2009smoothing} apply the Dirichlet
    estimator of \citet{minka2000dirichlet} as a step of the fitting procedure and give it that name.
    It belongs to the sampler and not to the maximisation of Section \ref{mapworks}: the same authors
    leave MAP out of their hyperparameter-learning experiments precisely because Minka's update does
    not apply to it. So the two halves of this appendix do not combine into one procedure. Taking the
    prior means taking the sampler, and estimating $\alpha$ rather than fixing it means the same
    again. With $m_{ig}$ the
    occurrences of $s_i$ currently assigned to topic $g$ and $M_i = \sum_g m_{ig}$,
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyalpha}
        \alpha \;\longleftarrow\; \alpha \,
            \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{g \in G} \bigl( \psi(m_{ig} + \alpha) - \psi(\alpha) \bigr)}
                 {\lvert G \rvert \sum_{i=1}^{m} \bigl( \psi(M_i + \lvert G \rvert \alpha)
                     - \psi(\lvert G \rvert \alpha) \bigr)} ,
    \end{equation}
    %
    interleaved with the sampler, which carries an assignment state per sample and sweeps them in
    turn. The update is derived by bounding the Dirichlet log-likelihood and converges monotonically,
    so it needs no step size either --- the same property that recommends the iteration of
    Section \ref{inference} over an ascent method. Alternatively $\alpha$ is given a hyperprior and
    sampled with the rest, which costs less thought and about as much time.

    \todo{What $m$ has to be for the estimate to mean anything, and what to do below it. A single
    sample is stated above as uninformative; a handful is not obviously better, and the paper should
    say where the boundary lies rather than leave a reader to assume any cohort will do. The
    fallback when $m$ is too small is a fixed $\alpha$ chosen and declared, which is honest, rather
    than an estimated one that is noise wearing a number.}

    \subsection{Counts that are not integers}\label{integers}
    A sampler over occurrences needs occurrences, and the
    corrected $\hat{c}_n$ of Section \ref{error} are not whole numbers. Rounding them is the obvious
    repair and the wrong one: it distorts the correction most where counts are small, which is where
    a rare taxon lives. It is not needed. The correction is a single factor $(1 - e_b)^k$ applied to
    every $c_n$ alike, and what the prior weighs itself against is the ratio of counts to
    concentration, not their absolute size. Sampling the raw integer occurrences $c_n$ under
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{smoothyalphaeff}
        \alpha_{\mathrm{eff}} \;=\; \alpha \, (1 - e_b)^k
    \end{equation}
    %
    leaves that ratio exactly where $\hat{c}_n$ with $\alpha$ would have put it, and keeps the state
    integral.

    That holds only while the correction is a single factor, and Section \ref{error} has since given
    it a second one that is not. The consistency weight $w(r_n)$ differs from node to node, so no
    rescaling of $\alpha$ can reproduce it: the weights change the counts relative to \emph{each
    other} and not merely their common size. A sampler would have to carry them as per-occurrence
    weights, which is no longer collapsed Gibbs sampling but a weighted variant of it, or fall back on
    rounding $w(r_n) c_n$ with the loss that was the objection in the first place. The iteration of
    Section \ref{inference} has no such difficulty --- a weighted likelihood is what it already
    maximises --- which is one more reason the prior sits in this appendix and not in the method.

    \todo{What the sampler costs and how it is run: burn-in, how many sweeps are retained, and what
    is used to decide that it has mixed. Report it rather than choosing it silently --- a sampler with
    an undeclared burn-in is not a reproducible measurement. The degenerate taxa of
    Section \ref{pgn} are where mixing will be worst, since it is precisely there that the posterior
    has a ridge rather than a peak, so they are the place to look.}

    \section{Sparsity and the value of \texorpdfstring{$\alpha$}{alpha}}\label{sparsity}
    Section \ref{pgn} keeps absent taxa out of $G$ by admitting a topic only on evidence. A prior can
    do the same work from inside the fit. Any $\alpha$ below one enforces sparsity, and the exact
    value matters less than the field's rules of thumb suggest --- $\alpha = 50 / \lvert G \rvert$
    after \citet{griffiths2004scientific} is one suggestion from one paper, not a law. The inflated
    counts of Section \ref{model} are no obstacle and in fact help: an unoccupied topic is drawn with
    weight $\varphi_{ng} \alpha$ against $\varphi_{ng'} m_{g'}$ for an occupied one, so the larger the
    counts the less easily an empty topic revives.

    What the inflation costs is calibration and confidence rather than sparsity. A prior weighed
    against counts that overstate the evidence by a factor between $k$ and $L - k + 1$ is switching
    sparsity on and regularising nothing, and $\alpha$ should not be read as though it had a strength.
    The same inflation makes the posterior sure of itself: a taxon carried by two reads can be pruned
    with none of the doubt that ought to attach to it. And $\alpha$ cannot carry the threshold of
    Section \ref{pgn}: rejecting what fewer than $T$ $k$-mers support would take
    $\alpha \gtrsim T$, above one for any useful $T$ and past the point where anything is sparse.

    That is why the threshold sits in the method and the prior in an appendix. Two further options
    need neither: fit, drop what falls below a threshold, and \emph{refit} on the surviving support;
    or replace the fixed point of Section \ref{inference} with an optimiser that reaches the boundary,
    projected gradient or an active set, which gives exact zeros in finitely many steps and leaves the
    objective alone.

    \section{Two ways of getting the smoothing's scope wrong}\label{nodesmooth}
    Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} spreads a fixed share $\lambda$ over the nodes of
    $\mathrm{path}(g)$. Two other scopes suggest themselves --- the whole node set, and a fixed
    share at each node rather than across them --- and both are set out here, since each fails for a
    reason worth knowing rather than an obvious one.

    \subsection{Over the node set}\label{nodesmoothset}
    Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} spreads a row's background over $\mathrm{path}(g)$. The obvious
    alternative is to spread it over the nodes the sample reports, $\tilde{p}(n \mid g) = (1 -
    \lambda) p(n \mid g) + \lambda / \lvert N_s \rvert$ for every $n \in N_s$, which is what a reader
    coming from the retrieval literature would reach for: the background there is the collection, not
    a per-document support. It is kept here because it is the natural first attempt and because the
    reason it fails is not the obvious one.

    The obvious objection is that it wastes its mass. A row occupies $\lvert \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert$
    nodes, ten or twenty, against an $\lvert N_s \rvert$ in the hundreds or thousands, so all but a
    small fraction of $\lambda$ lands where the row has no business being --- and it asserts, of a
    genome whose $k$-mers the update guarantees are on its own lineage, that they might be anywhere.
    It also fills $\varphi$ in, which costs the sparsity the cost argument of
    Section \ref{inference} and the candidate set of Appendix \ref{prior} both rest on. None of that
    is fatal on its own.

    What is fatal is that it inverts the purpose. A topic's own node is covered by no other topic:
    under Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} the mixture there is $\theta_g$ times that row and nothing
    else, so $\theta_g = 0$ would make the mixture zero where a count stands and the likelihood with
    it. A genome the sample gives evidence for at its own node therefore \emph{cannot} be driven out,
    whatever happens elsewhere on its lineage --- which is what the smoothing was introduced to
    guarantee. Spread the background over $N_s$ and every other topic acquires a floor at that node
    too. They can then account for the genome's own evidence between them, $\theta_g = 0$ becomes
    feasible, and where a node of the lineage carries enough counts it becomes optimal: the
    stationarity score of the excluded topic falls below $C$ and the boundary is a genuine maximiser.

    A constructed example makes the size of it plain. Take a genome present in the sample with $50$
    counts at its own node, silent at a shared node carrying $c_B$, fitted against a competitor that
    is not silent there. Under Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} the estimate falls with $c_B$ but never
    reaches zero --- $2.2 \times 10^{-2}$, $2.5 \times 10^{-3}$, $2.5 \times 10^{-4}$ as $c_B$ runs
    over $2 \times 10^3$, $2 \times 10^4$, $2 \times 10^5$ --- and exclusion stays impossible at
    every $c_B$. Smoothing over $N_s$ instead gives $2.0 \times 10^{-2}$, $8.6 \times 10^{-5}$ and
    then zero exactly. At ordinary counts the two agree to a percent; they part where the floor is
    the only thing holding the topic in, which is the case it exists for.

    So the choice is not between a tighter and a looser floor. Spreading over the path puts the floor
    where a genome's evidence can be contradicted and nowhere else; spreading over the node set puts
    it everywhere, and thereby dissolves the one place a genome cannot be argued away from.

    \subsection{A fixed share at each node}\label{nodesmoothper}
    The second alternative keeps to the path and changes how the share is divided: give each node of
    $\mathrm{path}(g)$ a mass $\lambda$ of its own rather than $\lambda / \lvert \mathrm{path}(g)
    \rvert$, so that $\tilde{p}(n \mid g) = (1 - \lambda \lvert \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert) p(n \mid g)
    + \lambda$. The motivation is exact: under Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} a deep genome's floor
    is smaller than a shallow one's, and path length is a fact about where a genome sits rather than
    evidence about a sample, so a floor that does not vary with it looks like an improvement.

    It is one, and it is worth almost nothing. Where the floor binds --- a genome present, silent at
    a node carrying two million counts --- the fixed-share-per-node scheme is exactly flat across
    depth and Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} drifts by half a percent between a path of three nodes
    and one of forty. That is the whole of the gain.

    The cost is not almost nothing. Giving each node $\lambda$ makes the background of a row total
    $\lambda \lvert \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert$, so a deep row is mostly background and its real
    structure washes out. At $\lambda = 0.01$ and forty nodes, two fifths of the row is flat, and a
    topic that is \emph{not} silent --- which is the ordinary case, at almost every node --- comes
    out a quarter lower than under Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} for no reason but its depth. The
    dependence on path length is therefore not removed by the change. It is moved from the floor,
    where it costs half a percent and only where a topic is nearly evicted, to the signal, where it
    costs a quarter and applies everywhere.

    A constraint comes with it. The scheme needs $\lambda \lvert \mathrm{path}(g) \rvert < 1$ for
    every topic, so $\lambda$ is capped by the deepest lineage the database carries and one
    finely-resolved clade lowers it for everything else. Setting $\lambda$ to match
    Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth}'s floor at one depth then leaves it several times weaker at
    shallower ones.

    Behind all of it is a scale that Equation \eqref{smoothysmooth} gets right by construction. A row
    spread over $L$ nodes has entries of order $1 / L$, so a share of $\lambda / L$ is a constant
    \emph{fraction} of what the row holds at a node, whatever $L$ is. A fixed $\lambda$ per node is a
    constant absolute quantity instead: negligible beside a shallow row's entries and comparable to a
    deep row's. The floor should be measured against the row it protects, and dividing the share is
    what measures it that way.

    \section{The effective genome count, worked through}\label{effexample}
    Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance} divides by $R_t / \lvert G_t \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}}$ rather than
    by $R_t / \lvert G_t \rvert$, and the difference is easiest to see on four pools. Recall that
    $\rho_h$ is what one genome contributes to the row of $t$ --- its $k$-mers that $d$ holds at the
    nodes $\mathrm{path}(t)$ --- so $\sum_h \rho_h = R_t$, and that the goal accumulates that sum and
    the sum of squares as it walks. Take a clade whose complete member contributes two million
    $k$-mers at $t$ and above.

    \begin{table}[!htbp]
        \centering
        \footnotesize
        \setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
        \begin{tabular}{@{}l|r|r|r|r|r|r@{}}
            \textbf{the pool at $t$} & $R_t$ & $\sum_h \rho_h^{2}$ &
                $\lvert G_t \rvert$ & $\lvert G_t \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}}$ &
                $R_t / \lvert G_t \rvert$ & $R_t / \lvert G_t \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}}$\\
            \hline
            complete $+$ one-$k$-mer draft & 2\,000\,001 & $4.0 \times 10^{12}$ & 2 & 1.000 &
                1\,000\,000 & 1\,999\,999\\
            complete $+$ three such drafts & 2\,000\,009 & $4.0 \times 10^{12}$ & 4 & 1.000 &
                500\,002 & 1\,999\,991\\
            two comparable genomes & 3\,800\,000 & $7.2 \times 10^{12}$ & 2 & 1.994 &
                1\,900\,000 & 1\,905\,263\\
            ten comparable genomes & 20\,000\,000 & $4.0 \times 10^{13}$ & 10 & 10.000 &
                2\,000\,000 & 2\,000\,000\\
        \end{tabular}
        \caption{Four pools at one taxon $t$, with the divisor each count produces. The last two
        columns are what Equation \eqref{smoothyabundance} would divide by under the plain count and
        under the effective one. Read the first two rows against the last two: where the members are
        alike the effective count \emph{is} the count, to three decimals at ten members and to within
        a third of a percent at two, and the two divisors agree; where a fragment is present the
        count says two or four members and the effective count says one, because that is how many
        genomes the pool amounts to.}\label{efftable}
    \end{table}

    The first row is the case that motivates the change. A draft assembly contributing a single
    $k$-mer is counted by $\lvert G_t \rvert$ as a member equal to the finished genome beside it, so
    the divisor halves and every abundance at $t$ doubles --- on evidence of one $k$-mer. Three such
    drafts quarter it. Nothing about the clade has changed between the first two rows and the truth
    has not moved: one organism of it holds two million $k$-mers at $t$ and above, which is what the
    effective count returns in both.

    The last two rows are the check in the other direction. Ten genuinely comparable genomes give
    $\lvert G_t \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}} = 10$ exactly and the same divisor as the plain count, and two
    comparable ones give $1.994$ against $2$ --- the small shortfall being real rather than error,
    since the members are not quite equal. So the effective count is not a correction applied on top
    of the count but the count generalised: it coincides with it wherever the count was telling the
    truth.

    The identity behind the last column is worth seeing once. Writing it out,
    $R_t / \lvert G_t \rvert_{\mathrm{eff}} = \bigl( \sum_h \rho_h \bigr) \bigl( \sum_h \rho_h^{2}
    \bigr) / \bigl( \sum_h \rho_h \bigr)^{2} = \sum_h \rho_h^{2} / \sum_h \rho_h$, every sum running
    over the genomes at or below $t$ as in Equation \eqref{smoothyrho}, which is the form
    Section \ref{abundances} argues from and the reason only two accumulators are stored: the
    per-genome $\rho_h$ are needed while the row is pooled and never afterwards.
