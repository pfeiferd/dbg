    \abstract{\textbf{Background:} A $k$-mer database built for read classification records, for
    every $k$-mer, the lowest taxon shared by the genomes that carry it. That is most of what an
    evolutionary distance needs, and it is computed already. We ask what such a database can say
    about the relatedness of the organisms it holds, without alignment and without sketching.

    \textbf{Results:} We estimate the distance between any two genomes of a Genestrip database from
    counters the build produces, adding only a cardinality sketch per genome and node. The lowest
    common ancestor update confines everything two genomes share to a single lineage of the
    taxonomy, so the comparison runs over a path rather than a tree and is made over the whole of
    both genomes rather than over a sample of each. Pairwise distances over a database give an
    overdetermined linear system for the branch lengths of any given topology, which we solve by
    non-negative least squares. Taking the topology from the refined taxonomy itself turns the fit
    into a test: the residual at a node measures how far the established taxonomy departs from the
    sequence data beneath it. That test is sound because the distances do not depend on the taxonomy
    they are used to judge --- they are fixed by the genomes' $k$-mer sets, and a genome filed in the
    wrong clade yields the same distance to its true relative as a correctly filed one. The same
    independence makes the method a way of placing a newly sequenced organism: enter it at a guess,
    and the measurements rather than the guess decide where it belongs, at a cost of one row of the
    matrix. Draft assemblies are admissible: fragmentation costs almost nothing, and incompleteness,
    which costs a great deal, is detectable from the counters already kept, correctable, and costs
    resolution rather than correctness --- a collection of drafts still yields the right tree at
    species level and above.

    \textbf{Conclusions:} Distances and a drawn phylogeny come out of a classification database as a
    by-product, at the price of one sketch per genome and node. What the method compares are genomes
    the build has entered, which a newly sequenced assembly can join at any time; reads from a sample
    are a different question and belong to read classification.}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

A $k$-mer database built for read classification already holds, without being asked, a great deal of
what a phylogeny needs. Genestrip \cite{genestrip-pub} stores each $k$-mer at the lowest taxon shared
by every genome that carries it, so the database records not only which organisms it knows but where
their sequence agrees and where it parts. Genestrip-FT \cite{genestrip-ft} refines that tree,
inserting nodes where the reference taxonomy is coarser than the sequence data warrants. What this
paper sets out is that the two together are enough to estimate evolutionary distances between the
genomes a database holds, to draw those distances on the taxonomy it already carries, and --- because
the fit is overdetermined --- to say how well the taxonomy agrees with the sequence.

The distances turn out not to depend on the taxonomy at all, which is more useful than it sounds.
The quantities behind them are properties of two $k$-mer sets, so a genome filed under the wrong name
yields the same distance to its true relative as a correctly filed one; the tree affects how exactly
the estimate is reached and not what it converges on. Section \ref{taxindep} makes that precise. Two
things follow. The residual test above is not circular, since the measurements it judges the taxonomy
by owe the taxonomy nothing; and a genome whose position is unknown can be entered at a guess and
placed afterwards on the measurements, which Section \ref{placing} sets out and which costs one row
of the distance matrix per organism.

The estimates cost little because the database has done the work. Sections \ref{pairwise}
and \ref{treefit} add one sketch per genome and per node to what a build already produces, and take
the rest from counters that exist. Nothing is aligned and nothing is sampled: the distances are
computed over the whole of each genome's $k$-mers rather than over a sketch of them, which is where
the approach differs from the sketching tools of Section \ref{relatedwork} and where it should
differ most in accuracy, namely between genomes that are close.

Whether the genomes need to be finished is the first question a reference collection raises, and the
answer divides. Contiguity turns out not to matter: the estimate reads $k$-mer sets and knows nothing
of contigs, so cutting a genome into five hundred pieces costs only the $k$-mers spanning the cuts and
shifts the distance by $0.0001$. Completeness matters a great deal in principle, since sequence missing from one
genome is indistinguishable from sequence that diverged, and a genome five per cent short reads as far
from an identical copy of itself as two genuinely different strains are from each other. In a curated
collection it matters less than that in practice: measured across RefSeq, a typical draft is short by
about a hundredth of its genome, which is worth $0.0005$ in distance and belongs below the resolution
anything should be read at. Section \ref{incomplete} sets out both figures, shows that the effect of a
genuinely deficient assembly on the fitted tree is visible in what the method already computes, that
correcting for it is arithmetic where it is worth doing, and that what it costs when uncorrected is
resolution rather than correctness: across a collection of drafts the
topology is recovered whole at species level and above, and it is fine structure within a species that
a collection of half-complete assemblies cannot support. Where a best tree is wanted rather than a
verdict on the taxonomy, Section \ref{besttree} finds that correcting the short assemblies beats
discarding them and by a wide margin --- discarding returns a confident tree of whatever fraction of
the clade happens to have been closed, and that fraction is not a random sample of it. Draft
assemblies are therefore usable, which they had better be, since most of a reference collection is
drafts.

One boundary is worth drawing at the outset, and it is about genomes rather than about names. What
the method compares are two genomes \emph{that the database holds}, so both must have been entered ---
a newly sequenced organism qualifies as soon as its assembly is in the build, whatever it is called
and wherever it is filed, but reads from a sample do not. Assigning reads is read classification,
which is what Genestrip already does and is not this question.

\section{Related work}\label{relatedwork}

Estimating evolutionary distance from $k$-mer content rather than from alignment is established.
Mash \cite{Ondov:2016aa} sketches each genome with MinHash, estimates the Jaccard index between
sketches and converts it to a distance; sourmash \cite{pierce2019sourmash} builds the same idea into
a toolkit, and Skmer \cite{skmer} extends it to unassembled low-coverage reads by modelling the
sketch's incompleteness. All three sample: a sketch of a few thousand hashes stands for a genome of
millions of $k$-mers, and the sampling error sets a floor under how finely two close genomes can be
told apart. FastANI \cite{jain2018fastani} takes the alignment-based route instead, and is accurate
where it applies but does not scale to all pairs of a large collection.

What this paper adds is neither a new sketch nor a new alignment, but the observation that a
classification database has already computed the expensive part. The lowest common ancestor update
that Genestrip performs to make classification precise also localises, for every $k$-mer, the set of
genomes carrying it; Section \ref{pairwise} shows that this confines everything two genomes share to
a single lineage of the tree, which is what makes an exact whole-genome comparison affordable.

On the tree side, fitting branch lengths to a fixed topology by least squares goes back to
\cite{cavalli1967phylogenetic} and \cite{fitch1967construction}, and neighbour joining
\cite{saitou1987nj} remains the standard way to obtain a topology from a distance matrix when none is
given. Here one is given --- the refined taxonomy --- and Section \ref{treefit} treats that as the
hypothesis to be fitted rather than as something to be replaced.

\section{Phylogenetic Mapping}

Genestrip-FT's database refinement lends itself to a comprehensive visualization of even large phylogeny trees with evolutionary distances being integrated. Resulting tree representations refine the existing NCBI taxonomy \cite{taxonomy} and derive from a refined $k$-mer database. Here, we briefly discuss computational aspects for embedding the evolutionary distance in such trees.

   \subsection{The evolutionary distance $d$}
    
We briefly introduce an approximation of the evolutionary distance $d$ as a ratio of mutations in genomes or sequences, respectively. The approximation has been derived before in \cite{skmer} where it is expressed via the Jaccard index over shared $k$-mers between genomes. Besides, \cite{Ondov:2016aa} pointed out a similar approximation. For clarity of discussion, we also derive $d$, but express it in terms of counts available in a Genestrip database and related taxonomy trees. Afterwards, we build on these considerations to adequately integrate $d$ into depicted, refined trees such as produced via Genestrip-FT. 
    
The following assumptions are essentially the same as in \cite{skmer} and \cite{Ondov:2016aa}:
    Let $|s|$ be the length of a sequence $s$ transformed to a sequence $s'$ via mutations of bases. We assume that $|s| = |s'|$ and consider the number of observable mutations $m$, i.e. all base positions $1 \leq i \leq |s|$ where $s(i) \neq s'(i)$. We also assume that these mutations are uniformly distributed over $s'$.
    Let $X$ be a random variable for the number of differing $k$-mers between $s$ and $s'$ depending on the $m$ mutations. For simplicity we assume that there are no duplicate $k$-mers in either $s$ or $s'$ so that $X$ is also the number of differing \emph{unique} $k$-mers. As our last simplification, we consider observations of $k$-mers within $s$ and analogously within $s'$ to be mutually independent. 

    A mutation occurs at a chance of $m/|s|$. So the probability for a changed $k$-mer, which must contain at least one mutation, is $p(m) = 1 - (1 - m/|s|)^k$. Since the number of $k$-mers in $s$ and $s'$ is $|s|-k+1$, $X$ follows the binomial distribution $X \sim B(|s|-k+1, p(m))$.         
    In our context, \emph{we measure the number $E$ of unique $k$-mers differing between $s$ and $s'$ but we want to estimate the distance $d:=m/|s|$ as a ratio of mutations over the sequence $s$}. We therefore approximate $E \approx E[X] = (|s|-k+1) \cdot p(m)$ and solve by $d$:
 \begin{equation}
d \approx 1 - \sqrt[k]{1 - E / (|s| - k + 1)} \approx 1 - \sqrt[k]{1 - E / |s|} =: d(E/|s|)\label{distanceapprox} 	
 \end{equation} 
Figure \ref{nfunc} shows a plot of the function $d(E/|s|)$: It starts with a slope of $1/k$ because each mutation affects about $k$ $k$-mers (those spanning the mutated position), so $E \approx k \cdot m$ for small $m$, giving $d = m/|s| \approx (E/|s|)/k$. The estimate $d(E/|s|)$ becomes very sensitive, i.e. less reliable, when $E$ reaches $|s|$. For practical considerations, we therefore indicate it in taxonomy trees, when $d$ exceeds a configurable threshold $t_d$.

\subsection{Embedding of $d$ in taxonomy trees}\label{embedding}

We want to apply $d$ to a node $n$ in the context of a refined taxonomy tree. More precisely, we estimate $d_n$ to be the maximum evolutionary distance \emph{from $n$'s parent to the data taxa\footnote{A \emph{data taxon} is a node that carries sequence data of its own; \cite{genestrip-ft} defines the term and the intrinsic quality measures that go with it.} under $n$}:
Both counts are taken per genome, in the terms of Section \ref{pairwise}, and not as the totals a node
holds. A node high in the tree holds the $k$-mers of everything beneath it, most of which belong to
genomes other than the one whose distance is being estimated; summing those totals would measure the
clade where the intent is to measure a lineage. What is summed instead is $\kappa_g(n)$, the share of
a node that belongs to the genome $g$ at the end of the path in question, which
Section \ref{pairwise} estimates with a sketch.
\begin{itemize}
\item
For that, $above_g(n)$ shall sum $\kappa_g(n')$ over the nodes $n'$ above $n$ towards the tree's root excluding $n$.
\item
Regarding $below_g(n)$, we consider each path starting from $n$ to any leaf node under $n$ including $n$ itself, let $g$ be the genome at that leaf, and sum $\kappa_g(n')$ over the nodes $n'$ along the path. $below(n)$ is the maximum such sum and $p_n$ denotes some path attaining it, with $g_n$ the genome at its leaf.
\end{itemize}
We set $d_n := d(E_n/|s_n|)$ by letting $E_n := below(n)$ and $|s_n| := \kappa_{g_n}$, the whole of
that genome as Equation \eqref{genomesize} counts it --- which is $above_{g_n}(n) + below(n)$, the
two parts of its lineage, so the shape of the estimate is unchanged and only what is counted at each
node has altered. This agrees with the reading Section \ref{pairwise} takes, and not by coincidence.
The comparison here is between the sequence at $n$'s parent and the genome $g_n$ at the leaf: what
the two have in common is what $g_n$ holds above $n$, what separates them is what it holds at $n$ and
below, and the union of the two is those parts together --- which is $\kappa_{g_n}$. So $E_n$ is
again the union less the intersection and $|s_n|$ again the union, the same two quantities
Equation \eqref{differing} and Equation \eqref{pairdist} use for a pair of genomes.
This way, $d_n$ measures the evolutionary distance from $n$'s parent to the leaf of $p_n$. 
Since we use the maximum sum in $below(n)$, this estimate is conservative for $n$.
However, to depict evolutionary distances in a given taxonomy tree we must represent the portion $dp_n$ of $d_n$ relating to the branch from a node $n$'s parent to $n$ itself.
Let $c$ be the child of $n$ on the path $p_n$. It can be shown that $d_n \geq d_c$,\footnote{$E_n = below(n) = \kappa_{g_n}(n) + below(c) = \kappa_{g_n}(n) + E_c \geq E_c$ (where $\kappa_{g_n}(n) \geq 0$ is $g_n$'s share of the $k$-mers stored at $n$ itself), while $|s_n| = above_{g_n}(n) + below(n) = above_{g_n}(n) + \kappa_{g_n}(n) + E_c = above_{g_n}(c) + below(c) = |s_c|$, so $E_n/|s_n| \geq E_c/|s_c|$, and therefore $d_n \geq d_c$ by monotonicity of $d$.} so we simply set $dp_n := d_n - d_{c}\geq 0$.

\section{Relatedness between two genomes}\label{pairwise}

The $d$ of Equation \eqref{distanceapprox} has so far been read at a node of a tree. The same database also answers the
question one genome at a time: given two genomes $g$ and $h$ that the database holds, how far apart
are they? What the estimate needs is the number of $k$-mers the two have in common, and a Genestrip
database very nearly reports it already.

\subsection{Where two genomes can share a $k$-mer}

Write $\mathrm{path}(g)$ for the nodes from the node carrying $g$ up to the root, $g$'s own node
among them, and $g \wedge h$ for the lowest node the two genomes have in common --- the node at which
they meet. Let $K_g$ be the set of distinct $k$-mers of $g$ that the database holds and $K_g(n)$
those of them it holds at the node $n$, so that $K_g$ is the disjoint union of the $K_g(n)$ over
$\mathrm{path}(g)$.

The lowest common ancestor update confines the sharing to one lineage, and this is what makes the
count tractable. A $k$-mer carried by both $g$ and $h$ has a carrier set containing both, so its
lowest common ancestor lies at or above $g \wedge h$: the database holds it on
$\mathrm{path}(g \wedge h)$ and nowhere else. Below the meeting node the two genomes cannot share
anything, however much sequence they each keep there. So
%
\begin{equation}
  \lvert K_g \cap K_h \rvert \;=\; \sum_{n \,\in\, \mathrm{path}(g \wedge h)}
    \lvert K_g(n) \cap K_h(n) \rvert ,
  \label{sharedsplit}
\end{equation}
%
and the sum runs over a dozen nodes rather than over the tree.

\subsection{The meeting node is counted, the rest estimated}

The first term of Equation \eqref{sharedsplit} needs no estimate. Genestrip's $k$-mer index goal
already builds a Bloom filter per node and is run at every level, and at $n = g \wedge h$ it yields
$\lvert K_g(n) \cap K_h(n) \rvert$ directly: the two genomes meet there, so the $k$-mers they hold in
common at that node are exactly what the index reports for the pair. Write $I(g,h)$ for that count.

Above the meeting node the index does not answer the question --- the pair does not meet there, and
what the node holds is the property of a larger set of genomes --- so the overlap is estimated
instead, from two counts the database supplies. Let $u_n$ be the number of distinct $k$-mers the
database holds at $n$, which is counted exactly when the database is built, and let $\kappa_g(n) =
\lvert K_g(n) \rvert$ be how many of them belong to $g$. Deduplicating a genome's $k$-mers per node
is the one thing not already counted; a HyperLogLog sketch \cite{flajolet2007hyperloglog} estimates
it in constant space, one sketch per node of $\mathrm{path}(g)$ while that genome is walked and
nothing kept but the estimate.

Read as probabilities, $\kappa_g(n)/u_n$ is the chance that a $k$-mer drawn from what the database
holds at $n$ belongs to $g$, and $\kappa_h(n)/u_n$ the same for $h$. Taking the two as independent,
the expected number of the $u_n$ that belong to both is
%
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\lvert K_g(n) \cap K_h(n) \rvert} \;=\;
    u_n \cdot \frac{\kappa_g(n)}{u_n} \cdot \frac{\kappa_h(n)}{u_n}
  \;=\; \frac{\kappa_g(n)\,\kappa_h(n)}{u_n} ,
  \label{jointest}
\end{equation}
%
which is the whole of the estimate and costs two lookups per node. Putting the counted and the
estimated parts together,
%
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{J}(g,h) \;=\; I(g,h) \;+\!\!
    \sum_{\substack{n \,\in\, \mathrm{path}(g \wedge h) \\ n \,\neq\, g \wedge h}}
    \frac{\kappa_g(n)\,\kappa_h(n)}{u_n} .
  \label{jointtotal}
\end{equation}

What the independence in Equation \eqref{jointest} assumes, and how it fails, is worth stating.
It treats $g$ and $h$ as two genomes drawn at random from those beneath $n$, whereas they are not:
they meet below $n$ and are therefore closer to each other than a random pair from that clade is.
Relatives share more than chance allows, so Equation \eqref{jointest} understates the overlap and
Equation \eqref{jointtotal} is a lower estimate of what the two genomes share. The bias is smallest
where the term matters least. A node far above the meeting point holds $k$-mers conserved across a
wide clade, where carriage has little to do with which pair is asked about; a node just above it
holds the $k$-mers most sensitive to the pair's own relatedness, and there the term is one of the
largest. That the two coincide is what makes the count extensible, as
Section \ref{extendcount} sets out: the term worth paying to count is the term worth not estimating.

\subsection{Counting further up where it pays}\label{extendcount}

Nothing confines the counted term to the meeting node. The $k$-mer index is built at every level, so
the intersection $\lvert K_g(n) \cap K_h(n) \rvert$ can be had exactly at any node of
$\mathrm{path}(g \wedge h)$ that is worth the query, and each node so treated drops a term from the
estimated part of Equation \eqref{jointtotal} --- removing both its sampling error and the
independence bias it carries. Writing $C \subseteq \mathrm{path}(g \wedge h)$ for the nodes counted
rather than estimated, with $g \wedge h \in C$ always,
%
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{J}_C(g,h) \;=\; \sum_{n \,\in\, C} \lvert K_g(n) \cap K_h(n) \rvert
    \;+\!\! \sum_{n \,\in\, \mathrm{path}(g \wedge h) \setminus C} \!\!
    \frac{\kappa_g(n)\,\kappa_h(n)}{u_n} ,
  \label{jointadaptive}
\end{equation}
%
of which Equation \eqref{jointtotal} is the case $C = \{g \wedge h\}$ and an exhaustive count the
case $C = \mathrm{path}(g \wedge h)$. What follows is written $\widehat{J}(g,h)$ throughout and
means $\widehat{J}_C$ for whichever $C$ a build settles on; nothing below depends on the choice
except the accuracy of the result.

How far up to go is decided rather than guessed, because the estimate says where it is weak before
any of it is paid for. The estimated terms cost two lookups each, so the whole of
Equation \eqref{jointtotal} can be formed first and the counted share
%
\begin{equation}
  \pi_C(g,h) \;=\; \frac{1}{\widehat{J}_C(g,h)} \sum_{n \,\in\, C}
    \lvert K_g(n) \cap K_h(n) \rvert
  \label{countedshare}
\end{equation}
%
read off it. A pair whose sharing is almost all at the meeting node is finished at $C = \{g \wedge
h\}$; a pair whose $\pi$ falls short of a target has its largest remaining term identified by the
same arithmetic, and $C$ grows by that node. Taking the terms in decreasing size is greedy and, for
the purpose of reducing the estimated mass, optimal: each query removes exactly the term it is aimed
at.

Two things make the order agree with the tree. The largest estimated terms sit just above the
meeting node, since that is where two related genomes share most of what they do not share at the
node itself --- and it is also where the independence assumption is at its worst, for the reason
given above. So counting upward from the meeting node spends the queries where both the variance and
the bias are largest, and stops when what is left is the conserved sharing high in the tree, which is
small, nearly independent of the pair, and cheap to be wrong about. The scheme is therefore an
accuracy dial with a known price, not an approximation whose quality is discovered afterwards.

The same dial governs the cost of a whole matrix, which is where it is likely to bind. An
estimated term is two lookups and a division; a counted one is a query against the $k$-mer index,
and over $\binom{\lvert L \rvert}{2}$ pairs the difference between the two decides whether a
distance matrix over a database is minutes of work or days of it. The extremes bracket it. With
$C = \mathrm{path}(g \wedge h)$ every term is counted and the matrix costs as many index queries as
there are pairs times the depth of the tree. With $C = \emptyset$ --- dropping even the meeting
node, which Equation \eqref{jointadaptive} permits although Equation \eqref{jointtotal} does not ---
nothing is queried at all and the whole matrix is $O(\lvert L \rvert^2 \cdot \text{depth})$
arithmetic over arrays already in memory, at the accuracy the independence assumption allows.
Between them lies a policy, and it need not be uniform: a pair reported to a reader deserves more
counting than a pair that only contributes a row to the least-squares fit of
Section \ref{treefit}, where hundreds of other rows constrain the same branches.

\todo{Establish the cost of one counted term before choosing a policy, since it sets the scale of
everything else. If obtaining $\lvert K_g(n) \cap K_h(n) \rvert$ costs anything proportional to a
genome's $k$-mers rather than to what the pair holds at that one node, the counted route does not
survive past a few hundred genomes and the estimated route is the only one that scales --- which
would make the independence bias the thing to measure and correct rather than to count around.}

\todo{Measure the profile of $\pi_C$ before fixing a policy. Two things decide it and both are
database properties: how the estimated terms decay going up the path, and how far up the pairs of
interest meet. If $\pi_{\{g \wedge h\}}$ is already above nine tenths for pairs meeting at a species,
the adaptive part is never invoked for the pairs that matter and can be left out; if it is not, the
number of extra nodes needed to reach a target is what sets the cost of the whole matrix.}

\subsection{The union, and a Jaccard index over whole genomes}

The same reading gives what the two genomes hold between them. At a node of the shared lineage the
union of the two sets is $\kappa_g(n) + \kappa_h(n)$ less their overlap --- counted where that node
is in $C$ and estimated by Equation \eqref{jointest} where it is not, the union caring only that the
overlap is subtracted and not how it was arrived at. Below the meeting point the two are separate and no overlap
arises to be estimated: such a node lies under one of the genomes and not the other, so what it
contributes to the union is that genome's share of it alone, $\kappa_g(n)$ or $\kappa_h(n)$ as the
case may be. There are thus two kinds of node to sum over --- those on the shared lineage, where an
overlap is subtracted however it was obtained, and those below the meeting point, where one genome
contributes alone. Summing both kinds
along $\mathrm{path}(g) \cup \mathrm{path}(h)$ and writing
%
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_g \;=\; \sum_{n \,\in\, \mathrm{path}(g)} \kappa_g(n)
  \label{genomesize}
\end{equation}
%
for the size of one genome in the database, the estimated union telescopes to
%
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{U}(g,h) \;=\; \kappa_g + \kappa_h - \widehat{J}(g,h) ,
  \label{unionest}
\end{equation}
%
which is inclusion and exclusion recovered rather than assumed: estimating the union node by node
and estimating the intersection node by node give the same answer, because each node's two estimates
are built from the same two counters. Nothing further has to be sketched for it.

The Jaccard index over the two genomes follows,
%
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\jmath}(g,h) \;=\; \frac{\widehat{J}(g,h)}{\widehat{U}(g,h)}
  \;=\; \frac{\widehat{J}(g,h)}{\kappa_g + \kappa_h - \widehat{J}(g,h)} ,
  \label{jaccard}
\end{equation}
%
and it is an index over the \emph{whole} of both genomes and not over a sample of either. That holds
on one condition, which is the condition of the section: both genomes are in the database. The entry
phase stores every $k$-mer of a genome it enters, and the update leaves each of them somewhere on
that genome's own lineage, so Equation \eqref{genomesize} counts the genome entire and
Equation \eqref{jaccard} compares two complete $k$-mer sets. For a genome the database never entered
the same arithmetic returns something narrower --- its shared part against another genome's whole ---
and should not be read as a Jaccard index between organisms.

\subsection{From shared $k$-mers to a distance}

Nothing further is needed: Equation \eqref{distanceapprox} takes a count of differing unique
$k$-mers over a sequence length, and both are now to hand. The $k$-mers that differ are those the
union holds and the intersection does not,
%
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{E}(g,h) \;=\; \widehat{U}(g,h) - \widehat{J}(g,h)
  \;=\; \kappa_g + \kappa_h - 2\,\widehat{J}(g,h) ,
  \label{differing}
\end{equation}
%
which counts a substitution through the $k$-mers it destroys on either side and counts an extension
of one genome over the other through the $k$-mers that have no counterpart at all. The sequence the
two span between them is the union, $\widehat{U}(g,h)$ of Equation \eqref{unionest}, and taking that
for $|s|$ gives
%
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{d}(g,h) \;=\; 1 - \sqrt[k]{1 - \frac{\widehat{E}(g,h)}{\widehat{U}(g,h)}}
  \;=\; 1 - \sqrt[k]{\widehat{\jmath}(g,h)} ,
  \label{pairdist}
\end{equation}
%
since $1 - \widehat{E}/\widehat{U}$ is the Jaccard index of Equation \eqref{jaccard}. The estimate is
therefore a $k$-th root of the Jaccard index and nothing more, and it behaves at the ends as it
should: identical genomes give $\widehat{\jmath} = 1$ and $\widehat{d} = 0$, genomes sharing nothing
give $\widehat{d} = 1$.

Taking the union rather than a genome for $|s|$ is a decision about what is being measured, and it
should be read as one. The derivation behind Equation \eqref{distanceapprox} has two sequences of
equal length differing only by substitution, and there $|s|$ is one sequence's length: a
substitution destroys $k$ $k$-mers on each side, so Equation \eqref{differing} counts it twice,
and dividing by $\kappa_g + \kappa_h$ rather than by the union would halve it back. Doing so returns
the per-base substitution rate exactly, and Equation \eqref{pairdist} returns about twice it --- at
$k = 31$ a pair whose true rate is $0.01$ reads $0.018$, and one at $0.001$ reads $0.002$.

The reason for preferring the union is that real genomes do not differ only by substitution. One
carries genes the other has not, and that material is divergence as surely as a changed base is:
under the equal-length reading it has no place to go, since the genomes are not of equal length and
neither is the sequence the pair descends from. The union is that sequence --- everything either
genome holds --- and measuring the difference against it counts gain and loss alongside
substitution. What is bought with the factor of two is a distance that does not depend on which of
the two genomes is called $s$.

One limit of the transformation survives the choice and is worth stating, since neither denominator
repairs it. The $k$-th root in Equation \eqref{distanceapprox} assumes that a difference costs $k$
$k$-mers, which a substitution in the middle of a sequence does. An insertion of length $L$ does
not: it yields about $L + k - 1$ new $k$-mers rather than $kL$, so the transformation reads it as
though it were some $L/k$ substitutions and understates it accordingly. Equation \eqref{pairdist} is
therefore reliable for pairs that differ mostly by substitution, and conservative for pairs that
differ in gene content --- which is the direction to prefer for an estimate that is read as a lower
bound on relatedness rather than as an upper bound on distance.

Written in the Jaccard index, Equation \eqref{pairdist} sits beside the estimator of
\cite{Ondov:2016aa}, which reports $-\frac{1}{k}\ln\bigl(2\jmath/(1+\jmath)\bigr)$ and so takes the
equal-length reading. The difference is in what produces $\jmath$: a sketching tool computes it from
a sample of each genome taken independently of any tree, where here it comes from counters a
database already keeps, over the whole of both genomes, and the lineage structure of the update is
what reduces the sum to one path.

\subsection{The estimate does not depend on the taxonomy}\label{taxindep}

Everything so far has been read off a tree, which invites the objection that the answer must inherit
the tree's mistakes. A reference taxonomy does contain mistakes: a genome deposited under the wrong
species, a species under the wrong genus, a name that predates the sequence that contradicts it. It
is worth showing that the estimate is not at their mercy, and the argument is short.

What Equation \eqref{pairdist} is built from are $\lvert K_g \cap K_h \rvert$, $\lvert K_g \cup K_h
\rvert$ and the two $\kappa$. None of the first three mentions a node: they are properties of two
sets of $k$-mers, hence of two sequences, and a taxonomy has no vote on them. The tree enters only in
\emph{how they are arrived at}. So the target of the estimate is invariant under any reshaping of the
taxonomy whatever, and what remains to be checked is the arithmetic that reaches it.

That arithmetic survives too, in three steps. The size $\kappa_g$ of Equation \eqref{genomesize} is
placement-independent: the entry phase stores every $k$-mer of a genome it enters and the update
leaves each of them somewhere on that genome's own lineage, so wherever $g$ is attached,
$\mathrm{path}(g)$ collects all of it and $\kappa_g = \lvert K_g \rvert$. Equation
\eqref{sharedsplit} is placement-independent for the same reason applied to a pair: a $k$-mer carried
by both has its lowest common ancestor at or above $g \wedge h$ wherever that node happens to be, so
the sum still finds every shared $k$-mer and still misses none. And the counted term is exact at
whatever node it lands on, an intersection being an intersection.

Only the \emph{split} between the counted and the estimated terms moves, and for the case that
matters it moves the right way. Take a genome $g$ filed far from its true closest relative $h$. Their
meeting node $g \wedge h$ is then high in the tree --- and the $k$-mers the two share, having no other
carriers, have their lowest common ancestor at exactly that node. The misplacement therefore gathers
the pair's shared sequence \emph{into} the one term that is counted rather than estimated, and it
leaves a shorter path above that node to be estimated at all. What stays below $g \wedge h$ is what
$g$ and $h$ do not share, which is what the union needs and which no step of the estimate has to
guess at. A worked case: on a tree of six genomes in two clades, moving one genome into the wrong
clade changed the counted share of its nearest relative from $0.714$ to $1.000$ and left
$\widehat{d}$ unaltered in the fifth decimal.

The converse misplacement is the one to state honestly. Two genomes wrongly made neighbours meet
low, so the whole of what they share --- conserved sequence, since they are in truth distant --- falls
into estimated terms above them, and the independence assumption of Equation \eqref{jointest} bears
the full weight. In the same worked case the counted share of such a pair fell from $1.000$ to $0.000$
while $\widehat{d}$ again did not move, because what is being estimated there is carriage of $k$-mers
conserved across a wide clade, where the assumption is at its most nearly true. The rule of thumb is
that misplacement costs accuracy where the pair is distant and buys it where the pair is close, which
is the opposite of the trade one would fear. Section \ref{placing} turns this from a defence into a
method: a genome whose position is not known at all can be entered at a guess and placed afterwards on
what the $k$-mers say.

\subsection{Incomplete genomes}\label{incomplete}

Everything above assumed that a genome in the database is a whole genome. Most of RefSeq is not:
assemblies come in contigs, with sequence missing in the gaps between them, and a method that
required closed genomes would be left with a small and unrepresentative collection. Two questions
have to be separated here, and only one of them is a problem.

\paragraph{Fragmentation costs almost nothing.} Nothing in Section \ref{pairwise} refers to a contig,
a coordinate or an order. What the estimate consumes are the sets $K_g$ and $K_h$, and cutting a
sequence into pieces removes only the $k$-mers that spanned the cuts: $k-1$ per break. A 5\,Mbp
genome in five hundred contigs retains $4{,}985{,}000$ of its $4{,}999{,}970$ $k$-mers, so read
against a closed copy of itself it returns $\widehat{d} = 0.0001$, and even two thousand contigs
return $0.0004$. Both are below the distances that separate strains of one species, so a draft
assembly is as good as a closed genome for this purpose as long as the sequence is there. Contiguity
is not what matters.

\paragraph{Missing sequence costs a great deal.} Completeness is what matters, and the union
denominator is what makes it expensive. Let $c$ be the fraction of $g$'s $k$-mers the assembly
actually contains. Then the observed shared count is $c$ times the true one while $h$'s side of the
union is untouched, so $\widehat{\jmath}$ falls and $\widehat{d}$ rises although nothing has diverged.
At $k = 31$ a genome $5\,\%$ short of complete reads $\widehat{d} = 0.0017$ against an identical
complete copy of itself, one $20\,\%$ short reads $0.0072$, and one half complete reads $0.0221$.
Since strain-level divergence within a species is itself of the order of $0.001$ to $0.01$, even
modest incompleteness manufactures a difference as large as the differences being measured. It cannot
be left undetected.

\paragraph{What can be known about $c$.} It is worth being exact about this, because the obvious
answer does not survive contact with the data a build actually has, and the answer that does work is
not the obvious one.

NCBI states assembly level per assembly, in
\texttt{assembly\_summary\_refseq.txt}, and there the question is settled in one column: $73{,}778$ of
$501{,}300$ RefSeq assemblies are marked \enquote{Complete Genome}. A Genestrip database is not built
from that file. It is built from the bulk division FASTA and the release catalog
\texttt{RefSeq-release\emph{NNN}.catalog.gz}, which is keyed by \emph{sequence} accession and carries
no assembly accession. Accession prefixes suggest themselves as the way across and do not survive: the
two-letter \texttt{NZ\_} prefixes mix finished replicons (\texttt{CP}, \texttt{AP},
\texttt{CM}) with whole-genome shotgun scaffolds (\texttt{JH}, \texttt{KB}, \texttt{KE}) at the same
accession shape, and separating them means a hand-kept list of prefixes that INSDC extends over time.

The join that works uses neither accession. What the two files share is a taxon and a number of bases:
an assembly's recorded length is the sum of the lengths of the sequences it consists of, and the
catalog carries the taxon and the length of every sequence. The catalog is ordered by taxon and, within
a taxon, by accession, so the sequences of one assembly arrive as a run of consecutive accessions.
Summing such a run and looking the total up in the summary by taxon and length locates the assembly,
and with it the column that settles the question. The sum need not be exact --- a submitted total and
the sum of the released sequences differ by a few bases often enough that an exact match loses
assemblies --- so a tolerance is allowed, $100$ bases by default, and the nearest assembly within it is
taken. An assembly already accounted for is not taken again, which matters because a genome's
chromosome alone can match a different assembly of the same species more closely than the whole genome
matches its own; without that guard one genome is counted several times. Over a whole release this
identifies $92.5\,\%$ of the assemblies whose taxon the database reads, in one streaming pass of the
catalog holding eight records at a time.

For an identified assembly $c$ is then read rather than inferred, and in the absolute sense. A
complete assembly has $c = 1$: complete means every replicon closed, and the summary bears it out, in
that not one of the release's $73{,}778$ complete assemblies has a \texttt{genome\_size} differing from
its \texttt{genome\_size\_ungapped}. A chromosome-level assembly does not have $c = 1$, but its
shortfall is recorded rather than guessed, as the ratio of those same two columns; nine in ten carry
gaps, averaging about one per cent of the genome. Scaffold and contig assemblies yield nothing this
way, and the reason is worth stating because it looks like the opposite: their two lengths agree
exactly as a complete assembly's do, since what they are missing is not represented at all.

Two residuals bound what this costs. The tolerance admits a sum up to $100$ bases from the recorded
length, which on a five-megabase genome is $2 \times 10^{-5}$ --- two orders below the one per cent
that chromosome-level gaps contribute, and far below the distances being measured. And $c$ as read
counts bases where Equation \eqref{completecorr} needs $k$-mers: a gap of length $L$ destroys $L + k -
1$ $k$-mer positions rather than $L$, so with $G$ gaps the $k$-mer completeness falls short of the base
completeness by $G(k-1)/s$. At $k = 31$ and a few dozen gaps that is a few thousand bases against a gap
total in the tens of thousands, which moves $c$ in the third decimal.

What this does not reach still has to be had relatively. An assembly the join fails to identify, a
genome from a division the database does not read, and any FASTA a project supplies itself all arrive
without a row to look up. For those, and as an independent check on the metadata where both are
available, what is wanted is $c$ \emph{relative to a genome's
neighbours}. Two estimators give it and they share no inputs.

The first is size. $\kappa_g$ against the median $\kappa$ of $g$'s closest relatives says how much
smaller this genome is than the company it keeps. Its floor is that relatives are not of a size:
among closed genomes of one species, where incompleteness cannot be the explanation, the middle
ninety per cent of \texttt{genome\_size} spans $11.5\,\%$ of the median for a typical species and
$20\,\%$ for \emph{E.~coli}. Deficits of tens of per cent clear that; the $1.5\,\%$ by which a typical
RefSeq draft falls short of its species' closed genomes does not, and does not need to.

The second is the fit of Section \ref{treefit}, and it needs no sizes and no metadata whatever. A
genome's pendant edge carries its own inflation, so the excess of that edge over the median pendant of
its neighbours measures the same deficiency independently. Inverting the excess through the relation
above recovers the completeness of a short genome to within about one point in ten across the range
$c \in [0.5, 0.95]$ --- $0.953$ recovered for a true $0.95$, $0.711$ for $0.70$, $0.512$ for $0.50$ ---
which is well inside the ten per cent the correction of Equation \eqref{completecorr} requires, and it
is immune to the size variation that limits the first estimator.

Both are relative, and they fail in the same way for the same reason. Each measures a genome against
its neighbours, so where a whole clade is equally short there is nothing to see: with an eighth of a
clade at $c = 0.7$ the pendant estimator returns $0.710$, with a quarter $0.728$, with a half $0.766$,
and where every genome is equally short it returns $0.99$ and reports a collection of complete
assemblies.

That last failure is the one to be least worried about, for a reason the rest of this section works
out: a deficit shared by a whole clade adds nearly the same constant to every distance, which the fit
absorbs into the pendant edges and which leaves the internal branches, the topology, and every
relative statement the tree makes intact. A uniformly deficient collection yields a correct tree drawn
with long tips. What genuinely damages an estimate is \emph{differential} incompleteness, one genome
much shorter than its neighbours --- and that is precisely what both estimators are built to see. The
quantity that cannot be measured is the one that does least harm.

Why the fitted tree carries that information is worth setting out in full, because it is not obvious
and because everything below rests on it. Call the \emph{pendant edge} of a leaf the single edge
joining it to the rest of the tree --- $g$'s own branch, the last step on any journey to it. Two facts about it follow from
Equation \eqref{additive}. Every path from $g$ to another leaf begins by walking down that edge, so it
appears in every equation of the system that involves $g$. And no path between two \emph{other} leaves
passes through it, so it appears in no other equation at all.

Four genomes are enough to see it. On the tree of Figure \ref{pendantfig}, with $b_g$, $b_h$, $b_i$,
$b_j$ the four pendant edges and $m$ the internal one, Equation \eqref{additive} gives the six
distances as
%
\[
  \begin{array}{r@{\;=\;}l@{\qquad}l}
    d(g,h) & \textcolor{red!70!black}{b_g} + b_h            & \\
    d(g,i) & \textcolor{red!70!black}{b_g} + m + b_i        & \\
    d(g,j) & \textcolor{red!70!black}{b_g} + m + b_j        & \\
    d(h,i) & b_h + m + b_i                                  & \\
    d(h,j) & b_h + m + b_j                                  & \\
    d(i,j) & b_i + b_j                                      &
  \end{array}
\]
%
so the column of $A$ in Equation \eqref{designmatrix} belonging to $b_g$ is $(1,1,1,0,0,0)$: a one for
each of the three pairs that contain $g$, a zero for each of the three that do not.

Now compare that with what incompleteness does. It inflates the distance from $g$ to every partner by
nearly the same amount $\delta$, and it inflates nothing else, so the error it introduces is
$(\delta,\delta,\delta,0,0,0)$ --- the same shape as that column. The two being proportional is the
whole of the matter: least squares assigns the part of an error lying along a column of $A$ to that
column's coefficient, and here the entire error lies along one column. The fit therefore lengthens
$b_g$ by $\delta$, leaves $b_h$, $b_i$, $b_j$ and $m$ where they were, and is left with no residual.
The three distances that were wrong are explained, and they are explained by the one parameter that
touches them and nothing else.

This is exact when the inflation is exactly constant and close to it otherwise. Solving the six
equations above with $b_g = 0.004$, $b_h = 0.005$, $m = 0.008$, $b_i = 0.010$ and $b_j = 0.007$, and
then adding a constant $0.0072$ to the three distances from $g$, returns $b_g = 0.0112$ --- the true
value plus the whole of the inflation --- and returns the other four branches unchanged to seventeen
decimal places. Letting the inflation vary by a fifth across the three partners, as the figures above
say it does, puts $0.0062$ of it into $b_g$ and disturbs no other branch by more than
$10^{-4}$, against branch lengths of $0.004$ to $0.010$. So the fit does two things at once: it \emph{shows} incompleteness, as a terminal
branch far longer than its neighbours', and it \emph{contains} it, keeping the distortion off the
internal branches where the topology's evidence lies.

Which tree the pendant edge belongs to is worth answering, since the question decides how much the
signal can be trusted. The argument above used nothing about the topology except that $g$ is a leaf of
it, so it holds for the taxonomy and for a clustered tree alike: in either, $g$'s own branch is the
one edge that every path from $g$ uses and no path between other leaves touches. The two differ in
what could go wrong rather than in whether the mechanism works.

On the taxonomy the answer is immediate. The topology is given, and given by something that never
saw the distances, so an inflated row cannot move $g$ within it. There is nothing for the
incompleteness to corrupt except the length of the branch it is then found on, which is the whole
point.

On a clustered tree the topology is inferred from the very distances the incompleteness has inflated,
and the worry is that $g$ might be clustered somewhere it does not belong --- pushed outward, say,
because everything looks far from it. It is not, and the reason is worth having. Adding a constant
$\delta$ to every distance from $g$ leaves the matrix \emph{exactly additive on the same topology},
with $b_g$ replaced by $b_g + \delta$ and every other branch unchanged; that is the same observation
as before, read as a statement about the data rather than about the fit. A method that recovers the
correct tree from additive distances therefore recovers this one. On the five-leaf tree used above,
neighbour joining returned the true topology under a constant inflation of $0.0072$, of $0.05$, and of
an absurd $0.5$, and under inflations varying by a fifth and by a half across partners. What can
distort a clustered topology is the part of the inflation that is not constant, and it is small.

\paragraph{Many incomplete genomes.} A real collection does not have one short assembly but
hundreds, and the argument above has to be made again for that case, because it changes shape. The
perturbation splits in two and only the first part is absorbed.

The first part is. If every distance from leaf $x$ is inflated by its own constant $\delta_x$, so
that $d'(g,h) = d(g,h) + \delta_g + \delta_h$, the matrix is \emph{still exactly additive on the same
topology}: every pendant edge $b_x$ becomes $b_x + \delta_x$ and no internal branch moves. That holds
for all leaves at once, however many of them are short. So incompleteness spread across a whole
collection does not accumulate into a distortion of the tree; it accumulates into the tips.

The second part is not, and this is what the first-order argument hides. The inflation is not quite
$\delta_g + \delta_h$. Where both genomes are short the shared count falls by $c_g c_h$ while each
size falls by its own factor, and the observed inflation comes out a little \emph{below} the sum: at
$c = 0.9$ for both it is $0.0065$ against a sum of $0.0068$, at $c = 0.8$ it is $0.0130$ against
$0.0143$, and at $c = 0.5$ it is $0.0348$ against $0.0442$. Write $\varepsilon(g,h)$ for that
shortfall. With one short genome $\varepsilon$ appears in a single row and is swept up with the rest
of that row. With many, it appears in \emph{every cell whose two genomes are both short}, which in a
real collection is most of the matrix --- and a perturbation spread over the whole matrix is not
something any set of pendant edges can absorb. It is what limits the resolution.

How much it limits is a question of scale, since what $\varepsilon$ has to compete with is the length
of the internal branch a split is being decided by. On random twelve-leaf trees, comparing the
topology recovered from the inflated matrix against the true one:

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}l|c|c|c@{}}
 \textbf{internal branches} & \textbf{$c \in [0.9,1]$} & \textbf{$c \in [0.8,1]$} & \textbf{$c \in [0.5,1]$}\\
 \hline
 $0.001$ to $0.02$ \quad (species level and above) & $100\,\%$ & $100\,\%$ & $97\,\%$\\
 $0.0002$ to $0.002$ \quad (strain level)          & $100\,\%$ & $98\,\%$  & $28\,\%$\\
 $0.00005$ to $0.0005$ \quad (below strain level)  & $99\,\%$  & $66\,\%$  & $1\,\%$\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Those figures are for a topology \emph{inferred} from the inflated matrix, which is the hardest case
and not the one this paper recommends. Fitting a given topology changes the picture, and
Section \ref{topologychoice} is the reason it is available: the taxonomy supplies a tree that the
distances did not, so where it is taken as given there is no topology left for $\varepsilon$ to
scramble. Every part of the perturbation must then appear in the branch lengths, and the part due to
incompleteness has only one place to go.

That is worth having for two reasons rather than one. The first is that the failure above simply does
not arise: a fixed topology cannot be rearranged by a perturbed matrix, so the resolution limit
becomes a limit on how far branch \emph{lengths} can be trusted rather than on which splits exist.
Fitting the same twelve-leaf trees to their own topology, with branches between $0.0002$ and $0.002$,
the internal branches came out with a mean error of $0.00027$ where genomes ran from $80$ to
$100\,\%$ complete and $0.00044$ where they ran from $50$ to $100\,\%$, against $0.00019$ for a
collection of complete genomes --- so incompleteness roughly doubles an error that the
Jaccard-to-distance transformation contributes anyway, and leaves it below the branch lengths being
measured.

The second is that forcing the inflation into the pendant edges makes it conspicuous. With nowhere
else to go it goes there, and it goes there at a size that cannot be mistaken for a branch: on the
same trees the pendant edges were inflated by $0.0032$ on average at $80$ to $100\,\%$ completeness,
by $0.0086$ at $50$ to $100\,\%$, and by $0.017$ where every genome was half missing --- three to
seventeen times the length of a typical branch in those trees. More than that, the inflation
\emph{ranks} correctly: ordering the leaves by how much their pendant edge exceeded expectation
reproduced the ordering by incompleteness in $92$ to $100\,\%$ of adjacent pairs. So the fit does not
merely raise an alarm, it says which assemblies are worst and roughly by how much, which is what
$\kappa_g$ is then checked against.

The obvious objection is that this reads the taxonomy as correct in order to judge the genomes, while
Section \ref{topologychoice} reads the same fit to judge the taxonomy. The two are not in conflict
because they are different parameters. A large \emph{residual} concentrated at an internal node says
the taxonomy groups those genomes wrongly; a long \emph{pendant edge} at a leaf says that one genome's
distances are all inflated, which grouping cannot cause. Where both appear at once the diagnosis is
genuinely ambiguous from the fit alone, and that is the case $\kappa_g$ settles: a short genome
explains the pendant edge, and a genome of normal size does not.

At the resolution this method is meant for --- species and the ranks above them --- the topology is
recovered whole even when half the collection is half missing, and that is with the topology inferred
rather than given. It is fine structure that suffers:
telling two strains apart across a branch of $0.0002$ asks for a precision that assemblies at
$c = 0.5$ cannot support, and the tree returned there is not to be believed. The rule of thumb is to
compare the shortest internal branch one intends to read against the $\varepsilon$ of the genomes
beneath it, and to decline to resolve below it. That is a limit on how finely the answer may be read
and not a reason to distrust the rest of it, which is the same trade every distance method makes and
is stated here in units that can be checked.

The two estimators do more together than either alone, because they disagree in the informative case.
A long pendant edge on its own is ambiguous, since a genuinely divergent organism has one too. Set
beside $\kappa_g$ it is not: a long pendant edge with a $\kappa_g$ in line with its neighbours is real
divergence, and a long pendant edge with a $\kappa_g$ well below theirs is an assembly gap. That they
share no inputs is what makes the pairing worth having.

\paragraph{Correcting it.} If $c$ is estimated the repair is arithmetic, and it is worth writing out
because the two quantities are scaled by different factors. Assume the $k$-mers an assembly lacks are
a random sample of the genome's. Then a $k$-mer of $g$ survives into the assembly with probability
$c_g$, so the observed size is $c_g$ times the true one; and a $k$-mer shared by $g$ and $h$ is
observed as shared only if it survived in both assemblies, which happens with probability
$c_g c_h$. Dividing each observation by the factor that shrank it gives
%
\begin{equation}
  \kappa^{\ast}_g \;=\; \frac{\kappa_g}{c_g} ,
  \qquad
  \widehat{J}^{\ast}(g,h) \;=\; \frac{\widehat{J}(g,h)}{c_g\,c_h} ,
  \qquad
  \widehat{U}^{\ast} \;=\; \kappa^{\ast}_g + \kappa^{\ast}_h - \widehat{J}^{\ast}(g,h) ,
  \label{completecorr}
\end{equation}
%
after which Equation \eqref{pairdist} is read on the starred quantities and nothing else changes.
Note that the shared count carries \emph{both} factors where the sizes carry one each, which is the
whole of why incompleteness does not cancel out of the ratio on its own.

A worked case makes it concrete. Two assemblies of the same organism, whose genome holds five million
distinct $k$-mers, so that the truth is $\widehat{d} = 0$. Let $g$'s assembly be $80\,\%$ complete and
$h$'s complete: the observed counts are $\kappa_g = 4{,}000{,}000$, $\kappa_h = 5{,}000{,}000$ and
$\widehat{J} = 4{,}000{,}000$, giving $\widehat{U} = 5{,}000{,}000$ and $\widehat{d} = 0.0072$ ---
a difference between two copies of one organism. Dividing by $c_g = 0.8$ returns
$\kappa^{\ast}_g = \widehat{J}^{\ast} = 5{,}000{,}000$, hence $\widehat{U}^{\ast} = 5{,}000{,}000$ and
$\widehat{d} = 0$. With $h$ at $60\,\%$ as well, the raw figures are $\kappa_h = 3{,}000{,}000$ and
$\widehat{J} = 2{,}400{,}000$ for $\widehat{d} = 0.0208$, and dividing $\widehat{J}$ by
$c_g c_h = 0.48$ while dividing each size by its own factor again returns $0$.

Where $c$ comes from is Section \ref{incomplete}'s first signal: $\kappa_g$ against the median
$\kappa$ of $g$'s closest relatives, which is the same ratio measured on genomes whose sizes should
agree. In the example above $4{,}000{,}000$ against a relative's $5{,}000{,}000$ is what says
$c_g = 0.8$ in the first place, so the correction consumes nothing that was not already collected.

The assumption is the weak point and it fails in a known direction. Assembly does not lose sequence
at random: repetitive regions and regions of low coverage are what fail to assemble, and repeats are
disproportionately the conserved, widely shared part of a genome. The $k$-mers lost are therefore
enriched for exactly the ones that would have been shared, so $\widehat{J}$ is reduced by more than
the factor $c$ and the correction repairs less than all of the bias. It errs towards reporting
genomes as more distant than they are, which is the safer direction for a measure read as evidence of
relatedness. \cite{skmer} face the same problem from the other end, modelling the incompleteness of a
sketch taken from low-coverage reads, and their treatment is the one to compare against.

\section{From a distance matrix to a tree}\label{treefit}

Section \ref{pairwise} applies to any two genomes the database holds, so running it over all pairs
gives a full distance matrix $\widehat{d}(g,h)$ on the genome set $L$. Turning that matrix into a
tree divides into two questions that are better kept apart: which tree, and how long are its
branches. The first is a combinatorial question and is answered by clustering; the second is a
linear one and is answered by least squares, which is the part this section sets out, since it is
also what replaces the heuristic branch portions $dp_n$ of Section \ref{embedding}.

\subsection{The topology, and why the taxonomy is the interesting one}\label{topologychoice}

A distance matrix can be clustered into a topology --- average linkage after \cite{Sokal1958ASM}
gives an ultrametric tree, neighbour joining \cite{saitou1987nj} an unrooted one that assumes no
molecular clock and is the better default of the two, since nothing in Section \ref{pairwise} makes
rates equal across lineages. Either is available here and the next subsection treats them like any
other topology.

Neither is the reason to do this. A refined Genestrip database already carries a tree over exactly
these genomes, and it is not a tree derived from the distances: it is the NCBI taxonomy
\cite{taxonomy} with the refinements of \cite{genestrip-ft} in it, built from nomenclature and
curation rather than from the measurements of Section \ref{pairwise}. Fitting branch lengths to
\emph{that} topology asks a different question from clustering. Clustering asks what tree the
distances imply; fitting the taxonomy asks how well the taxonomy accounts for them, and because the
system is overdetermined the answer is quantitative and local. A node whose incident pairs are fitted
closely is a node the sequence data supports; one whose residual is large is a node where the
established grouping and the $k$-mer evidence disagree, and the fit says by how much and where.

This is the qualitative claim of Section \ref{nodeprec} made measurable. Node precision reports
where a database's tree is coarser than its data; the residuals of Equation \eqref{lsq} report where
its tree is \emph{wrong} for the data, which is a different defect and one that refinement does not
repair. A build that is clustered instead cannot ask this at all, having thrown the hypothesis away
before testing it.

A third reason has nothing to do with judging the taxonomy and is set out in
Section \ref{incomplete}: a given topology cannot be rearranged by a perturbed distance matrix, so
the distortion that incomplete assemblies introduce is forced into the branch lengths, and the part
of it belonging to any one genome is forced into that genome's own pendant edge, where it is
conspicuous. A clustered tree offers the perturbation somewhere else to go.

The test is only worth anything because the distances are independent of the tree being tested, and
that is what Section \ref{taxindep} establishes. Were $\widehat{d}$ a function of where the taxonomy
puts a genome, fitting the taxonomy to it would be circular --- the measurements would already agree
with the hypothesis by construction, and a small residual would say nothing. They are not: the
distance between two genomes is fixed by their $k$-mer sets, the taxonomy affects only which part of
the shared count is exact and which is estimated, and a genome filed in the wrong clade yields the
same $\widehat{d}$ to its true relative as a correctly filed one. So the residual at a node is
evidence about the taxonomy and not an echo of it, which is the property the whole of this section
rests on.

\subsection{Branch lengths as an overdetermined linear system}

Fix a topology with edge set $\mathcal{E}$ and write $b_e \geq 0$ for the length of edge $e$. On a
tree there is exactly one path between two leaves; let $P(g,h) \subseteq \mathcal{E}$ be its edges.
The additive model --- the one Equation \eqref{distanceapprox} already assumes when it treats
distance as a ratio of accumulated mutations --- says that the distance between two genomes is the
mutation accumulated along the path that separates them:
%
\begin{equation}
  d(g,h) \;=\; \sum_{e \,\in\, P(g,h)} b_e .
  \label{additive}
\end{equation}
%
This is linear in the unknowns. Collecting the $M = \binom{\lvert L \rvert}{2}$ pairs into a vector
$\widehat{\mathbf{d}}$ and writing $A \in \{0,1\}^{M \times \lvert \mathcal{E} \rvert}$ for the
path-incidence matrix,
%
\begin{equation}
  A_{(g,h),\,e} \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    1, & e \in P(g,h) , \\
    0, & \text{otherwise} ,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  A\mathbf{b} \;=\; \widehat{\mathbf{d}} ,
  \label{designmatrix}
\end{equation}
%
and the system is overdetermined as soon as there are four genomes: an unrooted binary tree on $n$
leaves has at most $2n-3$ edges against $n(n-1)/2$ pairs, so ten genomes give $45$ equations in $17$
unknowns and a hundred give $4950$ in $197$. The branch lengths are not read off the distances but
fitted to all of them at once, and it is this redundancy that makes the residuals worth reading.

The solution is the weighted least-squares one, constrained to be non-negative since a branch of
negative length has no meaning:
%
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathbf{b}} \;=\; \argmin_{\mathbf{b} \,\geq\, \mathbf{0}}
    \bigl( A\mathbf{b} - \widehat{\mathbf{d}} \bigr)^{\!\top} W
    \bigl( A\mathbf{b} - \widehat{\mathbf{d}} \bigr) ,
  \label{lsq}
\end{equation}
%
whose unconstrained form solves the normal equations $A^{\!\top} W A \, \mathbf{b} = A^{\!\top} W
\widehat{\mathbf{d}}$. Fitting branch lengths to a fixed topology by least squares is the criterion
of \cite{cavalli1967phylogenetic} and \cite{fitch1967construction}; what this paper adds is where the
distances and the weights come from. The non-negativity turns Equation \eqref{lsq} into a
non-negative least-squares problem, for which the active-set algorithm of \cite{lawson1995solving} is
standard and terminates finitely.

\subsection{Weights}

$W$ is diagonal and its entries are the reciprocal variances of the individual distance estimates,
$W_{(g,h)} = 1 / \sigma^2(g,h)$. This is where Section \ref{pairwise} pays off a second time. The
classical criteria have to guess at these --- \cite{cavalli1967phylogenetic} weights every pair
equally and \cite{fitch1967construction} takes $1/d^2$ as a stand-in for proportional error --- but
here the error is not a guess. Each $\widehat{d}(g,h)$ is assembled from a counted intersection at
the meeting node, sketched shares above it, and exact genome sizes, and the variance of the result
follows from those: pairs whose shared $k$-mers are mostly counted rather than estimated carry more
weight than pairs meeting high in the tree, which is the right ordering, since a pair meeting high
is both estimated more and further apart.

A second source enters once the collection contains drafts. Where the distance has been corrected for
incompleteness by Equation \eqref{completecorr}, the uncertainty in $c_g$ and $c_h$ propagates into
$\widehat{d}(g,h)$ and belongs in $\sigma^2$ alongside the sketching error: a pair of genomes whose
completeness is known to a per cent carries nearly full weight, and a pair whose completeness is a
guess carries little. That is what makes $W$ the instrument Section \ref{besttree} reaches for
instead of a threshold, since discarding a genome is the same as giving every pair it belongs to a
weight of zero, and the reasons for doing so are rarely that absolute.

\subsection{What cannot be identified, and what the residuals say}

Equation \eqref{lsq} has a unique solution only where $A$ has full column rank, and it does not
always. An internal node of degree two lies on every path through it or on none, so its two edges
enter every equation together and only their sum $b_{e_1} + b_{e_2}$ is determined. The taxonomy is
full of such nodes --- a rank with a single child beneath it is the ordinary case, not the exception
--- so where the topology is taken from the taxonomy the chains must be contracted before the fit and
the fitted length divided among them afterwards by a convention, or else reported as the one length
the data supports. The same applies at a root: no pair-path separates the two edges below it, which
is why the fit is done unrooted and the root placed afterwards.

Nothing in Equation \eqref{lsq} imposes a clock. The fitted lengths are accumulated substitution,
not elapsed time, and two branches of equal length need not represent equal intervals; a tree drawn
from them is additive but not ultrametric, and the horizontal indentations of
Section \ref{embedding} should be read as distance and never as age.

The residual $A\widehat{\mathbf{b}} - \widehat{\mathbf{d}}$ is not waste. Equation \eqref{additive}
asserts that the distances are tree-like, and with tens or hundreds of equations per unknown the fit
has ample opportunity to fail. A residual large in one region of the tree and small elsewhere says
that region is not well described by a tree at all --- recombination and horizontal transfer being
the biological reasons, and a failure of the independence assumption of Section \ref{pairwise} the
methodological one --- and distinguishing the two is what Section \ref{validation} has to do. A third
reading belongs to a single leaf rather than to a region: an incomplete assembly inflates all of one
genome's distances by about the same amount, which the fit absorbs into that genome's own pendant
edge, so an unaccountably long terminal branch is a question about the assembly before it is a
question about the organism. What that absorption does not take up is the part of the inflation that
depends on both genomes rather than on one, and where many assemblies are short that part is spread
over the whole matrix and lands here, in the residuals, as a floor below which no split should be
resolved. Section \ref{incomplete} gives its size.
Reported per node as the root-mean-square residual over the pairs whose path crosses it, it gives
each branch of the drawn tree a measure of how much the data supported it, which the heuristic
portions of Section \ref{embedding} could not offer.

\subsection{The best tree a flawed collection allows}\label{besttree}

Judging the taxonomy is one use of the fit and getting the best tree the data supports is another, and
the second has to be reached through the first. Section \ref{incomplete} leaves a collection in which
some assemblies are known to be short and their distances known to be inflated; the question is what
to do with them before a topology is inferred.

The obvious answer is to drop them, and it works. On twelve-leaf trees at strain-level resolution with
completeness spread over $[0.5, 1]$, discarding every genome below $c = 0.8$ and clustering the rest
returned the correct topology \emph{of the survivors} in $99\,\%$ of trials. The price is in what
\enquote{of the survivors} conceals: on average $5.6$ genomes of twelve remained, and at a threshold
of $c = 0.9$ only $4.5$ did, with four in five collections left too small to resolve at all. A correct
tree of a third of a clade is not obviously better than an uncertain tree of the whole of it.

The price is worse than it looks, because what is discarded is not a random sample. Assemblies are
closed where a clade has been studied and left in fragments where it has not, so completeness tracks
attention, and attention tracks the organisms that were already well described. Dropping drafts
therefore prunes hardest exactly where a phylogeny is most wanted --- the under-studied corners of a
genus --- and returns a confident tree of the part nobody needed a tree of.

Correcting is the better instrument, and Section \ref{incomplete} has already built it in
Equation \eqref{completecorr}: each size is divided by its own completeness and each shared count by
the product of the two. Run on the same trees, with
every genome kept, the corrected matrix returned the full twelve-leaf topology in $100\,\%$ of trials
where $c$ was estimated to within $5\,\%$, against $30\,\%$ for the same matrix uncorrected. So the
information that dropping throws away is enough, when used, to recover the whole tree rather than a
fragment of it.

It has a condition and the condition is sharp. At $10\,\%$ error in the estimate of $c$ the recovery
fell to $87\,\%$, and at $20\,\%$ to $34\,\%$ --- no better than not correcting at all, since a
correction of the wrong size is itself a perturbation. What decides the matter is therefore how well
$c$ can be measured, and Section \ref{incomplete} is exact about the shape of that. Where the assembly
behind a genome is identified, $c$ is read from the release's own record and the condition is met
outright: exactly $1$ for a complete assembly, a recorded ratio for a chromosome-level one. Where it is
not, what the two estimators return is deficiency relative to a genome's neighbours. That is then the
quantity the correction can use, and only while short genomes are the minority --- inside which the pendant estimator is accurate to about a
point in ten, comfortably within what Equation \eqref{completecorr} needs.

The policy follows from where each estimator is blind. A draft short by the typical $1.5\,\%$ is
invisible against relatives that vary among themselves by more, and needs no correcting, since the
distance it distorts is distorted by $0.0005$. A genome a third or a half shorter than its neighbours
stands clear and is worth correcting, because what it distorts is worth repairing. A clade in which
most assemblies are equally short is invisible in a third way --- nothing stands out because nothing
differs --- and is also the case that needs no correction, since a uniform deficit leaves the
topology and every relative statement intact and only draws the tips too long. So: correct what
stands out from its neighbours, leave the rest, and use the weights below rather than a threshold in
the band where the estimators are uncertain enough that a correction might do as much harm as good.

That gives the order of operations, and each step is one the paper already has. Fit the taxonomy as
Section \ref{topologychoice} recommends, and read the pendant edges: their excess over a genome's
neighbours both ranks the genomes by how inflated their distances are and, inverted, estimates the
deficiency. Check it against $\kappa_g$, which measures the same thing from sizes and shares no
inputs with it, and accept the estimate only
where the two signals agree --- a long pendant edge with a normal $\kappa_g$ is a divergent organism
and correcting it would erase the very signal being sought. Correct the matrix. Cluster the corrected
matrix for the topology the sequence supports. Drop, finally, only the genomes for which $c$ could not
be estimated well enough to correct: those with no close relative to be measured against, and those so
fragmentary that a few per cent of their size is no longer a few per cent of the answer.

Dropping and correcting are not in fact two strategies but one, which Section \ref{treefit} already
expresses. A dropped genome is a genome given zero weight in Equation \eqref{lsq}, and the weight
matrix $W$ is continuous: a genome whose $c$ is known to $2\,\%$ belongs in the fit at nearly full
weight, one known to $20\,\%$ at very little, and one that cannot be measured at none. Choosing a
threshold is choosing a step function where a smooth one is available and better motivated, since the
variance that sets $W$ is exactly the quantity the corrected estimate carries.

One further use follows once the collection has been corrected, and it repairs something
Section \ref{topologychoice} would otherwise have to concede. Comparing a clustered tree against the
taxonomy is only informative if the disagreements are real, and on an uncorrected collection some of
them would not be: incompleteness alone moves genomes about, as the figures above show. After the
correction the surviving disagreements are attributable to the sequence rather than to the assemblies,
which is what makes the comparison of Section \ref{validation} worth running.

\subsection{Placing a new organism}\label{placing}

The independence of Section \ref{taxindep} has a use beyond reassurance. If the estimate does not
depend on where a genome sits, then a genome need not be sitting anywhere in particular for the
estimate to be made --- and that turns the method into a way of placing new organisms on measured
data rather than on nomenclature.

The procedure is three steps and the middle one is the whole of it. A newly sequenced genome $g$ is
entered into the database at a best guess: the genus it is presumed to belong to, or whatever the
depositor's own annotation says. The distances $\widehat{d}(g,h)$ to the genomes already there are
then computed by Section \ref{pairwise}. Finally $g$ is placed where those distances put it --- for
which Section \ref{treefit} already has the machinery, since inserting a leaf on a candidate edge and
refitting by Equation \eqref{lsq} gives a residual per candidate, and the edge with the least residual
is the placement the sequence supports. That is one fit per candidate edge, so a search over all of
them costs $\lvert \mathcal{E} \rvert$ fits --- affordable because each is warm-started from the fit
without $g$ and differs from it in one column, and because the nearest neighbour by $\widehat{d}$
narrows the candidates to one clade before any fitting is done. The guess is what gets $g$ into the database; it is not what
decides the answer.

That the guess cannot decide the answer is the point, and it is what distinguishes this from
placing a genome by its annotation and leaving it there. A guess that is wrong by a whole clade
yields the same $\widehat{d}(g,h)$ to every $h$ as a guess that is right, so the data corrects the
guess rather than inheriting it. Nothing in the placement is circular for the same reason nothing in
Section \ref{treefit} is.

\paragraph{One row, not a matrix.} The cost is what makes this practical. Placing $g$ needs
$\widehat{d}(g,h)$ for each $h$ already in the database and nothing else: one row of the matrix,
$\lvert L \rvert$ comparisons, against the $\binom{\lvert L \rvert}{2}$ a matrix costs. The distances
among the existing genomes are unaffected in the quantity they estimate --- they are properties of
those genomes' $k$-mer sets, which $g$'s arrival does not touch --- so the matrix computed before $g$
arrived stays valid and is simply extended by a row and a column. A database of a thousand genomes
costs half a million pairs once and a thousand per newcomer thereafter.

\paragraph{An incomplete newcomer is the easy case.} Section \ref{incomplete} found that
incompleteness spread over a whole collection is only partly absorbable, because the part of the
inflation belonging to a \emph{pair} of short genomes appears in every cell where two of them meet.
Placing a single organism escapes that entirely, and it is worth saying why, since it is the one
setting in which the additive argument holds without qualification.

Only one genome is new. Its distances to the collection are inflated by its own $\delta_g$ and the
collection's distances among themselves are not touched at all, so the perturbation is exactly a
constant added to one row --- which is to say, exactly a lengthening of the one pendant edge the
newcomer is about to be given. Every candidate edge offers that same pendant edge and absorbs the
same constant, so the residual each candidate earns is unchanged and the edge that wins is the edge
that would have won had the assembly been complete. Tried on six-leaf collections with a seventh
genome attached at a known point, inflating every one of its distances by $0.006$ --- more than the
branch lengths in those trees --- left the winning placement unaltered in every trial.

So an incomplete assembly is placed where it belongs and drawn further out along its own branch than
it deserves. The position is right and the length is wrong, which is the harmless half of the trade:
the pendant edge is the one quantity Section \ref{incomplete} is already reading for exactly this, and
$\kappa_g$ says how much of its length to believe. The pairwise term that spoils the collection-wide
case needs two short genomes to appear, and one newcomer against a collection already corrected by
Section \ref{besttree} never supplies the second.

\paragraph{Why to make a real guess anyway.} One thing the guess does affect is the split between
counted and estimated terms, and it affects it in two directions at once. A badly placed $g$ meets its
true relatives high, which as Section \ref{taxindep} sets out gathers the $k$-mers it shares with
them into the counted term: its own distances come out \emph{more} exact, not less. But those same
$k$-mers are thereby held higher in the tree than they would otherwise be, and pairs among the
existing genomes that also carry them lose them from their own counted terms. Adding a seventh
genome to the six of Section \ref{taxindep}, as a close relative of two of them, makes the size of it
plain: entered in the wrong clade its own counted share went from $0.667$ to $1.000$ while a
pre-existing pair's fell from $0.778$ to $0.333$, and every distance involved stayed the same to five
decimals. So a sensible guess is worth making --- not for the
newcomer's sake, but for the precision of the rest of the matrix.

This also makes the procedure worth iterating where precision matters, and the two costs should be
kept apart: one row places $g$, and a rebuild improves the split for everything. Place $g$ at a guess,
read the row, move it to where the row points, and rebuild if the matrix is to be reported rather than
only searched: the second round is measured against a database in which $g$'s $k$-mers sit where they
belong, so the split is better for every pair, and the placement it returns either confirms the first
or says the first was not yet settled. Since the
distances themselves do not move between rounds, the iteration converges on the split rather than on
the answer, which is why one round is usually enough and two are decisive.

\section{Evolutionary distance distribution}

\subsection{Posterior distribution for the evolutionary distance}

We derive the probability distribution of $d$ given the observed count $E$ of differing unique $k$-mers, using Bayes' theorem. Let $N := |s|-k+1$ be the number of $k$-mers in $s$. We adopt a uniform prior $\pi(d) = 1$ on $d\in[0,1]$, reflecting no preference for any particular mutation rate. The binomial model from above gives the likelihood
\[
  P(E \mid d) = \binom{N}{E}\,p(d)^E\,[1-p(d)]^{N-E}, \qquad p(d) = 1-(1-d)^k.
\]
By Bayes' theorem the posterior satisfies
\[
  P(d \mid E) \;\propto\; [1-(1-d)^k]^E\,(1-d)^{k(N-E)}.
\]
To identify the normalising constant we substitute $v := (1-d)^k$, so $d = 1-v^{1/k}$ and $\mathrm{d}d = \frac{1}{k}\,v^{1/k-1}\,\mathrm{d}v$. The posterior density in $v$ becomes
\[
  P(v \mid E) \;\propto\; v^{\,N-E+\frac{1}{k}-1}(1-v)^{E},
\]
which is recognised as a $\mathrm{Beta}\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\;E+1\right)$ density. Hence the normalised posterior for $d$ is
\begin{equation}
  P(d \mid E) \;=\;
  \frac{k\,(1-d)^{k(N-E)}\bigl[1-(1-d)^k\bigr]^E}
       {B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\;E+1\right)},
  \qquad d\in[0,1].
  \label{eq:dposterior}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Posterior mean and variance}

Since $d = 1-v^{1/k}$ with $v\sim\mathrm{Beta}\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\,E+1\right)$, and the $t$-th moment of a $\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$ variable equals $B(\alpha+t,\beta)/B(\alpha,\beta)$, the posterior mean is
\begin{equation}
  E[d\mid E] \;=\; 1 \;-\;
  \frac{B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{2}{k},\;E+1\right)}
       {B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\;E+1\right)}.
  \label{eq:dpostmean}
\end{equation}
For the variance we use $\mathrm{Var}(d\mid E) = \mathrm{Var}(v^{1/k}\mid E) = E[v^{2/k}\mid E]-(E[v^{1/k}\mid E])^2$, giving
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Var}(d\mid E) \;=\;
  \frac{B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{3}{k},\;E+1\right)}
       {B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\;E+1\right)}
  \;-\;
  \left(\frac{B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{2}{k},\;E+1\right)}
             {B\!\left(N-E+\tfrac{1}{k},\;E+1\right)}\right)^{\!2}.
  \label{eq:dpostvar}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Delta method approximation for large $|s|$}

For genomic sequences $|s|$ is in the millions, so the posterior concentrates tightly around the point estimate $\hat{d} = d(E/|s|) = 1-(1-E/|s|)^{1/k}$ from Eq.~\eqref{distanceapprox}. Setting $\hat{v} = (1-\hat{d})^k$, the posterior variance of $v$ is approximately $\mathrm{Var}(v\mid E)\approx \hat{v}(1-\hat{v})/|s|$. Applying the delta method to $d = g(v) = 1-v^{1/k}$ with $g'(v) = -\tfrac{1}{k}\,v^{1/k-1}$ yields
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Var}(d\mid E) \;\approx\;
  \frac{(1-\hat{d})^{2-k}\bigl[1-(1-\hat{d})^k\bigr]}{k^2\,|s|}.
  \label{eq:ddeltavar}
\end{equation}
An approximate $\pm 1\sigma$ credible interval for $d$ is therefore $\hat{d}\,\pm\,\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(d\mid E)}$. The bands are tight for moderate $E/|s|$ but widen rapidly as $E$ approaches $|s|$, confirming that the estimate becomes unreliable in that regime.

    \subsection{Are the distances right?}

Equation \eqref{pairdist} is checked against an alignment-based standard, for which
FastANI \cite{jain2018fastani} is the natural choice: accurate where it applies, and applicable at
the scale of one clade even though it does not scale to a whole collection. The comparison is a
regression of $\widehat{d}$ on $1 - \mathrm{ANI}$ over all pairs of a clade, and what is read from
it is not only the correlation but the residual structure --- whether the departures fall where
Section \ref{pairwise} predicts, at pairs meeting high in the tree and at pairs whose shared $k$-mers
are mostly estimated rather than counted.

    \subsection{What does whole-genome counting buy over sketching?}

This is the comparison that decides whether the method earns its place, and it is a falsifiable
prediction rather than an expectation. Mash \cite{Ondov:2016aa} and sourmash
\cite{pierce2019sourmash} estimate the same Jaccard index from a sketch of a few thousand hashes
where Section \ref{pairwise} counts over millions of $k$-mers, so their sampling error should set a
floor that ours does not have. Section \ref{pairwise}'s error analysis says where to look: the
advantage should be at small $d$, where a sketch runs out of resolution, and should vanish at large
$d$, where our own estimated terms dominate. A plot of the disagreement between the two against
$\widehat{d}$ should therefore fall, and if it does not the whole argument for counting rather than
sketching is wrong.

    \subsection{Do drafts behave as Section \ref{incomplete} says?}

The claims of Section \ref{incomplete} are testable on the same clades and without any outside
standard, by deletion. The claim to test hardest is the one about many short assemblies at once,
since that is the case a real collection presents and since the first-order argument does not cover
it: deleting sequence from a growing fraction of a clade's genomes and watching which splits of the
recovered tree survive gives the resolution limit directly, in the units of Section \ref{incomplete}'s
table, on real branch lengths rather than simulated ones. The single-genome case is the controlled
version of the same experiment. Take the closed genomes of a clade, compute the matrix, then delete
sequence from one of them and recompute: the distance from the truncated genome to every other should rise by
about the predicted amount, $\kappa_g$ should fall by the deleted fraction, and the fit should put the
rise into that genome's pendant edge and leave the rest of the tree where it was. Deleting at random
and deleting whole repetitive regions gives the two loss models, and the gap between them is what
says whether the $1/c$ correction is worth applying or whether incompleteness should simply be
reported beside the distance. The experiment also calibrates the detection: what deficit is visible
above within-species size variation, on real clades rather than in principle.

    \subsection{Is $c$ estimated well enough?}\label{cvalid}

Section \ref{incomplete} claims two things a measurement can contradict: that the relative estimators
recover a short genome's deficiency to within about a point in ten while short genomes are a
minority, and that they stop seeing anything as that minority becomes a majority. Both are testable
without an outside standard, by deletion --- take a clade of assemblies, delete sequence from a
growing fraction of them, and watch what each estimator returns against what was removed. That
experiment is the one to run first, because it needs nothing but the clade.

An outside standard is worth having for the part deletion cannot reach: whether the assemblies that
arrive from RefSeq are short in the way deletion simulates. \texttt{assembly\_summary\_refseq.txt}
supplies the assembly level and CheckM or BUSCO the completeness, both for the evaluation clades only
and neither inside the tool, for the reasons Section \ref{incomplete} gives. Regressing the estimators
on marker-based completeness over a few hundred assemblies says how far the relative measurements
track the absolute one, which is the question the method's own data cannot ask of itself.

Two things should be reported that an accuracy figure alone would hide. The first is how often the
estimators agree with each other, since they share no inputs --- one reads sizes, the other reads the
fitted tree --- so their agreement is evidence where an external standard is unavailable, and their
disagreement localises a genome that is divergent rather than deficient. The second is the fraction of
a real clade in which short assemblies are the majority, because that is where both estimators go
quiet: the evaluation should say how often a clade of interest is in that regime rather than only how
accurate the estimators are when it is not.

One failure mode deserves separating from the rest. A complete but contaminated assembly carries
\emph{extra} sequence and so reads as $c > 1$, which the correction of
Equation \eqref{completecorr} would take for a genome larger than it is. Whether to clamp at one or to
report it is a decision the measurements should inform rather than the text assert.

    \subsection{Does the taxonomy fit?}

The topology question is asked of the fit of Section \ref{treefit} rather than of the distances.
Trees clustered from $\widehat{d}$ are compared to published phylogenies for the same clades and to
GTDB \cite{parks2022gtdb} by the Robinson--Foulds metric \cite{robinson1981comparison}, which counts
